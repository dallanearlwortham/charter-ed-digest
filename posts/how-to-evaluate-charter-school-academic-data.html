How to Evaluate a Charter School's Academic Data

# How to Evaluate a Charter School's Academic Performance Data in 2026

Choosing a charter school is one of the most consequential decisions a family makes. Yet most of the information families encounter—glossy brochures, enrollment websites, open house nights—is built to impress, not to inform. If you want to genuinely understand whether a charter school is delivering for its students, you need to go a layer deeper: into the actual performance data.

Here's what to look for, what to skip, and how to ask the right questions before you sign an enrollment form.

---

Why Data-Driven School Choice Actually Matters

According to EdChoice's 2026 Schooling in America survey—which polled a nationally representative sample of more than 2,400 current school parents—both charter school and private school parents now rank a safe environment as their number one reason for choosing a school, followed closely by academic quality. Academic quality was the top priority for both groups until very recently; safety has gradually climbed the ranks and finally took the top spot in 2026. For charter school parents specifically, location rounds out the top three.

That framing matters. Most charter school families aren't choosing on a whim—they're responding to something that wasn't working. Among parents who had switched their child's school type, unmet academic needs was a close second reason behind excessive stress or anxiety at the former school. In other words, the bar families are holding new schools to is real and specific. The data should be, too.

---

Start With Your State's School Report Card

Every public charter school in the United States participates in state assessments and is required to report results publicly. Your state's Department of Education website publishes a school report card for every public school, including charters. This is your first stop—and it's free.

What to look for:

  • Proficiency rates in math and English/reading — what percentage of students scored at or above grade level on state tests
  • Year-over-year trends — are scores improving, holding steady, or declining over three or more years?
  • Subgroup performance — how are English learners, students with disabilities, and economically disadvantaged students performing compared to peers statewide?
  • Graduation rate — for high schools, this single number is one of the most reliable indicators of whether students are completing their education

Don't evaluate a school on a single year's snapshot. A school that scored well in 2023 but has trended downward since tells a very different story than one that has steadily improved. Pull at least three years of data wherever you can.

---

Growth vs. Proficiency: The Most Important Distinction You'll Make

What does "academic growth" actually mean, and why does it matter more than proficiency alone?

Proficiency scores tell you what percentage of students met a grade-level standard on a single test. Growth metrics—sometimes called value-added measures—track how much individual students improved over time, regardless of where they started. A school serving a high-poverty population may show lower proficiency rates than a school that enrolls higher-income students, but it may be doing more for those students academically.

This is the most important data concept for families to understand. A school with strong growth scores is adding real value regardless of its starting point. When comparing schools, look at both metrics together—neither one alone tells the full story.

Research backs this up. Stanford University's CREDO National Charter School Study III, published in 2023, found that charter school students gained an average of 16 additional days of learning in reading and 6 days in math per year compared to peers in traditional public schools—though researchers note results vary significantly by individual school. That variance is exactly the point: the category tells you less than the specific campus data does.

---

Look Beyond the School's Own Website: Authorizer Reports

Charter schools answer to authorizers—state boards, university sponsors, or independent entities—who conduct their own reviews and publish accountability reports. These are often more candid than what a school puts on its own website, and they're a resource most families never think to check.

Transparency is a cornerstone of authorizer work. The most effective authorizers share performance information across schools to encourage improvement and make that data accessible to parents and the public. If a charter school's authorizer doesn't publish performance data, that itself is worth asking about.

This is becoming a broader policy priority. South Carolina's Act 123 of 2026 was specifically enacted to clarify expectations for schools and authorizers, strengthen oversight and transparency, and create a firmer foundation for responsible growth in the state's charter sector—a signal of where accountability policy is heading nationally.

---

For High Schools: College and Career Readiness Metrics

For high school families, the question isn't just whether students graduate—it's whether they graduate ready. Look for schools that publish:

  • ACT/SAT score averages or participation rates
  • AP or dual enrollment completion rates
  • College acceptance and first-year enrollment rates
  • FAFSA completion rates (a surprisingly reliable predictor of whether seniors actually enroll in postsecondary education)
  • Industry certification or credential attainment rates for schools with a career and technical focus

Career Path High, a tuition-free charter school serving grades 9–12 in Kaysville, Utah, is an example of a high school model built around career pathways—precisely the kind of school where families should look beyond graduation rates to ask what credentials and opportunities students hold when they walk across the stage.

---

Red Flags Families Shouldn't Ignore

Not all schools are equally forthcoming. These are signs worth investigating further:

  • High student attrition between 9th and 12th grade — if a school loses a large share of its students before graduation, that affects published graduation rates and may signal enrollment management practices worth questioning
  • Proficiency gaps that have persisted for three or more years — chronic underperformance in math or reading is not easily explained away
  • Missing subgroup data — small-sample exemptions can obscure real gaps in outcomes for English learners or students with disabilities
  • An authorizer on probation or in a renewal dispute — a school facing authorizer scrutiny should be able to explain the situation clearly and describe what has changed

---

Questions to Ask at Every Open House

Bring these questions to any school visit. How a school answers them—with specific numbers or with talking points—tells you a great deal:

  1. What was your school's academic growth score last year, and how does it compare to the district and state average?
  2. What percentage of your graduates enrolled in college or earned an industry credential within one year of graduation?
  3. How do students with IEPs or English learners perform on state assessments compared to statewide averages?
  4. Has your school faced any authorizer performance concerns in the last three years? If so, what changed?
  5. What is your student retention rate from 9th to 12th grade?

A school that can answer these questions with numbers—not just stories—is a school that takes accountability seriously.

---

The Bottom Line on Charter School Data

The best charter schools welcome scrutiny. They publish data proactively, explain it in plain language, and use it to improve. Families who know how to read that data are better equipped to make a real choice—not just a hopeful one.

School choice works when it's informed. The data is public, it's free, and it's worth an hour of your time before you commit to an enrollment decision that will shape the next four years of your child's education.
