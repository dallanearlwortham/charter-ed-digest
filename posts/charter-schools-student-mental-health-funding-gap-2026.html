Charter Schools & the Mental Health Funding Gap

# When the Federal Safety Net Shrinks, Charter Schools Can Build Their Own

The youth mental health crisis in American schools hasn't peaked—it's settled in. More than half of mental health providers (57%) reported a decline in student mental health over the past year, up from 46% the year before, according to eLuma's State of Student Mental Health Report released in January 2026. At the same time, the federal funding that schools have relied on to address that crisis is suddenly unstable in ways that should alarm every school leader heading into the 2026-27 school year.

For charter schools especially—which often serve higher proportions of students in poverty, students of color, and students who lack access to private mental health care—understanding this moment clearly, and responding strategically, is not optional.

What Happened to the Federal Mental Health Money

The short version: a billion dollars in school mental health grants was here, then it wasn't, then some of it came back—but less of it.

The Department of Education halted nearly $1 billion in school mental health grants in April 2025, then awarded $208 million in new grants in December 2025, leaving net federal investment well below Bipartisan Safer Communities Act levels. Those grants had been the funding backbone for an effort to train and place 14,000 new mental health professionals in K-12 schools. Medicaid—the largest single federal payer for school-based clinical behavioral health services—is also under pressure, with the 2025 reconciliation bill cutting roughly $1 trillion from Medicaid spending over a decade.

The volatility is hitting hard. A Healthy Schools Campaign survey of school district leaders found that 80% expect reductions and layoffs of school health staff, and 70% anticipate cuts to mental and behavioral health services.

Among the charter schools that felt the loss was the Multicultural Learning Center, a public charter school on the outskirts of Los Angeles County, which had used its federal grant to support student well-being in the years after the pandemic—until the second Trump administration axed the grants in April 2025.

Why Charter Schools Feel This Differently

The staffing picture at most schools was already precarious before any grants were cut. The National Association of School Psychologists recommends a ratio of 1 school psychologist per 500 students, but the national ratio for the 2024-2025 school year is 1 school psychologist for every 1,071 students—more than double the recommended level.

Public charter schools across the nation have been leveraging unique partnerships and innovative programs to connect families and staff with highly effective and culturally competent mental health services—but they continue to face significant challenges, in part because they typically cannot rely on the district-level administrative infrastructure that traditional public schools use to navigate grant administration, Medicaid billing, and crisis staffing.

So what does a resilient charter school mental health model actually look like in 2026? It doesn't depend primarily on federal grants. It is built from the school outward, into the community.

The Community-Partnership Model: A More Durable Architecture

The most durable student mental health infrastructure for charter schools isn't a grant-funded counselor position—it's a web of community relationships that existed before the grants arrived and will still be there after they've been cut.

Research and guidance from the National Charter School Resource Center identifies several strategies that work:

  • Bringing community health partners onto campus. Charter schools can bring in services from community health and mental health organizations to offer a continuum of care that includes wraparound supports, rather than trying to staff every clinical role in-house.
  • Training existing staff in trauma-informed practice. Professional development in social-emotional learning and trauma-informed approaches means that every teacher becomes a first-line support, reducing the load on dedicated mental health staff.
  • Establishing school-based crisis teams with community members. Including caregivers and community stakeholders in crisis planning structures both the response and the ongoing relationship between school and neighborhood.
  • Making caregiver engagement a priority, not an afterthought. School administrators continue to prioritize caregiver engagement, professional development for staff, and universal screening systems as their top needs year after year. Caregiver engagement is free infrastructure—it requires time and intentionality, not a grant award.

State funding streams are also worth pursuing aggressively. Wisconsin, for instance, shifted its school mental health allocation from a competitive grant of $25 million in prior fiscal years to a per-pupil allocation of $40 million for fiscal year 2026, distributed to every district and independent charter school in the state. Charter leaders should audit what their own state offers before chasing federal opportunities that may evaporate mid-grant.

What This Looks Like in Practice

The community-partnership framework isn't theoretical. BRICK Buffalo, a charter school launched in Buffalo, NY in 2023 to serve students from East Buffalo neighborhoods, explicitly builds this logic into its model: the school immerses families in what it describes as "an ecosystem of rich community partnerships" designed to support the stable and healthy homes that children need in order to thrive academically. Rather than treating student wellness as a clinical add-on funded by a single grant, the model treats community connection itself as core infrastructure—backed by concrete supports like free aftercare and free transportation that reduce the practical stressors on families before a crisis ever develops. That kind of pre-cradle-to-career community integration is structurally harder to defund than a line item in a federal grant.

Three Questions Charter Leaders Should Ask Right Now

As the 2026-27 school year gets underway, every charter school leader should be working through these:

  1. Which mental health services at our school currently depend on federal grants—and what is our plan if those grants don't renew?
  2. What community-based mental health organizations are already operating in our students' neighborhoods, and do we have formal agreements to bring their services onto campus?
  3. Are we billing Medicaid for every service we're eligible to bill for? As of early 2024, 25 states had expanded their school Medicaid programs beyond IEP-linked services, meaning schools can bill for medically necessary services delivered to any Medicaid-enrolled student. Many charter schools leave significant Medicaid revenue on the table.

The Bigger Picture

Students are roughly six times more likely to access mental health care at school than anywhere else, and 18% of public school students used school-based mental health services in 2024-25. That means schools—including charters—have become the primary mental health delivery system for American children, whether or not they planned for that role.

The federal government's unreliable posture on school mental health funding in 2026 isn't an argument for abandoning advocacy—charter leaders should absolutely keep pushing for stable, adequate federal investment. But it is a clear argument for building mental health infrastructure that can stand on its own when Washington whipsaws. Community partnerships, caregiver engagement, trauma-informed staff development, and strategic Medicaid billing aren't glamorous. They're resilient.

The schools that serve children best over the next decade will be the ones that treated mental health as an architectural feature of the school—not a program waiting on a check.
