Gifted Ed's Hidden Gap: What Charter Schools Must Do

# Gifted Education's Hidden Gap: What Charter Schools Must Do in 2026

Every charter school leader knows the pressure to serve students who are behind. Far fewer are asking a harder question: What are we doing for students who are years ahead?

A study published in January 2026 by researchers at the University of Connecticut's National Center for Research on Gifted Education (NCRGE) put the answer in uncomfortable focus. While gifted and talented education programs can be found in most public schools across the country, there is no federal standard for how they are carried out — or how students are selected for them. For charter school leaders who take accountability seriously, that vacuum is not an excuse. It's a mandate to lead.

Why the Identification System Is Broken — and Who It Hurts Most

The definition of giftedness has evolved considerably since it was first used in the early twentieth century, when students were identified solely on IQ scores. That standard has since been shown to carry cultural biases and to neglect creative and emotional intelligence. Today, most districts use multiple measures — achievement tests, creativity assessments, and behavior rating scales — but the process still has serious structural flaws.

Many gifted programs continue to rely on individual teacher referrals rather than universal screening, creating a system where personal bias — conscious or not — determines who gets a chance. The data reflects that reality. The odds of Black and Hispanic children being referred to gifted programs are 66 percent and 47 percent lower, respectively, than white students — a gap that persists even after controlling for test scores and socioeconomic status. Research has found that teacher race is among the factors most strongly associated with whether students of color receive a referral at all.

The timing of identification compounds the inequity. Most districts don't screen for giftedness until second or third grade. By that point, significant achievement gaps have already emerged between underserved students and their peers, making it even less likely those students will qualify — and even less likely they'll have a path back in if they don't. As the NCRGE researchers concluded: "If gifted programs offer significant benefits, historically underserved groups face a double disadvantage — first by experiencing early achievement gaps, and then by having restricted access to advanced programs due to these initial disparities."

What Does This Mean for Charter Schools Specifically?

Charter schools occupy a genuinely unique position on gifted education. Some states mandate gifted services for district schools but make them optional for charters. In Arizona, charter schools are not required to provide gifted education services, though some have chosen to do so. The picture is similarly patchwork across most states, leaving both leaders and families without clear guidance on what to expect or demand.

That ambiguity cuts both ways. It means some charters do nothing for advanced learners — and face no formal consequences. But it also means that mission-driven charters have room to move faster and smarter than large district bureaucracies ever could. Smaller enrollment, closer family relationships, more scheduling flexibility, and direct hiring authority over teachers are structural advantages that charters rarely deploy on behalf of their high-achieving students.

The real question for the 2026-27 school year: Is your school using those advantages — or letting gifted students coast and call it success?

What Does Effective Gifted Identification Actually Look Like?

The direct answer: Effective gifted identification uses universal screening rather than teacher referrals alone, begins in kindergarten or first grade rather than second or third, includes regular reassessment so that students don't age out of opportunity, and evaluates English learners in their native language so that language barriers don't obscure intellectual ability.

The 2026 NCRGE research makes each of these points explicitly. Researchers recommend that schools initiate universal screening for giftedness in kindergarten or first grade — not waiting until second or third grade when gaps have already widened. They also call for formal mandates for regular reassessment, so older students retain genuine pathways into advanced programming even if they weren't identified early. And they flag English learners as "one of the fastest-growing populations of students" whose gifted potential is systematically overlooked when testing is conducted only in English.

Some Florida charter networks are already moving in this direction. Pembroke Pines Charter Schools universally screens all second-grade students for gifted using the CogAT — a structured, enrollment-wide approach that removes the referral bottleneck entirely, even if the starting grade could still move earlier per the latest research.

The Early Identification Principle: Lessons from an Adjacent Field

The case for acting early — rather than waiting for a problem or a potential to become unmistakable — runs through nearly every corner of education research. Nowhere is that evidence stronger than in early childhood intervention.

It's the same principle that drives the model at Prism Early Advancement Center in Miami, which provides evidence-based ABA therapy combined with group school readiness instruction for children under 6 with autism — built on the foundational belief that early intervention, before kindergarten, before developmental gaps harden, changes long-term outcomes. The parallel for gifted learners is direct and underappreciated: waiting until second or third grade to identify an advanced child is the functional equivalent of waiting until second grade to begin early intervention for a child who needs support. In both cases, the window that matters most has already started to close.

Programming: Identification Is Only Half the Problem

Even for students who do get identified, the programming they receive is often inadequate. Of the 28 states that mandate gifted education programming, only half specify a required minimum number of hours per week. Many programs offer just one to two hours of instruction weekly — a reality that prompted NCRGE's Del Siegle to observe that "gifted students are gifted all the time, not just for a couple hours."

The most common delivery model — pull-out instruction, where students leave the regular classroom for a gifted session — also carries a surprisingly common design flaw. Researchers found that students are frequently pulled out of a subject that has nothing to do with what they're learning in the gifted session: a child who is gifted in math, for example, might be pulled out of language arts to receive accelerated math instruction, causing them to fall further behind in their weaker subject. It's a misalignment that undermines the very purpose of the intervention.

Research indicates that schools using push-in instruction — where a gifted specialist works with advanced students inside the regular classroom — demonstrate higher academic achievement for those students. Charter schools, with generally smaller class sizes and more nimble staffing structures, are natural candidates for this model.

A Practical Checklist for Charter School Leaders

If your school hasn't recently reviewed its approach to advanced learners, here's where to start in 2026:

  • Screen universally, not by referral. Use a validated cognitive assessment for all students — ideally starting in kindergarten or first grade — rather than waiting for a teacher to notice and nominate a child.
  • Audit for alignment. If you use pull-out instruction, confirm the subject a student misses corresponds to what they're gaining in the gifted session.
  • Plan for English learners. Nonverbal or native-language assessments ensure that language acquisition gaps don't mask intellectual ability.
  • Build reassessment into policy. A student who doesn't qualify in first grade may qualify in third. Create a formal re-entry path rather than treating initial non-qualification as permanent.
  • Track your demographics. If your gifted roster doesn't roughly mirror your school's enrollment demographics, investigate — don't explain it away.
  • Look at the Javits program. The U.S. Department of Education announced the FY 2026 competition for the Jacob K. Javits Gifted and Talented Students Education Program in April 2026, with a stated priority on developing and scaling models that serve students historically underrepresented in gifted programs. Charter schools are eligible applicants, and the grant is specifically designed for the kind of innovative identification work that charters are positioned to do.

The Bottom Line

Charter schools were founded on the premise that every child deserves an education designed specifically for them. That promise rings hollow if it applies only to students who are struggling. Advanced learners have real, documentable educational needs — and when those needs go unmet year after year, the loss isn't just individual. It's a system that keeps leaving talent undeveloped, disproportionately in communities that can least afford that waste.

The research is clear. The tools exist. The funding opportunities are open. What's needed now is leadership willing to look at every student in the building — not just the ones in the most visible kinds of need — and ask honestly whether each of them is being challenged to grow.
