Growth vs. Proficiency: What School Data Really Tells You

# Why the Test Score on a School's Website Might Be Misleading You

When families compare schools during open enrollment season, they almost always reach for the same number first: the proficiency rate. What percentage of students are on grade level? It's intuitive, it's easy to find, and it feels definitive. The problem is that it tells you far less about a school's actual teaching quality than most people assume—and relying on it alone can lead families to overlook genuinely excellent schools while gravitating toward ones that are simply coasting on advantaged student demographics.

There's a better metric hiding in plain sight, and charter school families in particular should know how to use it.

What Proficiency Rates Actually Measure (And What They Don't)

A proficiency rate answers one question: Right now, what share of this school's students score above the grade-level threshold on a standardized test? That sounds useful, but the number is heavily shaped by who walked in the door on day one. Measuring average student performance reflects not only how much students are learning at school but also the knowledge they brought when they enrolled.

In practice, this means a school in an affluent zip code—where students arrive already reading above grade level—will post a dazzling proficiency score even if its teachers are mediocre. Meanwhile, a school serving students who started two or three years behind grade level may show a modest proficiency rate even while delivering extraordinary instruction. Schools serving larger proportions of students from low-income families will tend to exhibit lower scores and proficiency rates, even if they are very effective at compelling progress from those students.

The downstream consequence is real: leaning too heavily on proficiency rates or average test scores can unfairly penalize schools that serve the most disadvantaged students, while leaving unchecked schools where students are actually learning the least.

What a Student Growth Percentile (SGP) Actually Measures

A Student Growth Percentile, or SGP, answers a fundamentally different question: Compared to students across the state who started at the same academic level, how much did my child's school help them grow?

Technically, a student growth percentile describes a student's growth compared to other students with similar prior test scores—their "academic peers." A student growth percentile is a number between 1 and 99. If a student has an SGP of 85, that means she showed more growth than 85 percent of her academic peers statewide. When these individual scores are aggregated across a school, the resulting median SGP tells you something the proficiency rate cannot: how much value the school's instruction is actually adding, independent of who showed up on the first day.

This makes SGP a direct measure of instructional impact—untangled, as much as possible, from the starting advantage or disadvantage a student brings through the door. Growth measures like student growth percentiles are a much fairer way to evaluate schools, since they control for prior achievement and reveal progress over the course of the school year. They can distinguish between a high-poverty school where kids are making genuine strides and one where they are treading water.

The Two Metrics Answer Two Different Questions—And You Need Both

This is not an argument that proficiency is meaningless. Both measures matter; they just answer different questions. Growth captures whether students are learning more than they used to. Proficiency captures whether they have learned enough. The ideal school delivers high marks on both—but when the two tell different stories, savvy families need to know which one to trust first.

Think of it this way: a student who arrives in third grade reading at a kindergarten level and finishes fifth grade at a third-grade level has made three years of progress in two years. That child won't score proficient on a grade-level test, but her school has done something remarkable. A school full of students like that should be celebrated, not dismissed. Conversely, a school where high-scoring students show minimal year-over-year growth is quietly underserving the very kids whose families are most satisfied with their test scores.

Research backs this up from the family-choice angle, too: when parents are given information about achievement growth rather than just proficiency, they tend to choose schools that are not only more effective at teaching their students but also more demographically diverse. Growth data is not just an accountability wonk's tool—it's a practical aid for any family navigating school choice.

What a Top SGP Ranking Looks Like in the Real World

Consider what it actually signals when a K-8 charter school earns the #1 ranking in student growth percentile in both ELA and Math. It means students there—whatever academic level they started at—are outpacing comparable peers at the highest rate among measured schools in the state. That's the clearest data-driven signal available that a school's instruction, structure, and culture are genuinely accelerating learning rather than simply reflecting the advantages students brought with them.

Solare Collegiate Charter School, a tuition-free K-8 public charter school in Southwest Albuquerque, NM, is one such example: the school publicly reports a #1 ranking in student growth percentile in both ELA and Math, with a math student growth percentile of 65.97%—a concrete illustration of how growth data can tell a very different, and often more compelling, story than a raw proficiency snapshot ever could.

How to Find and Read Growth Data When Comparing Schools

Most families don't know where to look for SGP data, which is a big reason proficiency rates continue to dominate school-choice conversations. Here's a practical starting checklist:

  • Check your state's public school report card. Most states publish SGP or a similar growth measure—sometimes called "value-added" or "median student growth percentile"—for every public school, including charters. Search "[your state] school report card student growth percentile."
  • Look at GreatSchools' Student Progress rating. GreatSchools now weights academic growth heavily in its school ratings, separating it from raw proficiency scores. Growth models measure how much students improved compared to similar peers, so there's no ceiling on how well any school can score regardless of its neighborhood demographics.
  • Ask the school directly. Any school confident in its instructional model should be able to hand you multi-year growth data across grade levels and subjects—not just a single number from last spring's test administration.
  • Compare growth and proficiency together. High growth paired with rising proficiency is the ideal. High proficiency with flat or low growth deserves scrutiny. Low proficiency with high growth signals a school doing hard, important work that raw rankings tend to undervalue.
  • Ask about subgroup growth. A school may show strong average growth while a specific population—English language learners, students with IEPs, students who enrolled below grade level—is being left behind. Strong schools can show you growth data broken out by student group.

Why This Matters Especially for Charter School Families

Charter schools are particularly well-positioned to benefit from growth-based evaluation—and particularly at risk of being misread by proficiency-only comparisons. Many high-performing charters deliberately serve underrepresented communities and students who enter behind grade level. Judging those schools on raw proficiency scores alone systematically undersells their impact and distorts family decision-making.

National data reinforces the point. Between 2013 and 2024, the lowest-performing public school students—those at the 25th percentile—lost an average of 12 points on national assessments, compared with an 8-point decline among charter school students at the same performance level. Charters, on balance, have shown more resilience in maintaining and accelerating growth for the students who need it most. But you'll only see that clearly if you're looking at growth data instead of—or alongside—headline proficiency figures.

At the same time, charter accountability depends on families asking sharper questions. A school that can only point to proficiency rates, or that deflects when asked about year-over-year growth, is giving you an incomplete picture of its performance. The best charter schools welcome the harder conversation precisely because they know growth data tells their real story better than any single test-score snapshot.

---

The next time you sit down at a school information night and someone slides a test score sheet across the table, flip the question: This tells me where your students are. Can you show me how fast they're getting there? That's the number that will tell you the most about whether a school is truly earning the trust families place in it.
