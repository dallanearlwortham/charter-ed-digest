Dual Enrollment in Charter Schools: The Equity Case

# Dual Enrollment in Charter Schools: The Equity Case for Earning College Credit in High School

For a first-generation student in an underserved community, arriving at a college campus for the first time can feel like landing in a foreign country — unfamiliar culture, unfamiliar expectations, and a four-year price tag that can make the whole venture feel precarious. Dual enrollment — taking real, credit-bearing college courses while still in high school — has emerged as one of the most evidence-backed tools for closing that gap. And charter schools, with their curricular flexibility and college-going culture, are increasingly positioned to make it count.

What Is Dual Enrollment (and Why Does It Matter in 2026)?

Dual enrollment — sometimes called concurrent enrollment — allows high school students to take college-level courses that simultaneously earn both high school and college credit. The model has grown dramatically: the Community College Research Center at Columbia University reports that 2.8 million high school students participated in some form of dual enrollment in the 2023–24 academic year. That growth is not incidental. Students who participate in dual enrollment are 2.5 times more likely to enroll in college immediately after high school, and 12 percent more likely to complete a degree — with first-generation students showing among the strongest effects.

The college-cost angle is just as compelling. Every credit hour earned in high school is a credit hour that does not need to be paid for later. In Chicago Public Schools alone, more than 1,000 seniors in a recent cohort completed 15 or more college credits, saving their families an estimated $10 million. For families without college savings, that kind of head start is transformational.

The Persistent Equity Gap

Despite the boom in participation, dual enrollment has a well-documented equity problem. Researchers have consistently found that students who are white, come from affluent households, or have a multigenerational history of college attendance are overrepresented among program participants — while students who are racially minoritized or come from low-income households remain persistently underrepresented, despite standing to benefit the most.

Several factors drive this gap. Access to high-quality opportunities remains uneven across schools and districts. Too many credits lead nowhere — a pattern researchers describe as "random acts of dual enrollment" — when courses aren't connected to a credential, degree pathway, or career field. And in many states, students and families are still paying to participate, which creates a significant financial barrier before a single credit is earned.

This is precisely where charter schools serving low-income and minority communities have both an opportunity and a responsibility to intervene.

What Effective Charter School Dual Enrollment Looks Like

The research points to a handful of design principles that separate high-impact programs from ineffective ones:

1. Make it free and proactively offered. States like Tennessee have created strong incentives by making dual credit free for low-income high school students across the board, driving meaningful increases in participation among underrepresented groups. Charter schools can mirror this intent by covering any remaining fees from school budgets and actively recruiting students into courses rather than waiting for students to self-select.

2. Connect credits to a real credential pathway. Researchers at the Community College Research Center emphasize that dual enrollment should function as a purposeful on-ramp to college degree programs — not a collection of disconnected electives. Programs that align coursework to transfer pathways or career-track credentials produce meaningfully better outcomes for underserved students.

3. Wrap it in advising. When programs are underfunded, colleges can offer classes but not much else. Without structured advising and academic support, students often take courses randomly rather than building toward a credential. The most effective charter school programs treat dual enrollment as part of a counseling-driven four-year college plan, not an add-on.

4. Don't gatekeep participation. Research has shown that even students who fail a dual enrollment course still demonstrate higher long-term college enrollment and persistence rates than peers who never attempted college-level work at all. The exposure itself is preparation. High-performing charter schools increasingly understand that dual enrollment is not a reward for already-college-ready students — it is a tool for making students college-ready.

Colorado's Concurrent Enrollment Context

Colorado is a particularly instructive case. Since the Concurrent Enrollment Programs Act passed in 2009, the program has expanded steadily, and state data show that concurrent enrollment students successfully completed nearly 93 percent of their credit hours — with 63 percent enrolled in a credential-seeking program. Critically, tuition for concurrent enrollment in Colorado is covered by state policy, meaning the cost barrier that stymies access in other states is largely removed here.

That policy tailwind matters enormously for Colorado charter schools: the infrastructure exists, and tuition cost is already off the table. The remaining work is intentional design, strong advising, and reaching students who wouldn't otherwise raise their hands.

Lotus School for Excellence, a tuition-free K–12 STEAM charter school in Aurora, Colorado, serving a diverse and historically underserved community of more than 1,300 students, reflects what a coherent college-access scaffolding can look like in this environment. The school offers college classes to eligible students, has integrated Pre-AP coursework into every high school core subject, provides AP courses across most subject areas, and maintains dedicated College & Career Resources — the kind of layered, advising-supported structure that gives dual enrollment pathways real direction rather than leaving students to navigate them alone.

What Families Should Ask Before Enrolling

If you're evaluating a charter school's dual enrollment or concurrent enrollment program, these are the right questions to ask:

  • Are courses free to students? In Colorado, concurrent enrollment tuition is covered by state law — but fees and textbook costs can still add up. Ask specifically.
  • Where do the credits transfer? Credits earned through a community college partner may or may not transfer cleanly to a four-year university. Get specifics in writing.
  • Who advises students through the process? Dual enrollment without structured counseling frequently results in credits that don't connect to anything meaningful downstream.
  • When does the pathway start? The most impactful programs begin building academic readiness and self-efficacy in middle school, well before a student sits in a college classroom.
  • What is the school's completion rate for dual enrollment courses? A high enrollment rate paired with a low completion rate is a red flag worth exploring.

The Bottom Line

What is dual enrollment, and does it actually help underserved students? Yes — when designed with intention, it is one of the strongest equity levers available in K–12 education. Research consistently shows the benefits are largest for low-income students, first-generation college-goers, and students of color: precisely the populations most mission-driven charter schools are built to serve. The distinction between schools that do this well and schools that do it superficially comes down to whether dual enrollment is treated as a structural college-access strategy — built into advising, counseling, and curriculum from middle school onward — or simply listed as an option in a course catalog.

Charter schools that close that gap aren't just helping students earn cheaper college credits. They're changing the trajectory of what college means for an entire family.
