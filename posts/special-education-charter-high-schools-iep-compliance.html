Special Ed at Charter High Schools: What You Must Know

# Special Education at Charter High Schools: Rights, Responsibilities, and What Families Should Expect

When families of students with disabilities consider a charter high school, a reasonable first question is: Will my child's IEP be honored here? The short answer is yes — and charter schools that communicate this clearly, build strong compliance systems, and weave inclusion into their everyday model are increasingly winning the trust of families who once assumed charters weren't built for students with special needs.

Here's what charter leaders and families actually need to know.

Charter High Schools Are Legally Bound by IDEA — Full Stop

There is sometimes a misconception that charter schools exist outside the rules governing special education. They do not. A charter school must carry out the IEP of every eligible student it enrolls, just as any other public school would. Federal guidance is clear: students with disabilities keep all of their IDEA rights when they attend a charter school.

That obligation covers the full scope of what IDEA requires: Free Appropriate Public Education (FAPE), evaluation timelines, annual IEP meetings, progress monitoring, and procedural safeguards. A student who enrolls at a charter high school with an active IEP cannot experience a gap in services while the school "gets organized." IDEA requires the school to provide services comparable to the existing IEP without delay, then either adopt that IEP or develop a new one with the family. A gap in services is a compliance problem — not an allowable adjustment period.

LEA Status: Who Is Actually Responsible for What?

The practical question isn't whether a charter must serve students with disabilities — it's who is responsible for each part of the process. That depends on how the charter is classified under its state's law.

A Local Education Agency (LEA) is the public entity legally responsible for providing special education in its area. When a charter school is its own LEA, it holds the full set of IDEA responsibilities directly: Child Find, evaluations, IEP development, service delivery, progress monitoring, and state reporting. When a charter is treated as part of the authorizing district's LEA, the district retains meaningful responsibility for ensuring eligible students receive FAPE — even though the student attends the charter.

Problems tend to appear in the gap between these two models. If neither party has confirmed its role, both may assume the other is handling evaluations or services, and the student is left waiting. Clarifying that arrangement — in writing, before the school year begins — is what keeps it from happening.

Charter leaders should also keep in mind that LEA classification is not universal. Two charters that look nearly identical can carry different responsibilities simply because they were authorized under different state frameworks. The rules don't automatically travel with you across state lines.

What the Research Says: Do Students With Disabilities Thrive in Charters?

Do students with disabilities actually do well in charter schools? Emerging 2026 research says yes — often as well as or better than in traditional public schools.

A Michigan State University study published in February 2026 examined students with disabilities in Michigan charter schools, finding that when students with disabilities switched from traditional public to charter schools, they perform just as well, despite spending less time in intensive programs and more time in general education classrooms. Academic performance and attendance improved for both students with and without disabilities after entering charter schools — with absences falling by 3.3 percentage points in the first year after charter entry, according to underlying research cited in coverage of the study.

Even several years after switching, students in charters were less likely to be assigned to intensive, costly programs that separate them from their peers — yet their outcomes held. The study's authors note that this raises important questions about whether traditional public schools are deploying special education resources as efficiently as they could be, particularly for students with relatively mild disabilities.

A 2019 Boston study reached a similar conclusion: charters outperformed traditional public schools at preparing students with disabilities to meet college-level standards. The research suggests schools can improve outcomes for special education students without relying solely on traditional, pull-out specialized services.

Taken together, this body of evidence challenges the entrenched narrative that charter schools are poor fits for students with disabilities. The data increasingly points the other way — particularly for charters that combine rigorous academics with genuinely individualized support.

Inclusion in Practice: What It Looks Like at the High School Level

Inclusion isn't just a legal checkbox — it's a teaching model. At the high school level, best-practice inclusion typically means:

  • Push-in support over pull-out: Special education staff co-teach or provide real-time scaffolding inside the general education classroom, keeping students with their peers during core instruction
  • Flexible pacing tied to grade-level standards: Accommodations that open access to rigorous content, not a watered-down substitute curriculum
  • IEP implementation by every teacher: Accommodations live in every classroom, not just with the special education coordinator
  • Proactive progress monitoring: Regular, data-driven check-ins that surface problems early and are shared transparently with families
  • Child Find obligations taken seriously: A well-run charter identifies students who may need evaluation even if they arrive without a prior diagnosis — a reasonable suspicion of a disability affecting learning is enough to begin the process

For charter high schools built around individualized or accelerated learning, many of these practices are already woven into the school's everyday model — which may help explain why the research shows competitive outcomes for students with IEPs at charters.

Sena HS, a tuition-free charter high school in Albuquerque serving grades 9–12, is an example of how a mission-driven model and serious special education infrastructure can coexist. The school's published special education policy explicitly aligns with IDEA 2004, New Mexico's 6.31.2 NMAC special education regulations, FAPE requirements, and Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act — and the school operates as a public school under Albuquerque Public Schools' LEA, with its IEP process, evaluation process, and continuum-of-services documentation publicly available on its website. That kind of transparency tells families upfront that special education is a first-class part of the school's operation, not an afterthought.

Six Questions Every Family Should Ask a Charter High School

If you're a parent of a student with an IEP or a 504 Plan who is evaluating a charter high school, push past the brochure and ask these directly:

  1. Is the school its own LEA, or does the authorizing district remain responsible for special education services? This determines who you contact if something isn't being implemented correctly.
  2. Will my child's current IEP be honored from Day 1 — before any new IEP is developed? The legal answer must be yes.
  3. How does special education support work in classrooms — push-in, pull-out, or a combination?
  4. Who coordinates annual IEP meetings, and how are families notified and included in the process?
  5. What does your Child Find process look like? A school that can answer this with specifics is one that takes its obligations seriously.
  6. Can I see your special education policy, FAPE documentation, or continuum-of-services framework?

A well-run charter high school will answer all of these questions without hesitation. One that deflects, refers you only to the district, or seems unfamiliar with its own LEA status may not yet have its systems in order.

The Compliance-Quality Connection

Charter schools that treat special education as a compliance burden tend to do it poorly. Those that treat it as an extension of their core mission — serving every student who enrolls — tend to do it well, and the research increasingly backs that up.

When inclusion is implemented thoughtfully, students with disabilities spend more time engaged in grade-level learning alongside their peers, and teachers have clearer systems for meeting diverse needs without lowering expectations for anyone. The schools generating strong outcomes for students with IEPs aren't necessarily the ones with the most elaborate specialized programs. They're the ones with high expectations, strong classroom instruction, and systems that make sure no student — with or without a disability — falls through the cracks.

For families, that's the real signal: not just whether a school has a special education program, but whether high expectations and individualized support are genuinely baked into how the school operates every single day.
