Dual Enrollment Gap: Why Charter Schools Are Missing Out

# Dual Enrollment's Equity Gap: Why Charter Schools Are Leaving College Credit on the Table

College credit is one of the most powerful tools a high school can put in a student's hands—and most charter schools aren't offering it. While dual enrollment has boomed nationally, charter campuses participate at a rate that should alarm every leader who is serious about college access.

The data is stark, and the opportunity is real. Here's what charter school leaders and families need to know.

The Numbers Charter Schools Can't Ignore

Participation in dual and concurrent enrollment has exploded over the past decade. According to the National Alliance of Concurrent Enrollment Partnerships (NACEP), roughly 34 percent of U.S. high school students now take at least one college course before graduation—a threefold increase from the 10 percent participation rate recorded in 2010. A 2024 study from the Community College Research Center (CCRC) at Columbia University found that dual enrollment grew by 46 percent between 2015 and 2021, then climbed another 18 percent from 2021 to 2023.

Yet charter schools are largely sitting this trend out. NACEP data shows that 76 percent of traditional public high schools and 72 percent of magnet schools offer dual or concurrent enrollment programs. Among charter schools, that figure drops to just 40 percent—a 36-percentage-point gap relative to traditional schools.

That is not a minor disparity. It means six in ten charter school students likely attend a campus where they cannot earn a single college credit before walking across the graduation stage.

What Dual Enrollment Actually Does for Students

What does the research show about whether dual enrollment helps students get to and through college?

The evidence is consistent and compelling. NACEP's synthesis of multiple peer-reviewed studies found that students who complete college courses in high school are substantially more likely to graduate high school, enroll in college, stay enrolled, and complete a degree—often in less time than their peers. A landmark CCRC study found that 88 percent of dual enrollment participants went on to college after high school and completed degrees at higher rates and in shorter timeframes than non-participating peers.

The equity benefits are especially significant for the students charter schools most often serve. Low-income students who participate in dual enrollment are nearly 10 percent more likely to enter college after high school graduation than similar peers who did not participate. Students in majority-minority school districts who took college courses in high school were 32 to 56 percent more likely to go on to college than non-participating students in the same districts. And in at least one state-level study, six-year college graduation rates for students of color who participated in dual enrollment were 33 percent higher than those of non-participating peers—12 percentage points higher even than white dual enrollment participants.

These are not marginal effects. They are the kinds of outcomes that college-access-focused charter networks spend years and considerable resources trying to achieve through other means.

Why the Charter School Gap Exists

Several structural factors explain why charter schools lag so far behind:

  • Scale constraints. Smaller enrollment makes it harder to hit the minimum course-size thresholds that many community colleges require for on-site dual enrollment sections.
  • Instructor credentialing. Dual enrollment taught by high school teachers requires instructors to meet college-level faculty standards—typically 18 graduate credit hours in the subject area—which many charter teachers, even excellent ones, may not yet hold.
  • Institutional relationship-building. Establishing a formal partnership with a community college or university requires sustained administrative attention that lean charter leadership teams may not have bandwidth for.
  • Urban geography. NACEP data shows that only 57 percent of urban high schools offer dual enrollment, compared to 77 percent of town and rural schools. Most charter schools are urban, which compounds an already difficult landscape.
  • Poverty concentration. High-poverty high schools are 19 percent less likely to offer dual enrollment programs than their low-poverty counterparts—and high concentrations of low-income students are a defining feature of most charter networks.

These barriers are real. They are not, however, insurmountable—and a surging wave of state legislation is actively dismantling them.

The 2025 Policy Moment: All 50 States Now Have a Framework

Charter leaders should pay close attention to what happened in 2025. The College in High School Alliance's year-end legislative tracker counted 234 dual enrollment bills introduced across 45 states, resulting in 55 new laws enacted in 29 states—up from 39 new laws in 2024. Most significantly, New York became the 50th state to adopt a statewide dual enrollment policy, completing national policy coverage for the first time in the program's modern history.

The legislative activity clustered around the exact friction points that most discourage charter participation: credit transfer guarantees, tuition elimination, and course access expansion. In Illinois, for instance, new legislation created a standing Dual Credit Committee and a formal appeals process for instructor credentialing disputes—directly addressing one of charter schools' most common barriers to entry. States are, in effect, engineering away the excuses.

Charter leaders operating in states that passed dual enrollment legislation in 2025 should conduct a deliberate audit of what their state now makes possible—and, critically, what it funds.

What Implementation Actually Requires

Noble - Baker College Prep, one campus within Noble Schools' network of tuition-free, 6–12 public charter schools in Chicago—a network recognized nationally for college access and persistence and built around the mission of equipping students to complete college and lead choice-filled lives—illustrates the broader reality facing college-prep charter campuses everywhere: a genuine commitment to getting students through college increasingly depends on whether those students arrive at college already knowing they can do college-level work. Dual enrollment is the most direct proof of that.

For charter schools ready to build or expand dual enrollment access, practitioners and researchers recommend a focused sequence:

  1. Map your state's funding landscape first. Many states now subsidize or fully eliminate tuition costs for dual enrollment participants, especially low-income students. Know exactly what your students can access at no cost before designing a program around assumptions that may no longer be accurate.
  1. Start with a community college partnership, not a four-year university. Community colleges typically offer more flexible partnership structures, lower instructor credential thresholds, and deeper institutional experience working with high schools.
  1. Address teacher credentialing proactively. Identify which current faculty members are close to meeting college-level credential requirements and build a professional development pathway—rather than waiting for a promising partnership to collapse over this issue at the last minute.
  1. Design for your highest-need students, not just your highest-performing ones. Research consistently shows dual enrollment's largest measurable impact is among students from low-income backgrounds and students of color who are on the margin of college-going. Eligibility criteria that screen out those students undercut the entire equity rationale.
  1. Negotiate credit transfer guarantees upfront. Dual enrollment credits that don't transfer are a waste of students' time and families' trust. Make portability a non-negotiable condition of any college partnership agreement before a single student enrolls.
  1. Use virtual and hybrid delivery strategically. For small campuses that cannot fill a full section in a single subject area, many community colleges now offer hybrid dual enrollment models that allow students to take courses partially or fully online while still earning genuine, transferable college credit.

The Enrollment Signal Families Should Ask About

For families evaluating charter schools, dual enrollment participation is a meaningful proxy for a school's real college-prep infrastructure—not just its aspirations. A school that talks about college readiness but offers no pathway to college credit before graduation is describing a goal. A school that has negotiated institutional partnerships, cleared the credentialing hurdles, enrolled students in actual college courses, and tracked whether those credits transfer—that school is doing something demonstrably different.

As the policy environment continues to lower barriers and the research case for dual enrollment's impact on first-generation and low-income students becomes impossible to dismiss, the 40-percent charter participation rate looks less like a structural inevitability and more like an unfinished equity agenda.

The credits are real. The state-level momentum is real. Charter schools that move deliberately now will give their students a head start that compounds for years after graduation.
