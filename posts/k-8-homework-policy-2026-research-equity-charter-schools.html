K-8 Homework Policy: What 2026 Research Tells Families

# K-8 Homework Policy: What the 2026 Research Tells Families

Few school decisions follow a child home the way homework does — literally. And in 2026, a wave of new research and national survey data is challenging assumptions that educators, leaders, and families have long held about out-of-school assignments. For families choosing a K-8 charter school, understanding what the evidence actually says about homework — and what a thoughtful homework policy signals about a school's values — may be more useful than any brochure or open-house tour.

Homework Is Declining in American Schools — Here's Why

The numbers from a recent nationwide survey are striking. An EdWeek Research Center survey of 279 teachers found that 40% of respondents said the amount of homework they assigned had decreased over the past two years, while 33% said it had remained the same, and just 3% said it had increased. Perhaps most telling: nearly 1 in 4 teachers — 24% — reported that they don't assign homework at all.

Why the decline? Among teachers who said homework had fallen, the largest share — 47% — cited students simply refusing to do it. The next-highest share, 29%, said that students' use of technology such as AI to complete homework lessens its value. And 28% pointed directly to equity concerns: that some students have meaningfully more access to homework help than others.

That equity concern is not just a hunch. It now has rigorous research behind it.

The Equity Problem Homework Creates at Home

A 2026 study from the University of Manchester, published in the British Journal of Sociology of Education, makes the equity case in unusually direct terms. The researchers argue that homework is not simply an educational activity carried out by children — it also requires parents to invest their time, knowledge, and resources, potentially giving some children a structural advantage over others.

The study found that families able to spend substantial amounts of time supporting homework may gain a real educational edge for their children, while parents who face demanding work schedules, childcare responsibilities, or other pressures may have far less time available, even when they are equally committed to their child's success.

The researchers push the finding further: schools may mistake unequal access to time for unequal commitment. Parents who cannot devote as much time to homework assistance can be perceived — by teachers, by the system — as less engaged with their children's education. This creates what the study describes as a "deficit" view of families who are, in reality, working just as hard as anyone else, in harder circumstances.

There's an additional layer of inequity the study surfaces. More affluent families may be able to compensate for their own limited time by purchasing resources such as private tutoring, while families with fewer financial resources cannot make that substitution. The researchers conclude that educational inequality produced by homework is not simply about how much parents care — it's about who has the resources to convert that care into measurable academic advantage.

For K-8 charter schools that predominantly serve working families, families with multiple children, or families navigating economic pressure, this research should sit at the center of every homework policy conversation.

What Good K-8 Homework Policy Actually Looks Like

The debate isn't a binary "homework vs. no homework." The research points toward something more nuanced: homework done well, in the right amounts, at the right developmental stages, with equity built into the design from the start.

Jessica Calarco, a professor of sociology at the University of Wisconsin-Madison, captures the core challenge plainly: it is "very hard to assign homework at a level that every student can complete fully independently, without needing an adult to help them, and to make sure that all kids have the kinds of life circumstances where they can practice that kind of responsibility."

Janine Bempechat, a clinical professor emerita at Boston University's Wheelock College of Education and Human Development, argues that homework still carries real value — pointing to two main benefits: academic and motivational. When schools begin asking children to do homework, she argues, they are socializing children to become mature, self-directed learners who can prioritize tasks and handle responsibility. But she also agrees the equity problem is real. Rather than eliminating homework altogether, she argues schools can create opportunities for students to complete work during the school day or provide structured assistance after school — a more equitable approach that doesn't simply remove the benefit along with the burden.

For K-8 families evaluating charter schools, the practical question becomes: What does this school's homework philosophy actually look like in action?

Here's what separates an intentional, equity-aware homework policy from a thoughtless default:

  • Age-appropriate volume. Research consistently shows homework has little to no measurable academic benefit for early elementary students. A K-2 policy centered on shared reading at home looks very different — and is far more defensible — than worksheets sent home nightly.
  • Time guardrails. A well-designed policy sets explicit, realistic time expectations (the commonly cited guideline is roughly 10 minutes per grade level per night) rather than leaving families to absorb whatever spills out of the school day.
  • In-school completion time. Schools that build structured time for assignment work into the day — in advisory periods, tutoring blocks, or enrichment time — directly reduce the burden on families who can't always be available in the evenings.
  • Clear, consistent communication. Families who know precisely what is expected — and what is not — can support their children without anxiety or guesswork about whether they're doing enough.
  • Protected break periods. A policy that explicitly shields weekends and school holidays from homework obligations signals that a school understands families, and students, need genuine rest.

A School Design That Can Change the Equation

Schools designed around small class sizes and genuine personalization are often better positioned to accomplish during the school day what less attentive schools push onto families at night. When teachers know every student well and can adjust instruction in real time, there is less need to send unfinished learning home.

Solare Collegiate Charter School, a tuition-free K-8 charter school in Southwest Albuquerque, is built around exactly this kind of intentional model — a 14:1 student-to-teacher ratio, individualized support, academic rigor, and character development woven together within a warm, community-rooted environment. That combination of small class sizes and personalized instruction is precisely the design context in which homework's traditional purpose — filling gaps left by one-size-fits-all classroom instruction — becomes far less necessary.

The AI Wrinkle: One More Reason to Redesign, Not Just Reduce

The homework conversation in 2026 carries a dimension it simply didn't have a few years ago. More than 1 in 4 teachers in the EdWeek Research Center survey said they believe students' use of AI and other technologies is actively undermining homework's instructional value. Schools that haven't updated their homework design to account for this are assigning tasks that a generative AI tool can complete in under a minute — which raises hard questions about what those assignments are actually developing in students.

The schools responding most thoughtfully are treating homework as a genuine extension of classroom relationships: assignments that require a student's own observation, reflection, creative judgment, or personal conversation — things that can't be delegated. That's a deliberate design choice that requires a school to think carefully about every assignment it sends home. Families touring schools in 2026 can and should ask directly: How has your homework design changed in response to AI?

What Families Should Ask When Evaluating K-8 Charter Schools

Beyond the AI question, here are three direct questions worth raising on any school tour:

  1. What is your homework philosophy at each grade band — K-2, 3-5, and 6-8 — and how much time should it realistically take?
  2. Is there structured in-school time for students who need support completing assignments?
  3. How does the school communicate homework expectations to families, and what happens when a student simply cannot complete an assignment at home?

The depth and specificity of a school's answers to these questions reveals far more about its culture and values than a mission statement on a wall.

The Bottom Line for K-8 Families

What the 2026 research makes clear is that homework policy is not a minor logistical footnote — it is a direct expression of how a school thinks about equity, student well-being, and the relationship it wants to have with families. As the University of Manchester researchers put it, when homework rewards families with the most time, knowledge, and money to invest, it can end up reinforcing the very inequalities that education is supposed to overcome.

For families evaluating K-8 charter schools this enrollment season, the question isn't simply whether a school assigns homework. It's whether the school has thought carefully enough about its families' real lives to have developed a philosophy worth explaining out loud — and to have built the school-day structures that make that philosophy honest.

That standard of intentionality is worth holding every school to.
