The Charter Facilities Gap: What Leaders Can Do

# The Charter Facilities Gap: Why Your School Pays More for Its Building Than It Should

Your students deserve updated science labs, functional libraries, and classrooms that don't double as storage rooms. So does every child in a traditional district school down the street. The difference? That district school likely received a share of a local bond measure to fund those improvements. Yours probably got nothing—or close to it.

The charter school facilities funding gap is one of the most persistent, least-discussed structural inequities in American public education. And in 2026, with construction costs still elevated and charter enrollment growing faster than available financing, the problem is getting harder to ignore.

Why Do Charter Schools Receive Less Facilities Funding?

The short answer: charter schools are public schools funded like public schools for instruction, but treated like private entities when it comes to buildings.

Traditional district schools routinely draw on local property tax revenue and voter-approved bond measures to build and renovate facilities. Charter schools, in most states, are explicitly excluded from those same revenue streams—even when charter families pay the same property taxes as everyone else. Charter schools typically receive state per-pupil funding based on attendance (the same mechanism as district schools), but that funding is meant for instruction, not capital projects. The result is that charter leaders must either divert instructional dollars toward rent and repairs, or navigate a complicated and often inaccessible landscape of alternative financing.

As Colorado's League of Charter Schools documented this spring, over the past decade Colorado school districts collected more than $15.5 billion in bond revenue—yet charter schools, which serve more than 15% of the state's public school students, received only about 3% of that total. In one Northern Colorado district, charter students representing 14% of enrollment received roughly one-third of one percent of a $99 million bond. That's not a rounding error. It's a structural problem replicated across dozens of states.

The Real Cost to Schools—and Students

When a charter school spends a disproportionate share of its budget on facilities, the tradeoffs are immediate and visible. School leaders report choosing between updating aging HVAC systems and hiring a reading specialist. Teachers work in classrooms without reliable Wi-Fi or functional lab equipment. Enrollment growth stalls because there's no room to expand.

For schools in under-resourced communities—where roughly two-thirds of U.S. charter schools operate—the burden is compounded. Traditional commercial lenders are often reluctant to extend credit to charter schools, which lack the hard-asset collateral that real estate financing typically requires. Schools that do access commercial debt frequently pay interest rates far above what district-run schools pay through bond markets.

What's Changing: New Financing Tools in 2026

The good news is that the financing landscape is shifting—slowly, but meaningfully. Several mission-driven lenders and federal programs are creating pathways that didn't exist a decade ago.

Federal credit enhancements have become a key lever. The U.S. Department of Education's Credit Enhancement for Charter School Facilities Program provides guarantees that allow community development financial institutions (CDFIs) to offer charter schools more favorable loan terms—higher loan-to-value limits, longer interest-only periods, and predevelopment loans that conventional lenders won't touch. Earlier this year, the Equitable Facilities Fund issued $300 million in tax-exempt social bonds, offering qualifying charter schools long-term, low-cost financing at interest rates around 4.5%. The fund estimates its model saves borrowers roughly $60 million that can be reinvested in students.

Similarly, 22Beacon (formerly Charter Schools Development Corp.) and the Opportunity Finance Network closed a $100 million Treasury-backed bond issuance in 2026 specifically to help charters acquire and renovate facilities. Level Field Facilities Fund launched a $50 million national fund focused on early-stage capital—the riskiest phase of a school's facilities journey—using a pilot program that had already helped leverage $110 million in long-term debt across seven states.

Revolving loan funds at the state level are another emerging tool. In states like Idaho, these funds provide low- or no-interest early-stage capital that recycles back into new projects as schools repay, creating a self-sustaining pipeline.

Repurposing underutilized buildings is gaining traction too. Several states are creating incentives that allow charter schools to redevelop vacant or underused structures—lowering construction costs while revitalizing communities.

This is precisely the kind of systemic inequity that state associations exist to name and fight. Nevada Charters—the Charter School Association of Nevada (CSAN), a nonprofit membership organization that supports and advocates for nearly 90 public charter schools across the state—calls out the problem explicitly on its own website, noting that Nevada's charter students "on average, are funded less than the average district student due to Nevada's antiquated system of school finance" and that charter schools in the state "do not receive funds from local tax revenue that typically help with school facilities funding." That kind of frank, public acknowledgment from a state association matters: it signals to member schools, legislators, and funders alike that the gap is real, documented, and worth fixing.

What Charter Leaders Can Do Right Now

Waiting for state legislatures to fix the funding formula is a strategy, but it's a slow one. Here's what forward-thinking charter leaders are doing in the meantime:

  • Conduct a facilities audit with an educational ROI lens. Before pursuing any financing, map your current facility gaps to their academic impact. Which deficiencies are actively suppressing student outcomes? This framing makes a stronger case to lenders and policymakers alike.
  • Explore CDFI lenders before approaching commercial banks. CDFIs like 22Beacon, Reinvestment Fund, and Nonprofit Finance Fund specialize in charter school real estate and understand your financing structure in ways traditional banks typically don't.
  • Ask about credit enhancement programs. Many school leaders don't know the DOE's Credit Enhancement for Charter School Facilities Program exists. If you're borrowing for facilities, ask any potential lender whether they participate.
  • Build a "capital stack." The most financially resilient charter schools layer multiple funding sources—federal credits, state grants, philanthropic capital, and mission-aligned debt—rather than relying on a single lender or program.
  • Engage your state association and elected officials. Per-pupil facilities funding remains the single most effective long-term policy lever. Policymakers respond to constituent stories, not abstract data. If your school's facilities situation carries an academic cost, that story needs to be told at the statehouse.

The Bottom Line on Charter Facilities Funding

Why do charter schools pay more to house their students than district schools? Because most state finance systems were designed before charter schools existed, and they haven't been updated to reflect a public education landscape where more than 3.7 million students now attend charters nationwide. The fix requires both policy change and creative financing—and in 2026, both are more accessible than they were even two years ago.

For charter leaders, the most dangerous posture is assuming the system will catch up on its own. The schools navigating this moment most successfully are treating facilities as a strategic priority—not an afterthought—and building the lender relationships, advocacy coalitions, and financial literacy to back that up.

Buildings don't teach students. But they shape everything that's possible for the teachers and leaders who do.
