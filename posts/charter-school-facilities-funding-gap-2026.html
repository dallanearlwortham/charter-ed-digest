The Charter Facilities Gap: Why It's Costing Students

# The Charter Facilities Gap: Why It's Costing Students in 2026-27

Every dollar a charter school spends on rent, mortgage payments, or deferred maintenance is a dollar not spent on teachers, curriculum, or student support. That trade-off isn't a budgeting failure—it's a structural feature baked into most state funding systems, and it's getting worse as charter enrollment grows faster than the policies designed to support it.

The facilities funding gap is one of the least-discussed but most consequential inequities in K-12 education finance. Here's what it looks like in concrete terms, why it exists, and what charter leaders can realistically do about it now.

What Is the Charter Facilities Funding Gap?

Across fifteen major U.S. cities studied by the Thomas B. Fordham Institute, the average funding gap between charter schools and traditional district schools is 29 percent—a $5,721-per-pupil disadvantage. That gap hasn't closed; it has widened in more cities than it has narrowed.

The core driver is access to local tax revenue. In eight of the fifteen cities studied, charter school sectors received no local funds whatsoever. District schools routinely tap property tax revenue to finance construction bonds, renovations, and capital improvements. Charter schools—which are public schools serving public school students—are typically locked out of those same streams entirely. More state funding for charters sometimes partially offsets the shortfall, but in most cities it isn't enough to overcome the local deficit.

The result: per-pupil gaps range from modest in the best-performing cities to nearly $15,000 in the worst. All but three of the fifteen cities studied earned D or F grades on funding disparity. In more cities than not, the gap has widened since researchers first began tracking it.

The Capital Budget Divide

The inequity is sharpest when capital budgets are compared directly. District schools in most major metro areas can issue construction bonds backed by property tax revenue, access dedicated capital levies, and draw on general obligation debt to build and maintain facilities. Charter schools serving those same communities get none of it. Their students are public school students, but their buildings are funded as though they aren't.

A national analysis found that charter schools collectively face a gap of more than $13 billion in total public funding compared to district schools serving similar populations. That gap is not evenly distributed—it is concentrated most heavily in the capital budgets that determine whether a school has functional labs, updated technology, adequate counseling space, and buildings that don't require constant triage. When charter schools lack access to capital funding, they fill the gap the only way they can: by diverting operating dollars that were meant for instruction.

How Texas Is Addressing the Gap

Analysis tied to House Bill 2 found that Texas charter schools are funded an average of $1,621 less per student than independent school districts, with the disparity driven specifically by unequal facilities funding. The state provides a per-ADA facilities allotment for charter schools, but total available funding is capped at $60 million annually—a ceiling that has not kept pace with Texas charter enrollment growth of nearly 30 percent between 2020 and 2024.

To begin closing that gap, the Texas Legislature created the State Facilities Incentive Grant (SFIG), administered by the Texas Education Agency. The SFIG channels supplemental facilities dollars to high-performing Texas charter schools serving communities with the greatest need for educational choice. The 2026–2028 SFIG cycle is currently underway, with preliminary awards going to Texas charter networks that meet the grant's performance and community-need criteria. Texans Can Academies - Carrollton Farmer's Branch is a campus within Texans Can Academies—a charter network based and operating entirely in Texas—and the network was among those selected for a preliminary SFIG award in the current cycle, actively securing Texas state facilities funding rather than absorbing the gap through cuts to instruction.

What Does the Gap Actually Cost Students?

What does the charter facilities funding gap mean for student outcomes?

The answer is direct: when a school diverts operating dollars to facility costs that district schools cover through separate capital channels, instructional quality suffers. It shows up in compressed teacher salary scales that make recruiting and retention harder, deferred technology upgrades, and the absence of specialized spaces—science labs, counseling suites, career and technical education facilities—that better-resourced district schools take for granted.

Research consistently shows that a charter receiving far less per pupil than its district peer still struggles to keep teacher salaries competitive even if its overall budget seems adequate in isolation. That problem is compounded by who charter schools typically serve: higher proportions of economically disadvantaged students, English language learners, and students who have cycled through schools that weren't working for them. National authorizer research has documented how chronic underfunding causes inequity to be misread as underperformance—outcomes judged without any accounting for the resource gap that produced them.

What Charter Leaders Can Do Right Now

The underlying funding structure isn't changing overnight. But leaders who treat facilities finance as a year-round strategic priority—rather than an annual budget scramble—consistently find more options than those who don't.

  • Audit your state's charter facility allotment in full detail. Many schools leave money on the table because they don't fully understand their state's formula, applicable caps, or supplemental grant programs. Know exactly what you're entitled to before exploring alternatives.
  • Pursue CDFI and mission-aligned bond financing. Community Development Financial Institutions and lenders like the Equitable Facilities Fund—which recently issued $300 million in tax-exempt social bonds for charter school facilities nationally—offer below-market rates built specifically for charter schools. These are planning tools, not emergency measures; they work best when a school enters the process with clean financials and a multi-year facilities plan.
  • Negotiate access to underutilized district facilities. A growing number of states now require or incentivize districts to make vacant buildings available to charter schools. Where this has been implemented, it meaningfully offsets unmet facility needs without requiring new state appropriations.
  • Separate capital and operating budgets in your board reporting. Leaders who can clearly show their board the true per-pupil cost of facilities—isolated from instructional spending—are better positioned to make the case for facility investment to philanthropists and authorizers alike.
  • Bring specific data to your authorizer. Authorizers across the country have repeatedly recommended policies to improve charter school sustainability, but those recommendations gain legislative traction when individual schools supply concrete, school-level cost data rather than sector-wide averages.

The Equity Argument Charter Leaders Should Be Making

There's a narrative trap facilities funding discussions often fall into: framing the issue as charter schools versus district schools. The stronger, more durable argument centers students.

Charter schools disproportionately serve Black and Latino students, students from low-income families, and students who have been underserved by the schools assigned to them by zip code. When those students' schools are forced to redirect instructional dollars to facility costs while neighboring district schools bond-finance new construction with property tax revenue, the result isn't a funding formula quirk—it's an equity problem with a paper trail. That framing has proven more effective with legislators and the public than comparative funding charts alone, and the data fully supports it.

Facilities funding won't be resolved in a single legislative session anywhere in the country. But charter leaders who understand the gap, quantify its real cost in their own school's budget, and pursue every available financing and policy lever are the ones best positioned to protect their students from its worst effects.
