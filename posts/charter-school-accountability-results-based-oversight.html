Charter Accountability: Why Closure Is a Feature, Not a Bug

# Charter Accountability: Why Closure Is a Feature, Not a Bug

When the Texas Education Agency confirmed that seven charter schools would close before the 2026–27 school year — including one whose charter was revoked after it agreed to hit a minimum academic benchmark and then earned an F anyway — critics treated the headlines as an indictment of the entire charter model. Charter school leaders and advocates should do the opposite: own the story, and explain clearly why a system that can close failing schools is doing exactly what it was designed to do.

Results-based accountability isn't a weakness the sector has to explain away. It's the charter model's most honest and defensible differentiator — and in 2026, making that case clearly is more important than ever.

The Charter Bargain, Plainly Stated

The foundational deal behind every charter school is straightforward: in exchange for operational autonomy — over curriculum, staffing, schedule, and school culture — a charter school accepts a performance contract with real consequences for failure. Meet the terms, earn renewal. Fall short and fail to improve, lose the charter.

Traditional public schools operate under an entirely different arrangement. Closure for academic underperformance is rare, slow, politically contentious, and frequently reversed. Schools that have been rated as persistently failing for years routinely continue to operate because the political and procedural barriers to closing them are too high.

That asymmetry is worth stating directly and confidently, not defensively. Parents, policymakers, and the broader public deserve to understand which system is structurally designed to hold schools accountable for what happens to kids — and which one largely isn't.

What's Happening With Accountability Right Now

The 2026 policy landscape shows that states are taking the authorizer side of this bargain more seriously, not less.

In South Carolina, legislators finalized a major accountability bill this spring after two high-profile authorizer failures exposed gaps in oversight. The bill requires charter schools to post more financial information publicly, gives the state education agency authority to order closures of low-performing schools, and closes the "authorizer shopping" loophole — the practice of underperforming schools quietly switching to a more lenient authorizer to escape accountability. As one state senator put it, when a charter school escapes accountability, students are stuck with a subpar education.

That framing is exactly right. Accountability isn't a compliance burden on good schools — it's the mechanism that protects students from bad ones.

In Washington State, the 2026 annual charter school report describes a "growth-oriented accountability" model that applies simultaneous evaluation across academic performance, financial health, and organizational governance. Strong-performing schools earned expansion approvals. Weak performers faced intervention. That's the model working as intended.

The Academic Case for Accountability-Backed Autonomy

Rigorous accountability matters because it creates the conditions in which high-performing charter schools can justify their existence — and the research shows that, in those conditions, many charter schools are delivering meaningfully better outcomes, especially for students who have historically been underserved.

Stanford University's Center for Research on Education Outcomes (CREDO) found that a typical Black student learned the equivalent of 40 more days of reading in a year at a charter school than at a comparable traditional public school. Hundreds of charter schools — what CREDO researchers called "gap busters" — had eliminated the racial achievement gap between Black and Hispanic students and white students entirely.

A 2026 Chalkbeat analysis of Indiana's ILEARN results found that Indianapolis charter schools posted higher proficiency rates for Black and Hispanic students in both math and English than comparable schools run by Indianapolis Public Schools. Researchers pointed to extended instructional time, flexible staffing, and targeted tutoring partnerships — all direct products of the autonomy that accountability makes legitimate.

None of those outcomes happen by accident. They happen in schools that know their performance is being measured, compared, and acted upon — and that knowledge shapes culture from the top of the organization down.

What Strong Authorizing Actually Looks Like

Not all accountability is created equal. The quality of a state's authorizing infrastructure determines whether the charter bargain is meaningful or mostly cosmetic. Based on research and best-practice frameworks from authorizers across the country, the strongest systems share these characteristics:

  • Multi-dimensional performance metrics. Academic outcomes, financial health, and governance are evaluated together. A school that posts good test scores but mismanages its finances — or vice versa — is not a fully accountable school.
  • Pre-defined, public performance thresholds. Schools know in advance exactly what constitutes acceptable performance, what triggers intervention, and what leads to nonrenewal or revocation. No surprises.
  • Tiered intervention before closure. Quality authorizers don't go straight to closure. They offer structured improvement plans, technical assistance, and defined timelines — giving schools a genuine path to course-correct before consequences escalate.
  • Transparent public reporting. Performance data is published in accessible formats so families, journalists, and community members can evaluate schools independently — not buried in administrative filings.
  • Renewal earned, not assumed. Charter renewals are granted based on demonstrated results over the full contract term, not on a school's ability to avoid controversy.

Georgia's State Charter Schools Commission publishes comprehensive performance framework results — academic, organizational, and fiscal — for all state charter schools, with proposed scoring methodology changes reviewed at public meetings. That transparency is a model for what accountable authorizing looks like.

The Infrastructure That Helps Schools Perform

Strong accountability doesn't just punish weak schools — it creates the environment where capable ones thrive. But schools don't navigate that environment alone. State charter associations serve as essential infrastructure for helping member schools understand what's expected, build internal capacity, and stay current on the policy changes that shape their accountability obligations.

Organizations like North Carolina Assocation for Public Charter Schools — a statewide association whose mission centers explicitly on support, education, and advocacy for North Carolina's charter schools — provide member schools with the professional development, policy guidance, and legislative engagement that makes it possible to perform well under rigorous accountability frameworks. When schools understand the standards and have support building toward them, accountability becomes an engine for growth rather than a source of dread.

What Charter Leaders Should Say to Families

Parents choosing charter schools are frequently making that choice because they want a school that has skin in the game — one that can't simply coast on inertia. Lean into that expectation and meet it directly. Three messages that are both accurate and compelling:

"Our charter can be revoked if we don't deliver results." Most parents have never heard a school leader say this. It is honest, and it is powerful. It reframes accountability as a commitment to families rather than a compliance obligation to regulators.

"Our performance is publicly reported and independently reviewed." Transparency is trust. The ability to point parents to publicly available academic, financial, and governance data is a competitive advantage most district schools cannot match.

"We earn renewal — it's not automatic." This framing positions the school as one that has actively demonstrated its worth, not one that simply exists by default.

These aren't spin. They are accurate descriptions of how the charter contract is supposed to work. Saying them clearly, and regularly, builds the community confidence that sustains enrollment, staff retention, and long-term school health.

The Bottom Line

What is results-based accountability in charter schools? It is the contractual requirement that charter schools demonstrate measurable academic, financial, and organizational performance to retain their operating authority — with nonrenewal or revocation as the consequence for sustained failure. It is what makes charter autonomy a public trust rather than a private privilege.

Closure headlines aren't a scandal. They are the system functioning correctly. A sector that can honestly say "the schools operating here have earned the right to be open" is offering families something fundamentally different from a system where longevity is the default.

In 2026, with school choice expanding and public scrutiny of the charter sector at a high, the strongest case for the model isn't autonomy alone. It's autonomy that is earned, measured, and accountable to the students it serves. That's a case every charter school leader should be prepared to make — and proud to make.
