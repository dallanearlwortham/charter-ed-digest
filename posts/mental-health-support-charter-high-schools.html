Mental Health at Charter High Schools: What Works

The Mental Health Gap Is Charter Schools' Problem Too

High school is already hard. For many charter school students — first-generation college-goers, teenagers who transferred after struggling elsewhere, young people navigating poverty or housing instability — it is considerably harder. And the national data make plain that the mental health crisis shadowing American adolescents is not easing up.

In the 2024–2025 school year, 58% of schools reported that the number of students seeking school-based mental health services had increased from the prior year — and there was a 61% spike in staff concerns about students exhibiting depression, anxiety, trauma, or emotional dysregulation. Meanwhile, approximately half of public schools reported they could effectively provide mental health services to all students in need. That means roughly half could not.

For charter high school leaders, that gap is not an abstraction. It shows up in attendance data, in discipline referrals, and in the student who quietly stops coming back.

Why the Funding Ground Just Shifted

For years, many schools patched together mental health capacity through a combination of federal grants, pandemic-era relief dollars, and Medicaid reimbursements. All three of those streams are now under pressure simultaneously.

Federal funding for school mental health whiplashed dramatically: the Department of Education halted nearly $1 billion in school mental health grants in April 2025, then awarded $208 million in new grants in December 2025 — leaving net federal investment well below Bipartisan Safer Communities Act levels. Medicaid, the largest federal payer of school-based clinical behavioral health services, is also shrinking following cuts in the 2025 reconciliation bill. The share of public schools receiving any federal grant funding for mental health dropped from 53% in 2021–2022 to 33% in 2024–2025 — a trend already in motion before the most recent cuts, pushing more of the cost onto school budgets.

The downstream effects are measurable. A Healthy Schools Campaign survey of district leaders found 80% expect reductions and layoffs of school health staff, and 70% anticipate cuts to mental and behavioral health services specifically.

Charter high schools — which often operate on leaner per-pupil allocations than traditional public schools and without the central-office infrastructure large districts use to spread mental health staffing costs — are disproportionately exposed to this squeeze. That makes the question of what actually works more urgent, not less.

What Does School-Based Mental Health Support Actually Do?

The most important framing shift for charter leaders: school-based mental health is not a nice-to-have. For many students, it is the only realistic access point for care.

Students are roughly six times more likely to access mental health care at school than anywhere else. More than 70% of children who receive mental health services receive at least some of that care in a school setting, according to the National Association of School Psychologists. School-based services can improve access to care, allow for early identification and treatment of mental health issues, and are linked to reduced absenteeism and better mental health and substance use outcomes. They can also reduce access barriers for underserved populations — including students from low-income households and students of color — who face the steepest obstacles to off-campus clinical care.

When those services are integrated with a school's broader support structure rather than bolted on as an afterthought, the results are striking. Independent research conducted by the Yale Child Study Center evaluating high-fidelity clinical programs found that 95% of participating students were prevented from therapeutic out-of-district placement — students who stayed in their schools, with their peers, in their communities. The same research found 35% fewer disciplinary incidents, 31% fewer absences, and a 56% reduction in hospital stays.

The programming landscape has expanded as well. In the 2024–2025 school year, 97% of public schools offered at least one type of mental health service to students, with individual one-on-one counseling or therapy being most common (83% of schools). The use of telehealth to deliver mental health treatment has grown from 17% to 22% between 2021–2022 and 2024–2025 — a channel that is especially relevant for small charter schools navigating staffing constraints.

Five Practical Levers for Charter High Schools

Given funding constraints and a national shortage of licensed school mental health professionals, charter schools cannot simply replicate large-district behavioral health infrastructure. But they have structural advantages — smaller enrollment, stronger staff-student relationships, mission-driven culture — that large comprehensive high schools often lack. The goal is to leverage those advantages into a durable, layered support system.

1. Build early-identification into the school's existing culture. Half of lifetime mental health conditions begin by age 14, and three-quarters by age 24 — meaning the students who will struggle this year are, statistically, already enrolled, most of them not yet on anyone's caseload. Charter schools where every teacher knows every student by name have a built-in advantage in catching early warning signs before a quiet withdrawal becomes a dropout. Universal behavioral health screening — still offered by only about one-third of public schools despite being considered a best practice — is worth building toward, even incrementally.

2. Use telehealth as a force multiplier. For small charters that cannot afford a full-time licensed clinician on staff, telehealth partnerships with community mental health organizations allow schools to provide licensed counseling and therapy on campus without the cost of a full-time hire. This model also removes the stigma and scheduling friction that prevent students from accessing off-campus services on their own.

3. Train every teacher to be a first detector, not a last resort. Teachers are often the first adults to notice when a student is struggling, but they are not equipped — and should not be expected — to provide clinical support. Targeted professional development on recognizing warning signs and making warm referrals dramatically improves identification rates without expanding headcount. The lowest-performing model is one where a student has to self-identify and seek out help. The highest-performing model is one where trusted adults proactively connect students to care.

4. Embed mental health within MTSS, not alongside it. Schools that treat mental health as a standalone program see it cut first in lean budget years. Schools that embed it within their Multi-Tiered System of Supports — Tier 1 including SEL and trauma-informed classroom practices, Tier 2 including structured check-ins and small-group supports, Tier 3 including clinical referral pathways — make it structurally harder to eliminate, and more effective at scale.

5. Pursue durable funding, not grant-to-grant dependence. Districts and schools that sustain mental health investments haven't found secret money — they've restructured around durable funding sources and treat clinical support as core infrastructure rather than a grant-funded extra. For charter high schools, this means pursuing Medicaid reimbursement for eligible services, exploring formal community health center partnerships with memoranda of understanding, and building the enrollment and per-pupil funding case for mental health as a measurable retention and graduation driver.

What Good Looks Like at the School Level

The charter school design — smaller, more relational, built around a specific mission and student population — is genuinely well-suited to the whole-student mental health approach the research supports. What that requires is intentionality, not just goodwill.

Sena HS, a tuition-free charter high school in Albuquerque, New Mexico serving grades 9–12, illustrates how foundational design choices create the conditions for mental health support to actually function. The school's emphasis on small class sizes and one-on-one teacher relationships — built into its accelerated learning model — is precisely the kind of structural setup that allows trusted adults to notice when a student is struggling before a quiet withdrawal becomes a dropout. A student at Sena doesn't disappear into a crowd of 2,000; the adults in the building are close enough to see the change. That relational infrastructure is far harder to build after the fact than to design from the start.

The question for every charter high school leader heading into the 2026–27 school year isn't simply "do we have a counselor on staff?" It's "have we built a school where students feel known well enough to ask for help — and where someone will notice before they even have to ask?"

The Bottom Line

Student mental health continues to worsen by most national measures. More than half (57%) of school-based mental health providers reported a decline in student mental health over the past year, up from 46% the year before. Only 4% saw any improvement. The funding environment is more volatile than it has been in a decade.

But the evidence for what works is clear: early identification, integrated clinical support, strong adult-student relationships, and systems designed to keep students in their schools rather than push them toward out-of-district placements or out the door entirely.

Charter high schools didn't create this crisis. But with the right design, they are positioned to be among the most effective places in the country to address it — not because they have more resources, but because they have fewer places to hide from their students' needs.
