Dual Enrollment in High School: Charter Schools' Edge

# Earning College Credit in High School: Why Charter Schools Have a Hidden Edge — and How to Use It

High school students at flex and independent-study charter schools often hear the same worry from friends in traditional schools: "But do you get the same opportunities?" When it comes to dual enrollment — the ability to earn real college credit while still in high school — the honest answer in 2025 is that charter school students don't just have equal access. With the right model and intentional programming, they can have more.

What Dual Enrollment Actually Is (and Why It Matters Now)

Dual enrollment — also called concurrent enrollment — allows high school students to take college courses and earn both high school and college credit simultaneously, often at no cost to families. Programs go by many names depending on the state and institution, but the core promise is the same: students get a head start on a degree, career credential, or both before they graduate.

The policy momentum behind these programs in 2025 is striking. According to the College in High School Alliance's 2025 Year in Review State Legislative Tracker, 234 bills were introduced across 45 states, resulting in 55 new laws enacted in 29 states — an increase from 39 new laws in 2024, and a return to the higher levels of activity seen in 2022 and 2023. Notably, 2025 also marked a historic milestone: New York became the 50th state to adopt a statewide dual enrollment policy, completing the map of state-level policy coverage nationwide.

In plain terms: every state now has a policy framework for these programs, and legislatures are actively investing in making them stronger and more equitable.

The Equity Case Is Compelling — and Underutilized

Who benefits most from dual enrollment? The research gives a clear answer: students who historically have the least access to college.

Students from low-income backgrounds who participated in dual or concurrent enrollment were nearly 10% more likely to enter college after high school graduation than their non-participating peers. The gains for students of color are even more pronounced. Students in majority-minority school districts that participated in dual or concurrent enrollment were 32–56% more likely to go on to college after high school than non-participating students in the same district.

The financial benefits are concrete, too. One community college in Florida reported saving Pasco and Hernando County high school families approximately $4.15 million in tuition during the 2024–2025 academic year, averaging around $750 per participating student.

Despite all of this, a significant participation gap exists — and it runs directly through the charter sector. While 76% of traditional schools and 72% of magnet schools offered dual or concurrent enrollment courses, far fewer charter schools (40%), alternative schools (20%), and special education schools (34%) offered such programs. That gap is an opportunity, not a verdict.

Why Flexible Charter Models Are Structurally Built for This

The very features that define flex, hybrid, and independent-study charter schools make them natural launching pads for dual enrollment — if leaders are intentional about it.

Scheduling freedom. A student in a traditional school must fit a college course into a rigid bell schedule that's designed for something else. A student in an independent-study or asynchronous model already owns their daily schedule. Adding a Tuesday/Thursday community college course — in person or online — creates zero structural conflict.

Parent partnership. Dual enrollment works best when families are active co-navigators: understanding transfer credit policies, selecting courses strategically, managing dual registration. Independent-study charters already have parent-involvement baked into the model. That infrastructure is an asset.

Personalization at scale. A student passionate about engineering can pair community college calculus with high school physics. A student interested in healthcare can take certified nursing assistant coursework for credit. In a one-size-fits-all school, this requires bureaucratic exceptions. In a flex charter, it's just good advising.

College and career counseling. The best charter schools aren't just offering dual enrollment as a checkbox — they're building guidance systems that help students choose courses aligned to genuine post-secondary goals.

Excel Academy Charter School, a tuition-free TK–12 independent-study and virtual charter school serving six counties in California, illustrates how these elements can come together. The school offers 35 AP and Honors courses, a dedicated concurrent enrollment program under its high school guidance framework, and a 98% graduation rate — all within a model that gives families the flexibility to choose how their student learns. That combination of academic rigor, structural flexibility, and college-preparatory programming is exactly the profile that positions a charter school to make dual enrollment a genuine differentiator.

What the 2025 Legislative Wave Means for Charter Leaders

The new laws enacted in 2025 aren't abstract — several have direct operational implications for charter school administrators:

  • Credit transfer protections were the single most active category, with 33 laws enacted focused on program integrity and ensuring that credits students earn actually count toward college degrees. For charter families, this is huge: it addresses the most common anxiety about dual enrollment ("will these credits transfer?").
  • Finance reforms (29 laws enacted) focused on building funding structures that sustain program growth and eliminate tuition and fee costs for students. Charter leaders should audit whether their state's latest legislation changes what — if anything — their students pay.
  • Equity and access provisions (11 laws enacted) focused on broadening access to the students who need it most and expanding public reporting on participation data. This opens a door for charter schools specifically: if your school serves a high proportion of lower-income families or students of color, proactively enrolling them in dual credit courses is both a mission win and an accountability story you can tell.

Three Practical Starting Points for Charter Leaders

If your school isn't yet maximizing dual enrollment, here's where to begin:

  1. Map your college partners. Which community colleges or universities operate in your service area and have CCAP agreements (in California) or equivalent state frameworks? Start with institutions that offer online sections — that's the access point for students in distributed models.
  2. Designate a dual enrollment navigator. This doesn't require a new hire. A counselor or Teacher of Record who owns the dual enrollment process — knows the registration windows, the transfer credit rules, the advising conversations — makes participation rates climb.
  3. Tell the story to families at enrollment. Many families choosing a flex or independent-study charter don't realize that "flexibility" includes access to college-level coursework. Make dual enrollment part of your enrollment pitch, your orientation, and your annual communications.

The Bottom Line

Can charter school students earn college credit while in high school? Absolutely — and in many cases, the flexible schedule and family-partnership model of independent-study and hybrid charter schools makes it easier for students to participate in dual enrollment than it would be in a traditional setting. The barrier isn't structural. It's awareness, advising, and intentional programming.

With 55 new dual enrollment laws passed in a single year and every state now operating under a statewide policy framework, the infrastructure has never been more favorable. The question for charter leaders in 2025 isn't whether dual enrollment is accessible. It's whether your school is making it visible.
