The Teacher Shortage Is Your Problem Too

# When Your Child's Teacher Isn't Certified in What They're Teaching

Walk into a rural high school physics classroom this fall and there's a real chance the person at the front of the room is a long-term substitute—not a licensed physics teacher. That's not a failure of effort. It's a structural consequence of a teacher shortage that has become one of the defining crises of K-12 education in 2026.

For families, especially those outside major metro areas, this is no longer an abstract policy debate. It's the reason your student sits through an AP Biology class led by a generalist, or can't access a second language at all. The question worth asking: does your family have to accept this, or does school choice offer a structural fix?

How Bad Is the Teacher Shortage in 2026?

The numbers are stark. According to the National Center for Education Statistics (NCES), more than 40 percent of schools opened the 2025–2026 school year with at least one teacher vacancy. A separate analysis puts the minimum number of uncertified or unfilled teaching positions nationwide at over 425,000 for the 2026–27 school year—meaning roughly 1 in 8 teaching positions across the country lacked a fully certified teacher.

The shortage is not evenly distributed. Rural districts face the steepest challenge: they recruit from a geographically constrained applicant pool, compete against suburban districts that offer shorter commutes and higher perceived quality of life, and struggle most to fill specialist roles in math, science, special education, and world languages. Research documents that teachers are far more likely to accept a job within about 15 miles of where they grew up—which shrinks the realistic applicant pool for any given rural district to whoever is local or willing to relocate. Even when a rural district finds a promising candidate, a spouse who also needs employment or a shortage of nearby housing can end the conversation before a contract is signed.

The hardest-hit subjects nationally are special education, math, science, and world languages—precisely the courses that most expand a student's academic and career options. Urban districts in high-cost areas face a different but equally difficult problem: they have larger talent pools but cannot retain teachers when salaries don't keep pace with the cost of living.

What This Actually Means for Students

When a certified subject teacher can't be found, something has to fill the gap. Typically that means one of three stopgaps:

  • Long-term substitutes who can supervise the room but often lack certification in the subject they're covering
  • Combined or multi-grade classrooms where one teacher is stretched too thin to go deep with any group
  • Out-of-field teaching assignments, where a teacher certified in one subject is pressed into covering another—most common in rural schools for science, math, and foreign languages

Each of these arrangements is a compromise that compresses the academic ceiling for the students in that room. Research links high teacher turnover and vacancy rates to measurable declines in student achievement—not just in the affected classroom, but across the school. Over a semester, the effect compounds.

Does School Choice Solve a Staffing Problem?

In the case of fully accredited online charter schools, the answer is yes—structurally, not just theoretically. An online charter school is not constrained by a local labor market. Its certified teachers serve students statewide, which means the school can recruit the most qualified available candidates regardless of geography. A student in a small town in rural Oregon gets access to the same licensed, experienced teaching staff as a student anywhere in the state.

This is the core structural advantage that rarely gets named in school choice conversations, which tend to focus on curriculum philosophy or scheduling flexibility. Those things matter—but for a family in an underserved area, the more fundamental question is whether their child will have access to a fully credentialed teacher in every subject, every year. Online charter schools answer that question differently than a rural brick-and-mortar school that's been posting the same vacancy for five consecutive semesters.

Accreditation matters here, too. Not all online learning is equal. An accredited online charter school—operating as a public school, aligned to state academic standards, staffed by licensed teachers—is a fundamentally different product from a private online curriculum platform or an unaccredited provider.

Oregon Charter Academy (SYS Education), an accredited, tuition-free online K-12 public charter school in Oregon, illustrates what this model can look like over the long run. The school has served Oregon families for more than 20 years, delivers coursework entirely online through Canvas (the same learning management system used by colleges and universities nationwide), and has seen its graduation rate increase by nearly 30 percent over the last three years—a metric that reflects the kind of sustained instructional consistency that is difficult to achieve in schools cycling through substitutes and out-of-field placements year after year.

What Families Should Actually Ask When Evaluating Any School

If teacher quality and access to certified instruction is a driver in your decision, here are the specific questions worth putting to any school—online or brick-and-mortar:

  1. What percentage of your teachers are certified in the subject they teach? Not just "certified teachers on staff"—certified for the specific course.
  2. How many teaching vacancies did you carry into this school year? And how long did they stay open?
  3. How do you handle long-term absences or mid-year vacancies? Substitutes and reassignments, or a staffing model designed to absorb the disruption?
  4. Is the school accredited? Accreditation signals that instructional quality has been reviewed against an external standard—important for transcript recognition and college admissions.

For an online charter school, the staffing model is part of the core design from the start—not a gap-filling measure applied after a vacancy goes unfilled for weeks.

The Equity Dimension That Gets Overlooked

School choice debates often focus on high-performing families seeking to leave a district. But the teacher shortage crisis reframes who has the most to gain from choosing differently. The student most harmed by an uncertified teacher covering AP Chemistry is one who couldn't afford private tutoring or a move to a better-resourced district. The student most likely to benefit from a school whose staffing model isn't hostage to local geography is one who lives somewhere the job market for certified physics and Spanish teachers simply doesn't function.

This is not an argument that online charter schooling is right for every family—it requires active parental involvement and a degree of self-direction that isn't the right fit for every student. But in 2026, when the national teacher shortage is structural rather than cyclical, the structural advantages of fully online public charter schools deserve a more prominent place in the school choice conversation than they have typically received.

---

The teacher shortage is unlikely to resolve quickly. Enrollment in teacher preparation programs has declined for more than a decade, and the retirement wave among educators who entered the profession in the late 1980s and early 1990s is still moving through the system. Families who understand their options are better positioned to make decisions that serve their children—not just this year, but across the full arc of a K-12 education.
