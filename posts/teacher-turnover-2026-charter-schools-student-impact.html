Teacher Turnover in 2026: What It Costs Students

# Teacher Turnover in 2026: What It Really Costs Students — and What Charter School Families Should Ask

When a classroom teacher leaves mid-year, or a school opens September with unfilled positions, it rarely makes the news. But the cumulative effect of teacher turnover is one of the most consequential — and underreported — forces shaping K-12 education quality right now. For families comparing schools, it deserves far more weight than a glossy brochure or a school's average test score.

Here's what the research shows in 2026, why charter school families in particular need to pay attention, and the specific questions worth asking before you enroll.

---

How Bad Is the Teacher Shortage in 2026?

The numbers are stark. The Learning Policy Institute's latest national scan found at least 425,412 teaching positions that were either unfilled or filled by teachers not fully certified for the assignment — roughly 1 in 8 teaching positions nationally. 2026 marked the third consecutive year of increases in that combined shortage estimate.

More than 40 percent of schools started the 2026-27 school year with at least one teacher vacancy. The hardest-hit subjects are predictable but still troubling: math, science, and special education continue to see the worst staffing gaps, with credentialed candidates declining year over year.

The teacher pipeline problem is structural, not cyclical. Retirement rates continue to rise as the large cohort of educators who entered the profession in the late 1980s and early 1990s reach the end of their careers. Burnout remains a major driver of attrition — teachers are navigating heavier workloads, more complex student needs, and increased administrative demands. Pay compounds the problem: according to RAND, teachers earned an average base salary of $75,599 in 2026, while similar working adults with comparable education earned roughly $105,000. That $30,000 gap has real consequences for who stays in the classroom and who leaves for other fields.

---

Why Turnover Directly Harms Children — The Research Is Clear

This isn't just an HR problem. For students, teacher turnover disrupts instructional continuity, weakens teacher-student relationships, and negatively influences academic achievement — particularly in schools serving disadvantaged populations.

A 2026 study published in the Journal of Research on Educational Effectiveness found that general education teacher turnover negatively impacts test scores for both students with and without disabilities, with an outsized impact of special education teacher turnover on students with disabilities specifically. The harm isn't limited to test scores: when a trusted adult leaves a classroom, the relational scaffolding that supports learning — routines, inside jokes, hard-won trust — leaves with them.

Turnover is also expensive in ways that ripple back to classrooms. Replacing a departing teacher costs a district between $12,000 and $25,000 per position, according to the Learning Policy Institute — money that could otherwise fund classroom resources, tutoring, or enrichment programs.

The burden falls unevenly. Schools with the highest student poverty lost 29% of their teachers in a recent study year, while schools with the lowest poverty rates lost only 19%. Students who already face the greatest academic challenges are the most likely to experience a revolving door of adults at the front of the room.

---

Charter Schools Face a Specific Retention Challenge

Charter schools have long offered families autonomy, mission-driven cultures, and innovative instructional models. But the data shows they also carry a higher baseline turnover risk. Research has documented turnover rates of 17.8% in charter schools compared to 14.9% in traditional public schools.

That gap isn't inevitable — it's a function of working conditions, compensation structures, and leadership quality. Some charters have built remarkably stable faculties by investing heavily in teacher support; others churn through staff year after year. This is precisely why the specific school, not just the sector, matters when families are evaluating options.

A school like eStem Charter School Elementary in Little Rock, Arkansas — a tuition-free, open-enrollment school built on the pillars of Economics, Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics, with a stated mission of preparing students to be college, career, and world ready — illustrates the kind of mission clarity that research consistently links to stronger teacher retention. When teachers believe deeply in a school's purpose and feel genuinely supported in executing it, they tend to stay. Culture eats compensation data for breakfast.

---

What Does a High-Retention School Actually Do Differently?

What are the most effective teacher retention strategies in 2026? Research points consistently to five levers that work — and notably, most of them don't require a bigger budget:

  1. Structured mentorship and induction. Pairing new teachers with experienced mentors for at least one full year improves both effectiveness and retention. New teachers leave at the highest rates: turnover reaches 26–30% among teachers with seven or fewer years of experience, compared to 17–20% for veterans. The first three years are the highest-stakes window.
  1. Genuine teacher voice. Involving teachers in decisions about curriculum, scheduling, and school policy builds ownership and reduces turnover. Teachers who feel heard are teachers who stay.
  1. Protected planning time and manageable workloads. Schools that safeguard instructional planning time and limit non-teaching administrative burdens see steadier staffing year over year. Burnout accelerates when planning time is routinely cannibalized by meetings and paperwork.
  1. Professional growth pathways. Teacher-leadership roles — mentoring peers, leading professional development, coaching colleagues — give high performers a reason to stay without leaving the classroom.
  1. Active principal support. TNTP research found that retention jumped from 62% to 73% when a leader simply encouraged a teacher to stay — the single largest gain of any strategy tested. The principal's visible investment in teachers' success matters more than almost any structural policy.

Schools that layer two or three of these strategies together see steadier staffing than those relying on pay raises alone. The strongest results combine structural support — mentorship, protected planning time — with genuine teacher voice in school decisions.

---

What Charter School Families Should Ask Before Enrolling

You can learn a great deal about a school's teacher stability before you ever sign an enrollment form. High teacher turnover is one of the strongest indicators of deeper problems at a school. Schools where teachers stay for many years tend to build stronger classroom communities and deliver more consistent instruction.

Here are specific questions worth asking on a school tour or at an information night:

  • "How long have most teachers been at this school?" A school that can honestly say most of its teachers have been there five or more years is communicating something important about its culture and leadership.
  • "What percentage of teachers returned this fall from last year?" Year-over-year retention rate is a more precise metric than vague assurances about stability.
  • "How does the school fill a vacancy when a teacher leaves?" This reveals hiring standards — and whether students routinely face extended gaps covered by substitutes or uncertified staff.
  • "Are there unfilled teaching positions right now?" An honest, direct answer to this question speaks volumes about transparency.
  • "What does professional development look like for teachers here?" Schools that invest in teachers' ongoing growth signal that they treat staff as long-term partners rather than interchangeable labor.

Experts at the Center on Reinventing Public Education note that information on principal and teacher retention helps gauge a school's long-term strength, and that high turnover can signal dissatisfaction with leadership or instability. Beyond the tour, request a school's annual report or accountability report card — many states now require schools to publish teacher retention and certification data publicly, and that document will tell you more than any marketing material.

---

The Bottom Line for Families

School choice is about more than a program or philosophy on paper. The adults in the building — their experience, their commitment, and their longevity — are among the strongest predictors of your child's day-to-day experience. High teacher attrition disrupts instructional continuity and erodes the collegial trust and shared routines that make schools function well for kids.

In 2026, with roughly 1 in 8 teaching positions nationally either vacant or filled by an under-certified educator, teacher stability has moved from a "nice to have" to a core quality metric. Ask the question directly. Schools with strong cultures and thoughtful leadership won't hesitate to answer — and that confidence itself tells you something worth knowing before you choose.
