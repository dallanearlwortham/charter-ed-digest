Strong Boards Keep Charter Schools Open

# Strong Boards Keep Charter Schools Open — What the Closure Data Tells Us

The number is bracing: more than one in four public charter schools closes within five years of opening. By year 20, the average failure rate climbs to 55%. A comprehensive longitudinal study drawing on more than 2 million U.S. Department of Education records — released by the National Center for Charter School Accountability and the Network for Public Education — put hard data behind what many in the sector have long suspected.

Those aren't just statistics. Each closure displaces students, disrupts families, and hands critics a ready-made argument against the entire charter model. The hard truth is that most of those closures aren't caused by an unfair political environment or hostile state budgets. They trace back to something closer to home: the governing board.

Why Governance Is the Root Cause — Not a Side Issue

Nearly half of charter schools that closed between the 2022–23 and 2023–24 school years did so due to low enrollment, with fraud or mismanagement ranking as the second most common reason. But low enrollment rarely appears out of nowhere. It tends to follow weak academic leadership, poor community engagement, and financial instability — all of which land squarely in the board's lane.

Charter school boards play an essential role: they provide the strategic vision for the school, hire the leaders who run it, hold those leaders accountable for academic results, and provide financial oversight. When a board fills seats with loyalists rather than skilled leaders — or treats monthly meetings as rubber-stamp formalities — the school is already in trouble, even if no one has noticed yet.

Financial mismanagement is one of the leading causes of charter school failure, making sound financial oversight a critically important board function. Yet this is precisely the area where many boards fall short: no dedicated finance committee, no independent audit process, no board members with genuine financial literacy who can push back when the numbers don't add up.

What the Closure Breakdown Actually Looks Like

Recent national data breaks down charter closures by cause:

  • Low enrollment: 46.8% of closures
  • Fraud or mismanagement: 21.6%
  • Academic failures: 13.7%
  • Financial reasons: 10.1%
  • Other causes: 7.9%

For every child caught in a closure, it means being uprooted from familiar classrooms, teachers, and routines — a disruption that ripples through families long after new enrollment paperwork is filed somewhere else. In states where weak authorizer oversight enables "authorizer shopping," failing schools can avoid closure simply by switching sponsors, compounding governance weaknesses rather than correcting them.

What Effective Charter Boards Actually Do

So what separates the boards that build lasting schools from the ones that preside over closures? Research and practitioner consensus point to several consistent practices.

1. Recruit for skill gaps, not relationships. A board seat shouldn't be a reward for being a founder's friend or an early supporter. High-functioning boards conduct deliberate skills-gap analyses before recruiting new members. They look for people who bring legal expertise, financial acumen, community credibility, and instructional insight — not just enthusiasm for the mission.

2. Separate governance from management. One of the most common governance breakdowns is a board that either micromanages school operations or, at the opposite extreme, rubber-stamps whatever the executive director proposes. The board's job is to hire and hold accountable the school leader — not to run the school day-to-day. Getting this boundary right is the foundation of everything else.

3. Build a real finance committee. A dedicated Finance Committee works alongside the CEO and CFO to develop the upcoming fiscal year budget, present budget recommendations to the full board, monitor implementation regularly, recommend revisions as needed, and oversee policies for managing the organization's assets. Critically, the full board also needs enough baseline financial literacy to meaningfully evaluate what the committee brings forward — not just nod along and sign off.

4. Conduct annual board self-evaluations. Self-evaluating the board's effectiveness — both as individuals and as a collective — at least once per year is a key driver of performance oversight. Most underperforming boards have never formally asked themselves whether they are actually doing their jobs well. The ones that have tend to catch problems early.

5. Commit to ongoing training. State charter associations and national organizations offer governance training specifically designed for the charter context. The National Charter Schools Institute, for instance, runs a "Governing for Greatness" board development program built to help boards reflect on their impact, strengthen governance practices, and recommit to the students and communities they serve. These sessions go well beyond parliamentary procedure — they address the leadership dynamics that determine whether a school thrives or stagnates.

How State Associations Reinforce Board Quality

Individual school boards don't have to figure any of this out in isolation. State-level charter associations exist specifically to provide the training, legal guidance, peer networks, and professional development that help boards govern well — and that many small charter schools could never afford to build independently.

The North Carolina Assocation for Public Charter Schools is a clear example of this model in action. Its core mission of support, education, and advocacy extends directly to the boards and leaders of member schools, connecting them to resources, a legislative agenda, and a professional community that individual governing boards couldn't easily replicate on their own. The association also maintains a career job board, which means member school boards can recruit qualified administrators more efficiently — closing the leadership pipeline gap that so often precedes academic and financial decline.

For boards operating in states with active associations, the question isn't whether to lean on those resources. It's how quickly they can integrate them into the governance calendar before a fixable problem becomes a fatal one.

What Does a Strong Charter Board Look Like in Practice?

A strong charter school board consistently does the following:

  • Meets on a regular, predictable schedule with a structured agenda — not just when there's a crisis
  • Reviews academic performance and financial dashboards at every meeting, not just at annual reviews
  • Maintains a clear, documented succession plan for the executive director
  • Staggers board member terms to preserve institutional knowledge through leadership transitions
  • Engages families and the surrounding community as genuine stakeholders, not afterthoughts
  • Understands the school's authorizer relationship and keeps the renewal timeline front of mind throughout the year

None of these are exotic or expensive. Most are standard nonprofit governance practices adapted to a school context. The gap between boards that thrive and boards that stumble is almost always execution — and the willingness to prioritize governance work even when the school appears to be running smoothly.

The Stakes Are High Enough to Get This Right

Charter school enrollment rose 9% between the 2019–20 and the 2022–23 school years, while traditional public school enrollment dropped by 3.5% over the same period — a clear signal of genuine and growing family demand for charter options. That demand is an enormous asset, but only if the schools families choose are governed well enough to stay open, stay solvent, and keep delivering results.

A charter school that closes doesn't just fail its current students. It undermines the broader argument that school choice, done right, delivers stable and high-quality options for families who need them most. Strong boards are the mechanism by which the promise of the charter model becomes something families can count on — not just for one enrollment cycle, but for a generation.

The closure data isn't a reason for pessimism about the charter sector. It's a roadmap for exactly what boards need to prioritize, starting now, before the warning signs appear on someone else's dashboard.
