The K–2 Window: Charter Schools' Highest-Leverage Bet

# The K–2 Window: Why Charter Schools' Earliest Grades Are Their Highest-Leverage Equity Investment

The debate about charter school quality tends to center on high school graduation rates, college enrollment numbers, and AP pass rates. Those outcomes matter — but the research keeps pointing further upstream, toward a quieter battleground where the real work happens: kindergarten, first grade, and second grade. What a child learns — and how they learn to learn — in those first three years of formal schooling shapes nearly everything that follows. For charter school leaders, families, and authorizers, the K–2 window is not just an entry point. It is the highest-leverage equity investment a school can make.

And in 2026, that window is under new pressure.

A Nationwide Warning Sign: K–2 Reading Progress Has Stalled

This past summer, Amplify released national early literacy data that should be required reading for every elementary charter school leader. Using mCLASS DIBELS 8th Edition benchmark results from approximately 350,000 students across 1,800 schools in 43 states, the report found that early reading scores for the 2025–26 school year were essentially unchanged from the prior year — the first time year-over-year progress had flatlined since the 2020–21 pandemic school year. Nationally, 34% of K–2 students still are not on track to learn to read.

More troubling: while the share of students on track is up 7% from the post-pandemic low of 2021–22, progress has plateaued over the past three years — and performance still hasn't recovered to pre-pandemic levels. The early-grades literacy recovery that many assumed was on autopilot has stalled.

For charter schools serving predominantly low-income, Black, and Brown students — communities that were already reading below national averages before 2020 — a stalled national average means the equity gap is not closing on its own. It requires intentional design.

Why K–2 Is a Developmental Inflection Point

The case for treating K–2 as a strategic priority starts with what developmental science tells us about this window. A landmark meta-analysis published in Developmental Psychology — examining school-entry data from multiple longitudinal studies — found that early math skills have the greatest predictive power for later academic achievement, followed by reading, then attention skills. As the Southern Regional Education Board summarizes: early math skills are more predictive of later achievement than early literacy skills or social development.

That finding often surprises leaders who have built literacy-first frameworks — but the point is the same for both domains: the foundational concepts children internalize before third grade are not merely precursors to later skills. They are the architecture everything else is built on.

Miss the window and the costs compound. A national longitudinal study of nearly 4,000 students, cited repeatedly by the Annie E. Casey Foundation and education researchers, found that students who are not reading proficiently by the end of third grade are four times more likely to leave high school without a diploma than proficient readers. For the lowest-performing readers, the dropout risk climbs to nearly six times higher. And for students living in poverty who also read below proficiency — a combination that describes many charter school communities — the graduation odds are bleaker still.

The remediation cost grows steeply with each year the gap goes unaddressed. Charter schools that invest most deliberately in K–2 are not being conservative. They are being strategic.

What High-Performing Charter Schools Get Right in the Early Grades

Evidence from the National Charter School Study III shows that elementary-configured charter schools produce statistically positive growth in both reading and math. That isn't a coincidence. It reflects what intentional early-grades design can accomplish. The research points to a cluster of design choices that, taken together, consistently move outcomes:

  • Low student-to-adult ratios in K–2, where sustained individual attention is the core instructional resource — not a luxury
  • Co-teaching or two-educator classrooms, so instruction can be differentiated in real time; a decade of Massachusetts test-score data found that co-taught classrooms improve math scores for students both with and without disabilities in the elementary grades
  • A structured literacy spine grounded in the Science of Reading — phonological awareness, phonics, fluency, vocabulary, and comprehension taught explicitly and sequentially
  • Early numeracy sequences that build number sense and mathematical reasoning, not just counting and shape identification
  • Social-emotional learning embedded as academic infrastructure, not added on — because self-regulation is a prerequisite for all other learning in K–2
  • Family engagement as an instructional variable, not a PR function — research across multiple meta-analyses consistently links sustained family engagement in children's learning to higher grades, stronger motivation, and greater social competence

That last point deserves its own emphasis. In the early grades, when the home is still the dominant learning environment outside school hours, what families do with and for their children is not separable from what happens in the classroom.

The Cultural Dimension: Belonging Is a Prerequisite for Learning

There is a second layer to K–2 design that research is increasingly clear on: young children learn better — with more engagement, persistence, and identity security — when the classroom reflects who they are. Culturally affirming pedagogy in the earliest grades is not decorative. It shapes whether a five-year-old decides this place is for them.

Research on Black students and school belonging finds that culturally affirming classroom environments are directly linked to stronger engagement and a greater sense of academic identity. Ubuntu philosophy — rooted in the African principle of "I am because we are" — has gained significant attention in education research as a framework emphasizing interconnectedness, collective care, and shared responsibility. Studies applying Ubuntu-informed pedagogy find that it promotes social justice, responsive education, and students being treated with dignity and respect. Those aren't soft outcomes: they are the conditions under which young children actually learn.

A classroom rooted in Ubuntu is not just more culturally affirming; it is more developmentally aligned. Children in K–2 are relational learners. They learn through belonging, through seeing themselves in what they read, and through educators who understand their culture and experience.

One School Designing the Early-Grades Model With Intention

BRICK Rochester is a tuition-free public charter school in Rochester, NY, currently serving kindergarten and first grade — and growing one grade at a time alongside its students. The school places two teachers in every classroom, offers free afterschool care and enrichment, and roots its curriculum explicitly in Ubuntu principles, celebrating the history and achievements of Black and Brown leaders as a core part of the academic experience. Wraparound services, family engagement, and integrated community partnerships are named structural pillars — not programs bolted onto the side — and the BRICK Wealth Fund deposits $350 into a wealth-building account for every enrolled family, signaling from day one of kindergarten that this school is invested in long-term family mobility, not just annual test scores. It is a model that takes the research on early-grades design seriously enough to build it into the school's DNA.

What Charter Leaders Should Ask About Their K–2 Program Right Now

If you lead or authorize a charter school that includes early grades, these questions are worth pressure-testing this fall:

Are your K–2 classrooms actually differentiated — or just managed? Two adults in a room creates the possibility of differentiated instruction. The question is whether your instructional model uses that capacity in structured, deliberate ways, or whether the second adult functions primarily as a behavior support.

Has your K–2 ELA curriculum been audited against the Science of Reading? More than 40 states have passed or strengthened structured literacy mandates since 2020. Charter schools that haven't aligned their early-grades ELA scope and sequence to the phonics-first evidence base are increasingly out of step with both policy and research.

Are families engaged as learning partners — or as recipients? The most effective early-grades family engagement is bidirectional: families share what they know about their child, and schools equip families with instructional strategies they can use at home. Passive engagement — newsletters and back-to-school nights — has negligible impact on early literacy and math outcomes.

Does your school's culture send kindergartners the message that they belong? Curriculum audits, staff representation, and classroom materials all send signals. By the time a child is six, they have already begun forming beliefs about whether "school people" look like them, know their community, and value their history.

The Compounding Returns of Getting K–2 Right

What is the long-term payoff of investing heavily in K–2 at a charter school?

The payoff compounds in every direction. A child who enters third grade reading fluently, with strong number sense, a secure sense of academic identity, and a family actively engaged in their learning, doesn't simply post better third-grade test scores. They have more available working memory for new content, more resilience when coursework gets harder, and a learner identity that carries them through middle school's social pressures and high school's academic demands. Early academic intervention — as Connecticut charter school data and others have shown — significantly improves long-term student outcomes precisely because it allows educators to identify learning gaps and build strong academic habits before students enter the grades where falling behind becomes self-reinforcing.

The schools that will produce the most durable equity outcomes over the next decade are not necessarily the ones with the flashiest high school programming. They are the ones doing patient, precise, culturally grounded work with five- and six-year-olds — and getting it right when it matters most.
