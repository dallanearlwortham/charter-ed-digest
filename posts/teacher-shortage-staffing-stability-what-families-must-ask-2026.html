Is Your Child's School Losing Teachers? What to Ask

# Is Your Child's School Losing Teachers? What Families Must Ask in 2026

Most families evaluate schools on test scores, course catalogs, and extracurricular options. Very few ask the question that may matter most: How many teachers are still here from last year?

In 2026, that question has never been more important — and most schools are hoping you never think to ask it.

The Scope of the Problem: 1 in 8 Teaching Positions Is Compromised

The teacher shortage in the United States is no longer a headline from the pandemic years. According to the Learning Policy Institute's 2026 scan, an estimated 387,843 teachers across all 50 states plus the District of Columbia are not fully certified for their teaching assignments, and at least 37,569 positions remain entirely unfilled. Combined, that means roughly 425,412 teaching positions — about 1 in 8 nationally — are either vacant or held by someone not credentialed to teach that subject or grade level.

The National Center for Education Statistics adds a ground-level view: more than 40 percent of schools opened the 2026-27 school year with at least one teacher vacancy. That's not a staffing hiccup. That's a structural feature of the current landscape.

What has changed is where the crisis now lives. For years, the shortage was framed as a hiring problem — too few people entering teaching pipelines. In 2026, the deeper wound is retention. Teachers are being hired and leaving 18 months later. Schools are caught in a churn that most families never see, because enrollment materials don't mention it.

Why Turnover Directly Damages Your Child's Education

This isn't an administrative inconvenience for school leaders. Research from scholars at Stanford University, the University of Michigan, and the University of Virginia documents a consistent finding: teacher turnover harms student achievement broadly — not just for students who lose a specific teacher, but across entire school communities. When teachers leave, institutional knowledge disappears, staff cohesion degrades, and the shared sense of purpose that drives a school's culture erodes. Students in high-turnover schools spend meaningful instructional time with substitutes, uncertified placeholders, or teachers managing courses outside their credential area.

The damage compounds at high-poverty schools. Analysis from Education Resource Strategies found that schools serving the highest-poverty populations also see the highest teacher turnover — often approaching 20 percent annually — making consistent, relationship-based instruction an exception rather than a norm for the students who need it most.

What Does Good Staffing Stability Actually Look Like?

"What is your year-over-year teacher retention rate?" That single question separates schools that are genuinely stable from schools running on a rotating cast of first-year educators. A healthy retention rate — one that suggests a faculty that knows its students, its curriculum, and each other — generally runs 85 percent or higher. Schools with retention below 70 percent are actively reconstructing instruction every September, whether or not they describe it that way at open house night.

Beyond that anchor question, families evaluating a school's staffing health should also ask:

  • What percentage of teachers hold full certification in the subjects and grade levels they teach? Emergency and provisional credentials are legal stopgaps, not quality indicators.
  • How long has your current principal been in this role? Leadership stability and teacher stability are deeply correlated. Schools that cycle through principals almost always cycle through teachers too.
  • How are mid-year vacancies handled? The answer reveals whether the school has a real continuity plan or defaults to shuffled schedules and long-term substitutes.
  • What does this school offer teachers that the district next door doesn't? Schools with a compelling, specific answer to that question — professional autonomy, mission alignment, flexible scheduling, stronger compensation — tend to hold onto people.

The Charter School Advantage — and the Charter School Risk

Charter schools occupy a complicated position in this conversation. On one hand, research shows that charter schools in states with robust charter sectors have used salary flexibility and cultural differentiation to attract experienced teachers who have left traditional public school districts — educators who cite greater professional autonomy and mission clarity as their reasons for making the move.

On the other hand, charter school teachers have historically turned over at higher rates than their traditional public school counterparts, in part because many charter schools operate without tenure protections or the step-based salary schedules that reward longevity. A charter school's staffing culture is highly school-specific. That's exactly why asking direct questions matters more in the charter sector than anywhere else — the variance between a well-run charter and a struggling one is far greater than it is between comparable traditional public schools.

For families in Arkansas, where rural teacher pipelines are under particular strain — the Arkansas Teacher Corps recently received a $5.1 million Walton Family Foundation grant to fund 114 new fellowships targeting teacher shortages in rural and high-poverty districts, because the gap is severe enough to require that level of intervention — the question of where to find consistent, fully credentialed instruction is a practical one, not an abstract one.

Online charter schools that serve students statewide offer one structural answer to geographic staffing constraints: rather than limiting hiring to candidates within commuting distance of a single campus, they can recruit fully certified teachers from a broader pool and maintain instructional continuity without the disruption that a mid-year vacancy creates in a traditional classroom. Lisa Academy (Arkansas Online School), which serves K–12 students across Arkansas with both live and flex learning options, is built on exactly this model — connecting students statewide to teachers and support resources regardless of which part of Arkansas the family calls home.

Five Questions to Bring to Every School Visit

Regardless of school type — traditional public, magnet, brick-and-mortar charter, or online — these questions cut through marketing language and get to what actually matters for your child's day-to-day experience:

  1. What is your teacher retention rate from the 2025-26 school year to 2026-27?
  2. What percentage of teachers hold full certification for the subjects they teach?
  3. How long has the current principal been at this school?
  4. When a teacher leaves mid-year, what is your protocol for maintaining instructional continuity?
  5. What does this school do specifically to retain its best teachers — and what has worked?

Schools that answer these questions readily and specifically deserve your continued attention. Schools that deflect, generalize, or redirect the conversation back to test scores and rankings are, in their own way, giving you an answer too.

Staffing Stability Is a Student Outcome Issue

The teacher shortage tends to get framed as a policy problem — something legislators and district administrators need to solve. That framing is accurate, but it can leave families feeling like passive observers in a system-level crisis.

The more useful frame: staffing stability is a student outcome issue that families have every right to investigate before enrolling. The research connecting teacher turnover to measurable learning losses is clear enough that this should be a standard part of every school evaluation — treated with the same seriousness as graduation rates, accreditation status, or course offerings.

Schools that have built cultures strong enough to retain experienced educators in the current environment are doing something right. Before you commit, find out what it is.
