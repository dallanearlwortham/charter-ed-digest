What to Look for in a Virtual Charter School

# Beyond Flexibility: What Families Should Actually Look for in a Virtual Charter School

Virtual charter school enrollment is climbing fast—and the reasons families are making the switch go much deeper than schedule convenience. A 2024 Parent Pulse Survey of 2,621 parents of virtual school students found that safety concerns rated an average of 4.5 out of 5 as a motivation for choosing virtual schooling, with 87% of parents citing bullying concerns specifically. In Michigan, a parallel survey found that the top reasons for choosing online charter schools included previous experiences with bullying (57%), flexible scheduling (54%), and the quality of the curriculum (31%).

These are not families dabbling in a trend. They are making deliberate, often urgent decisions about their children's education. The question for both families evaluating options and charter leaders building virtual programs is the same: what actually makes a virtual charter school worth choosing?

The Enrollment Numbers Tell Only Part of the Story

The scale of this shift is hard to overstate. The National Alliance for Public Charter Schools reported that for the 2023–2024 school year, charter schools added more than 80,000 new students—making the sector the only segment of the public school ecosystem experiencing consistent growth, while district public schools have collectively lost nearly 1.8 million students since 2019–20. Virtual charters are driving a significant portion of that momentum.

In Pennsylvania alone, nearly 60,000 students were enrolled full time in cyber charters in 2023–24—more than any other state, including California, Texas, and Florida. In Philadelphia, virtual cyber charter enrollment jumped 55% compared to the 2020–21 school year. Nationally, the virtual charter market is projected to more than double over the next decade.

Growth at that rate attracts both excellent schools and opportunistic ones. Families who enroll based on marketing language rather than program substance often end up disappointed.

The Quality Problem Enrollment Can't Solve

The National Association of Charter School Authorizers (NACSA) has documented it plainly: well-documented academic and management problems continue to affect virtual charter schools sector-wide. NACSA recommends that states hold virtual charters accountable not just on test scores but on student enrollment, attendance, engagement, attrition, and financial operations—and that authorizers be willing to close chronically low-performing schools regardless of how many families they serve.

For families doing their own due diligence without waiting on state policy to catch up, the accountability burden falls on them. Enrollment numbers and a tuition-free price tag are table stakes. The real differentiator is what a school is building toward.

What High-Quality Virtual Charter Programs Actually Offer

What should parents specifically look for when evaluating a virtual charter school? The honest answer is a school that treats curriculum delivery as one piece of a larger, deliberate design—not the whole picture. The research on character-focused education, drawn from Stanford's Center for Research on Education Outcomes and reinforced by enrollment trends, suggests that schools integrating values alongside academics are consistently outperforming those that don't.

Here are the pillars that distinguish serious virtual charter programs:

  • A rigorous, explicitly STEM-forward curriculum. Virtual schools that deploy blended pedagogies prepare students with 21st-century skills in digital literacy, self-direction, and technology-enabled collaboration. Look for schools where STEM is an organizing principle embedded across grade levels—not a single elective or a marketing checkbox.
  • Character and values education built into the program. Parents in the 2024 Parent Pulse Survey rated curriculum alignment with family values an average of 4.3 out of 5 when selecting a virtual school. Schools that intentionally develop virtues like integrity, resilience, and civic responsibility tend to produce stronger academic outcomes alongside stronger citizens.
  • Explicit college and career readiness. Students in online environments can develop strong time management, communication, and independent study skills—but only if the school structures that development deliberately. The best virtual programs build college-readiness into their model from the earliest grades, not as a senior-year afterthought.
  • Access to extracurriculars and community. One of the most common concerns about virtual schooling is social isolation. Schools that invest in student clubs, competitions, and activities give students a sense of belonging that sustains engagement over years, not just semesters.
  • Transparent outcome data. Graduation rates, college-acceptance rates, state assessment proficiency levels—these numbers should be accessible and current. A school that hedges on these questions is a school that isn't watching them closely enough.

A Model That Shows What's Possible

Oklahoma-based Dove Virtual School is a concrete example of a virtual program built around exactly this kind of intentional design. Operating as a tuition-free PK–12 online public school serving Oklahoma students, the school publicly frames its mission around three explicit pillars: STEM education, character education, and college and career readiness—stating its goal to prepare students "in a safe, healthy and caring environment" for academic excellence, continuous learning, and responsible citizenship. That kind of multi-pillar clarity—rather than a single selling point like flexibility or technology—is what distinguishes programs serious about long-term outcomes from those simply absorbing displaced enrollment.

The Bigger Picture for Charter Leaders

Researchers studying the virtual charter surge suggest the underlying driver is parental demand for education that is more flexible and better aligned with holistic development—not one or the other. Families are not simply fleeing bad schools. They are choosing toward something specific.

That means charter leaders competing in this space need a clear answer to a question that no enrollment brochure can dodge: What kind of person does your school produce?

Academic proficiency matters—no serious educator disputes that. But the parents fueling virtual charter growth are, as the survey data shows, making choices rooted in safety, values alignment, and long-term life preparation. Schools that can speak credibly to all three will earn durable enrollment. Schools that can only talk about platform features and curriculum credits will struggle to hold it.

A Quick Evaluation Checklist for Families

Before committing to any virtual charter school, families should be able to confirm clear answers to each of the following:

  • [ ] The school serves the full grade span your child will need (PK–12 provides the most continuity)
  • [ ] STEM programming is described specifically, not just mentioned
  • [ ] College and career readiness is built into the curriculum at every level, not just high school
  • [ ] Character or values education is an explicit, structured part of the program
  • [ ] The school publishes current, accessible outcome data (test scores, graduation and college-acceptance rates)
  • [ ] Students have access to activities and extracurriculars despite the online format
  • [ ] The school is fully accredited and operates as a tuition-free public charter

---

The virtual charter sector is growing fast enough that an underprepared school can attract students on momentum alone—for now. Families who ask hard questions and leaders who build schools that can answer them are the ones who will define what this model actually delivers over the next decade.
