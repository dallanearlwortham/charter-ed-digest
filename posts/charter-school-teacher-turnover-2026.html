Charter School Teacher Turnover: What the 2026 Data Says

Charter schools now record a teacher turnover rate of 17.8% — the highest of any school type in the U.S. A March 2026 Learning Policy Institute report put that number in stark relief, showing charter schools outpacing traditional public schools (14.9%) and every other school category. For charter leaders navigating tight budgets and high family expectations, that gap isn't a footnote. It's a structural problem with a real price tag.

The Scope of the Crisis

Nearly one in seven (15.1%) public school teachers moved schools or left the profession entirely in a single school year, and charter schools sit at the top of that ranking. Most striking: 74% of that turnover was voluntary and driven by reasons other than retirement — meaning most departures are, in principle, preventable.

The financial cost should focus any board's attention. Replacing a single teacher costs an estimated $25,000 in large districts and $12,000 in small districts (in 2024 dollars). For a charter that loses four or five teachers over the summer, that math compounds quickly — and those dollars come directly from classrooms.

The problem is also concentrated where it hurts most. Turnover rates were 35–37% higher in schools serving larger concentrations of students of color and those from low-income backgrounds than in schools with the smallest such concentrations. Since many charter schools deliberately serve high-need urban communities, the sector's 17.8% average likely understates the challenge for the schools doing the most important work.

Three Factors That Actually Drive Teachers Out

Yes, salaries matter. The LPI found that with every $1,000 increase in salary (adjusted for cost of living), the probability of turnover decreased by about 0.34 percentage points. But pay alone doesn't explain why one charter retains most of its staff year over year while a comparable school nearby watches half its team walk out.

Two other factors in the 2026 data are just as powerful:

  • School leadership. Turnover rates were cut nearly in half when comparing teachers who reported low versus high levels of leadership effectiveness — 18.7% versus 9.0%. That is the single most actionable lever in the research.
  • Job and workplace satisfaction. Teachers with high satisfaction were significantly less likely to leave than those with low satisfaction — 8.0% versus 22.0%.

Today's educators — particularly those from Gen Z — prioritize work-life balance, professional autonomy, and feeling genuinely valued. A charter that pitches mission on hiring day but runs on overwork and top-down decisions will lose teachers to schools, or other industries, that take those expectations seriously.

What High-Retention Charter Schools Do Differently

Build leadership teachers actually trust. The data are clear: effective, supportive school leadership cuts turnover from 18.7% to 9.0%. Principals who protect teacher prep time, involve staff in real decisions, and give honest feedback earn the loyalty that retention bonuses alone cannot buy.

Protect prep time. When a colleague is absent and no substitute appears, pulling teachers from planning periods creates compounding exhaustion that routinely precedes resignations. Smart charter operators maintain sub rosters, cross-train support staff, and treat coverage as a leadership responsibility — not a teacher problem.

Invest in early-career and high-need teachers. Turnover is especially high among uncertified teachers (20.1% vs. 14.7% for fully certified peers) and above average in high-need subjects: ESL and bilingual education (19.0%), foreign languages (18.3%), and special education (16.4%). Strong mentoring and induction during the first three years can close those gaps before they become summer vacancies.

Anchor teachers to community purpose. Schools where teachers feel genuinely connected to the community they serve — not just employed by an organization located there — consistently show lower voluntary attrition. BRICK Buffalo, a charter school that launched in 2023 serving East Buffalo neighborhoods in Buffalo, NY, builds this directly into its model: leadership is deliberately reflective of the community, and the curriculum is grounded in culturally responsive practice and student identity. When teachers see their own values embedded in daily school life rather than just the enrollment brochure, the pull toward the exit weakens considerably.

Conduct stay interviews, not just exit interviews. An exit interview tells you why someone left. A stay interview — a brief one-on-one asking what keeps a teacher engaged and what might push them to leave — gives you actionable intelligence before the resignation letter arrives.

The Equity Dimension

Black teachers leave schools at 18.1%, compared to 14.7% for White teachers — a gap that reflects, in part, the concentration of teachers of color in schools with fewer resources and more challenging working conditions. For charters committed to diverse, community-reflective staffs, retention is an equity issue, not only an operational one. Schools that invest in competitive pay, genuine professional development, and leadership pathways for teachers of color will retain the educators their students most need.

Why Is Turnover Higher in Charter Schools? A Direct Answer

Charter schools tend to serve high-poverty, high-need urban communities — settings where turnover runs structurally higher regardless of governance type. Many charter teachers also carry heavier non-instructional responsibilities than peers in district schools, often without contract protections that cap workload. Add a persistent salary gap — teachers earn roughly 26% less than similarly educated workers in comparable fields — and charters that can't match district pay face an even steeper climb. The result is a 17.8% annual churn rate that drains institutional knowledge, disrupts student relationships, and costs money schools don't have.

The Bottom Line

Teacher retention is not a mystery. It is a leadership and culture challenge with documented solutions: competitive pay, supportive principals, and workloads that don't grind good people down. Charter leaders who treat retention as a strategic priority rather than an HR afterthought will spend less time replacing teachers and more time building the stable, mission-aligned teams their students deserve.

The 2026-27 school year is already underway. The schools that finish it with their best teachers still in the building are the ones that asked "what makes you want to stay?" before summer ended.
