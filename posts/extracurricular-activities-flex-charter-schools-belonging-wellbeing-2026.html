Extracurriculars Matter More Than Ever in 2026

# Extracurriculars Matter More Than Ever in 2026 — and Flex Charter Schools Are Taking Notes

A 2026 Child Mind Institute survey of 1,000 U.S. teens found that nearly half — 47% — reported moderate to severe loneliness, while 23% screened positive for anxiety and 21% for depression. At the same time, a separate national study from Springtide Research Institute found that young people between the ages of 13 and 25 who participate in at least one extracurricular group tend to show higher levels of flourishing and lower levels of loneliness, anxiety, and depression compared to those who are uninvolved.

Put those two findings side by side, and the message for charter school leaders is hard to ignore: structured participation in clubs, teams, events, and community activities isn't a "nice-to-have" enrichment layer. Right now, it may be one of the most consequential things a school can offer students.

What the 2026 Research Actually Says

The Springtide report — Getting Involved: Why Extracurriculars Matter, published September 2026 — is one of the most comprehensive looks at youth participation in organized groups in recent years. Researchers conducted in-depth interviews with 51 young people ages 13 to 25, then surveyed nearly 3,000 young people about their extracurricular involvement. Key findings:

  • Over three-quarters of young people ages 13 to 25 report participation in at least one extracurricular-type group — athletic teams, performing arts organizations, affinity clubs, or gaming communities — and this involvement is typically connected to their schools.
  • Nearly a quarter of young people are not involved in any group, club, or team. Among uninvolved teens, the leading barriers are difficulty finding or accessing groups, lack of transportation, and cost — not simply a lack of interest.
  • Despite challenges some participants face — difficulty fitting in, high expectations, maintaining balance — the positive associations between involvement and measures of well-being are robust across the data.

The Child Mind Institute's Building Bridges: Teen Loneliness and Connection study, released in October 2026, reinforces the stakes. Researchers found that teens who reported higher overall connectedness experienced lower loneliness and fewer symptoms of anxiety and depression. Notably, 88% of teens said in-person time is essential for feeling connected — and school ranked as one of their primary sources of that connection.

The Academic Case Is Just as Strong

The belonging benefits of extracurricular participation don't exist in isolation from academic outcomes. Studies consistently show that adolescents who participate in extracurricular activities earn higher grades, attend school more regularly, and report stronger social and emotional development than peers who do not. Longitudinal research links sustained participation to higher rates of college enrollment and persistence, particularly for low-income students. And according to aggregated research data compiled in 2026, extracurricular participation is associated with reducing the likelihood of dropping out of high school by 40% — a figure that should get the attention of every school leader thinking about chronic absenteeism and completion rates.

Research on extracurricular activity participation and school belonging also confirms a feedback loop: youth who engage in structured activities typically report greater school belonging, and that sense of belonging, in turn, predicts positive mental health outcomes. The two effects reinforce each other.

Why This Is an Especially Important Question for Flex Charter Schools

Can students at flex or independent-study charter schools experience the same sense of belonging as their peers at traditional schools?

The research says yes — but only if the school actively designs for it. Belonging doesn't emerge automatically from enrollment. And this is where flex and independent-study charter schools face both a persistent misconception and a genuine structural opportunity.

The misconception: that students who don't attend a fixed-schedule, single-campus school must be missing out on clubs, activities, and the social fabric that ties school communities together. This assumption still shapes how many families evaluate their options, even as more flex charter schools have built robust enrichment programming into their models.

The opportunity: flex charters are not bound by the scheduling constraints of a traditional school day. Rather than squeezing clubs and activities into the margins between dismissal and bus pickup, a well-designed flex school can build enrichment as a deliberate, school-wide pillar — scheduled, funded, and promoted as part of the school's core identity.

Excel Academy Charter School, a tuition-free TK–12 independent-study and virtual charter serving six counties in Southern California, illustrates what this looks like in practice. The school runs more than 80 community events annually — including a science fair, spelling bee, field trips, prom, and seasonal celebrations — alongside clubs and GATE programming. That's a deliberately designed social infrastructure, built into a flex model that otherwise centers family choice and student-paced learning. The school's reach across six counties also means those events draw families from a wide geographic area, creating cross-community peer relationships that wouldn't form inside a single neighborhood school.

What Intentional Enrichment Looks Like in Practice

The access gap in traditional schools is real and documented. Research from Washington, D.C. in early 2026 found that roughly 53,000 public school students lacked access to afterschool programs, while many programs maintained waitlists due to staffing, space, or cost constraints. The 21st Century Community Learning Centers initiative — the nation's only dedicated federal afterschool funding stream — serves roughly 1.4 million students annually nationwide, but advocates warn that funding consistently falls short of demand.

Flex charter schools that are doing enrichment well tend to share a few common design principles:

  • Recurring, academically connected events — science fairs, spelling bees, and history days that feel like celebrations of learning, not add-ons
  • Community-based field trips that connect coursework to real-world environments across a broad service area
  • Interest-based clubs (robotics, art, music, writing, student government) that give students consistent peer relationships and a sense of identity within the school
  • Cultural milestones — prom, graduation ceremonies, back-to-school gatherings — that provide the shared touchstones families value and students remember
  • Parent advisory structures that make enrichment community-owned and sustainable year over year

What School Leaders Should Do With This in 2026-27

The 2026 loneliness and belonging data gives flex charter leaders both urgency and evidence. Here are four concrete priorities for the current school year:

  1. Audit your enrichment calendar. Are events distributed across your full service area? Are they accessible to families who have to travel to participate?
  2. Lower barriers to participation. Transportation, scheduling conflicts, and cost are the most commonly cited reasons uninvolved students stay uninvolved. Build solutions into your program design — not buried in your FAQ page.
  3. Track participation as an outcome metric. If students who attend three or more school events show higher engagement scores or better retention rates, that's a compelling story for your LCAP, your authorizer, and your enrollment team.
  4. Lead with belonging in your enrollment messaging. Families comparing a flex charter to a traditional school aren't only asking "will my child learn?" They're asking "will my child feel like they belong somewhere?" The 2026 research gives you a data-backed, specific answer to that question — use it.

The loneliness numbers are sobering. But the other side of that data is equally clear: structured, school-connected participation works. Flex charter schools that build enrichment intentionally — not despite their model, but as a feature of it — are already ahead of where the research says every school needs to go.
