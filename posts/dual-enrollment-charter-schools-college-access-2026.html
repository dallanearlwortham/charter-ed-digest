Dual Enrollment Boom: What Charter Schools Must Know

# Dual Enrollment Is Booming—But Who's Getting Access?

More than 3 million high school students took at least one college course during the 2024–25 school year, setting a new national record. According to updated IPEDS data analyzed by the Community College Research Center (CCRC), dual enrollment reached 3.1 million students in 2024–25—up 10% from 2.8 million the prior year, and up 24% from 2.5 million just two years earlier. For charter school leaders and families, this surge isn't just an education statistic. It's a signal about what students and families increasingly expect from high school—and a clear opening for charter schools that are ready to lead.

But there's a catch. Even as participation climbs, access to dual enrollment remains stubbornly unequal. Understanding that gap—and what charter schools can do about it—is one of the most important school-choice conversations happening in 2026.

The Numbers Behind the Boom

The growth is nationwide. All but four states increased dual enrollment from 2023–24 levels, and every state increased from 2022–23 levels. Seven states grew more than 20% in a single year, and nine states grew more than 30% over two years.

Dual enrollment now accounts for 23% of overall community college enrollment nationally. In rural-serving community colleges, the share is even larger: high school students make up roughly a third of enrollment, compared to about a fifth at other community colleges. This matters for charter school leaders in rural and small-town communities—your students are a major driver of this trend whether your school has formalized it or not.

The scale of the impact on broader higher education is striking. The gain of 600,000 dual enrollment students accounted for 37% of all national growth in undergraduate enrollment from 2022 to 2024. In five states—Missouri, Montana, Ohio, Pennsylvania, and Wyoming—dual enrollment accounted for 100% of the growth in overall undergraduate enrollment. Without it, total college enrollment in those states would have declined.

Why Dual Enrollment Outcomes Matter

What does dual enrollment actually do for students? Research shows clear, measurable benefits—especially for students who are the first in their families to consider college.

A landmark 2024 CCRC report provided the first national and state-by-state breakdown of dual enrollment student outcomes, tracking students who began dual enrollment in fall 2015 through four years past high school graduation. The findings were consistent and significant:

  • 81% of dual enrollment students enrolled in college in the first year after high school, compared with about 70% of students overall.
  • Dual enrollment students who enrolled in college completed credentials at higher rates than students who entered without dual enrollment—with double-digit higher completion rates in 16 states.
  • Low-income dual enrollment students completed postsecondary credentials at a 45% rate, compared with 33% for low-income non-participants.
  • Black dual enrollment students completed at 40% versus 27% for non-participating Black students.
  • Hispanic dual enrollment students completed at 42% versus 33% for non-participating Hispanic students.

These aren't marginal improvements. For students who have historically faced the steepest barriers to higher education, dual enrollment is producing some of the largest documented equity gains in K–12 policy.

The "Program of Privilege" Problem

Despite the proven benefits, dual enrollment has a well-documented equity gap. Access to high-quality dual enrollment remains uneven: students from higher-income and college-educated families participate at substantially higher rates than Black, Latino, rural, low-income, and first-generation students. As a result, dual enrollment too often functions as a "program of privilege," disproportionately benefiting students who are already on track for college rather than expanding opportunity for those who need it most.

Nationally, just 8% of dual enrollment students in 2024–25 were Black—well below their 15% share of K–12 enrollment and 13% share of undergraduates overall. Hispanic, American Indian, Pacific Islander, and multiracial students were also underrepresented relative to their K–12 population shares.

Part of the problem is structural inertia. Although AP has long been promoted as an equity strategy, its benefits have largely gone to white students in affluent districts. When dual enrollment programs are designed with the same gatekeeping assumptions—rigid grade-point cutoffs, placement exams, off-campus requirements, English-only outreach—they replicate those exclusionary patterns rather than breaking them.

In rural Central California, high school counselors described what they called "college access deserts," where Latino students already travel long distances just to attend high school, let alone a college campus. Their response was to embed dual enrollment courses directly into the school day, making it possible for students to earn college credit without adding a logistical burden that would price out most families. That design choice—not just offering dual enrollment but engineering access to it—is what separates programs that serve everyone from programs that serve the already-advantaged.

Where Charter Schools Have the Edge

This is precisely where charter schools—particularly small, mission-driven ones—hold a structural advantage that large comprehensive high schools rarely can replicate.

Charter schools can make dual enrollment a core programmatic commitment rather than an add-on. They can build college partnerships into their founding model, set the expectation that every student will graduate with real college credit, and create the advising infrastructure that turns policy into practice. They can recruit students explicitly on the basis of that promise—and be held accountable for delivering it.

That combination of mission clarity and operational flexibility is rare in K–12. Families increasingly recognize it. When a charter school can tell an incoming ninth-grader, "You will leave here with college credits at no cost to your family, and here is exactly how we'll make that happen," it's a concrete, differentiated offer that no amount of branding can substitute for.

Moreno Valley Prep, a charter high school serving grades 9–12 in Angel Fire, New Mexico, illustrates the kind of environment where this approach takes root most naturally. Located in a geographically isolated community where students already navigate real barriers to access, the school has built a record of academic achievement that has earned it consistent placement on major national rankings, including Newsweek and the Washington Post Challenge Index. That track record in a small, rural setting reflects something that research confirms again and again: the close teacher-student relationships and tight community culture characteristic of small schools are precisely the conditions under which students take the risk of college-level coursework and succeed. Advising isn't a department—it's woven into every interaction.

What Families Should Ask When Evaluating a School's Dual Enrollment Program

Not all dual enrollment is created equal. A school that offers one elective through a nearby community college is a very different proposition from one that has built dual enrollment into its graduation pathway. Here are the questions that cut through the noise:

  • What percentage of students actually complete dual enrollment coursework? Enrollment rates can look impressive; completion rates reveal whether the school is supporting students through the work.
  • Are courses offered during the school day? Schools that embed dual enrollment in the regular schedule remove the single biggest access barrier for rural and low-income families.
  • What advising supports are in place? First-generation students benefit most from proactive guidance, not an open door they have to find on their own.
  • Does the credit transfer broadly? Credits that only apply to one nearby institution have limited long-term value.
  • Who is participating? If dual enrollment at a school is effectively limited to its highest-performing students, that school is perpetuating the "program of privilege" problem rather than solving it.

The Bottom Line

Dual enrollment is no longer an enrichment option for high achievers—it is a mainstream college-access strategy with a strong evidence base behind it. With 3.1 million students now participating nationally and growth accelerating in every state, the question is no longer whether dual enrollment matters. The question is whether the schools in your community are building the structures that make equitable access real, or leaving the students who would benefit most on the outside looking in.

Charter schools that commit to dual enrollment intentionally—and design it for the students who need it most rather than the students who least need the help—have a genuine opportunity to close one of the most persistent gaps in American education, and to give families a compelling, evidence-backed reason to choose them.

The opportunity is there. So is the urgency.
