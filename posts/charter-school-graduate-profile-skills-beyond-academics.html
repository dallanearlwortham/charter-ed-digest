What Should a Charter School Graduate Look Like?

# What Should a Charter School Graduate Look Like?

A diploma and a GPA tell part of the story. An increasingly loud chorus of researchers, employers, and education leaders says they don't tell enough of it — and charter schools are uniquely equipped to fill in the rest.

The "Portrait of a Graduate" Movement Is Reshaping High School

As of 2024, at least 20 states and countless districts had initiated "Portrait of a Graduate" reforms. In California alone, at least 100 districts have joined the trend. The premise is straightforward: a high school diploma should represent more than credit accumulation. It should signal that a young person is genuinely ready — for college, for work, and for life.

New York is on track to become the fourth state to adopt a portrait of a graduate in a short span, joining Kentucky, North Carolina, and Wyoming. Far more districts have adopted their own portraits — more localized versions that guide schooling at the campus level. A 2024 report by the ed-tech nonprofit Digital Promise looked at more than 60 graduate portraits from districts and states across the country, and the variation in ambition and specificity is striking.

These so-called "portrait of a graduate" documents detail the skills and attributes students need beyond academic ones to successfully enter postsecondary education or the workforce. A new analysis by the Collaborative for Academic, Social, and Emotional Learning (CASEL) set out to identify which attributes are most broadly valued across these frameworks. The skills that rise to the top? Communication, adaptability, and reflection — competencies that a traditional transcript almost never captures.

Why Charter Schools Are Particularly Well-Positioned Here

Charter schools have always operated with more latitude than traditional district schools to define what success means for their specific community. That flexibility is a significant structural advantage when it comes to building a meaningful, school-specific graduate profile.

Where a large district might spend years navigating bureaucratic and political friction to adopt a new vision of graduation, a charter school can move with intention and speed. Specifically, charters can:

  • Articulate a clear, school-specific graduate profile that reflects their mission and student population rather than a one-size-fits-all district standard
  • Align curriculum, advisory, and extracurricular programming directly to the skills and character traits named in that profile
  • Communicate the profile to families at enrollment so expectations and values are shared from day one
  • Use the profile as a living accountability tool — a concrete document that shapes how teachers design learning experiences year over year
  • Revisit and refine the profile as the school community grows and labor-market demands evolve

The Portrait of a Graduate helps ensure that students graduate not only with strong academic knowledge, but also with the skills, character, and confidence needed to succeed in the real world. For charter schools, which operate under a charter that already commits them to specific student outcomes, embedding a graduate profile into that compact is a natural extension — not an administrative add-on.

What a Strong Graduate Profile Actually Includes

Not all graduate profiles are created equal, and families and leaders should know the difference between a genuinely useful framework and a feel-good poster on a gymnasium wall. The strongest profiles tend to share several characteristics.

They balance academic and durable skills. The most actionable profiles don't pit rigorous academics against so-called soft skills — they integrate the two. Critical thinking, for example, is both a cognitive competency developed through challenging coursework and a workplace skill that employers explicitly and repeatedly request when surveyed about what new hires lack.

They are specific enough to guide instruction. Vague aspirations like "lifelong learner" or "global citizen" are inspirational but extremely hard to teach toward in a daily lesson plan. The best profiles translate broad goals into observable behaviors and dispositions that teachers and students can actually identify, practice, and measure.

They account for postsecondary diversity. Students need to master academic subjects, develop problem-solving skills, manage time effectively, and build communication and teamwork abilities — but readiness looks different for a student heading to a four-year university than for one entering a community college, a trade apprenticeship, or direct employment. A well-designed graduate profile prepares students for all of those paths rather than optimizing narrowly for one.

They are visible and lived-in by the whole school community. A graduate profile that exists only in a strategic plan binder doesn't drive culture. Schools that embed their profile into new-student orientation, advisory programs, project rubrics, and even hallway displays make it a real part of student identity — something students can articulate, not just something administrators reference at board meetings.

One School That Takes the Idea Seriously

Sena HS, a tuition-free charter high school in Albuquerque, NM serving grades 9–12, takes this commitment seriously enough to publish a dedicated Graduate Profile page as part of its public-facing identity. The school's broader mission centers on accelerating student success through equitable opportunities — and the graduate profile frames what that success is actually meant to look like on the other side of graduation, not just inside the classroom. For families comparing charter options, a school that publishes a clearly defined, publicly visible graduate profile is signaling something important: that it has thought rigorously about its own purpose and is willing to be held to it.

Questions Families Should Ask When Evaluating Any Charter High School

If you're choosing a high school for your child — or if you're a charter leader benchmarking your own program against peers — the graduate profile question cuts right to the heart of a school's educational philosophy. Here are the questions worth pressing on:

  • Does the school publish a written graduate profile or portrait of a graduate, and is it easy to find?
  • How does the school measure progress toward those outcomes beyond GPA and standardized test scores?
  • Are durable skills like communication, self-advocacy, and critical thinking explicitly woven into coursework and assessments — or just listed on a website?
  • Can the school point to specific programming — advisory periods, project-based learning, internships, senior capstones, community service — that actively develops those skills?
  • Do graduates and alumni describe feeling genuinely prepared for what came next, not just credentialed?

What does "Portrait of a Graduate" mean in a charter school context? At its core, a Portrait of a Graduate — sometimes called a Graduate Profile — is a school- or district-defined framework describing the knowledge, skills, and character traits a student should be able to demonstrate upon completing high school. Unlike state academic content standards, a graduate profile typically adds competencies like collaboration, resilience, civic responsibility, and career readiness. Charter schools can develop profiles tailored to their specific mission, community, and student population, making the framework more meaningful and actionable than a generic district-wide document tends to be.

The Bigger Picture

The workforce readiness gap continues to grow. At least 21 states have now developed Portrait of a Graduate frameworks, and more are actively exploring similar efforts — a sign that the field is moving toward a richer, more honest definition of what it means to be ready. Traditional educational systems have largely struggled to keep pace with social, economic, and technological change, and test-based accountability alone has not closed that gap.

Charter schools, with their mission-driven identities and structural flexibility, are arguably the best laboratory in American public education for proving that a different, fuller definition of "done" is not only possible — it's better for students. For charter leaders, developing or refining a graduate profile is not a branding exercise. It's a commitment: this is who our students will be when they walk across that stage. Getting specific about that commitment — and building the entire school culture around it — may be one of the highest-leverage investments a charter school can make in its own long-term impact.
