Why Charter Schools Are Winning With ELL Students

# Why Charter Schools Are Quietly Becoming the Go-To Choice for English Language Learner Families

The narrative that charter schools primarily serve a narrow slice of the student population is crumbling under the weight of new data. Across the country, families whose children are still learning English—one of the fastest-growing and historically underserved groups in American K–12 education—are choosing charter schools in growing numbers. And the outcomes they're finding there are hard to argue with.

ELL Enrollment in Charters Is Surging Nationwide

Over the last five years, the number of English language learners enrolled in New York City charter schools increased by 76%, with 138 charter schools featuring a preference for ELLs in their 2024–25 admission lotteries. That's not an outlier. Oklahoma's 2024–25 annual charter school report found that charter schools in that state serve significantly higher percentages of English Language Learners—34.3 percent of enrollment—and remain among the most racially diverse publicly funded schools in the state, with 58.5 percent of students identifying as Hispanic.

The trend reflects a broader reality: multilingual families are actively seeking out charter schools, and waitlists in many cities suggest demand is outpacing available seats.

What Does the Research Actually Say About ELL Outcomes in Charters?

Do charter schools produce better academic outcomes for English language learners than traditional public schools?

The most rigorous national evidence says yes—meaningfully so. A major report by The Century Foundation found that English learners in charter schools had stronger academic gains in reading and math than ELs in traditional public schools, with charter-attending ELs showing achievement gains equivalent to "36 more days of learning" each year in both reading and math. The 2023 CREDO national charter school study from Stanford University found that Hispanic ELL students specifically gained an additional 11 days of learning in reading and 8 extra days of learning in math by attending charter schools instead of their local district school option.

The long-term picture is even more striking. Research on Boston charter schools found that one year in a charter reduces the ELL achievement gap by 88.4 percent compared to the gaps that persist in traditional public schools—and that the likelihood ELL charter students will enroll in a four-year college doubles.

Texas: A Case Study in What's Possible

Nowhere is the ELL-charter connection more thoroughly documented than in Texas, where the charter sector serves a disproportionately large share of English learners and the outcomes are exceptional.

Nearly 120,000 English language learners attend Texas public charter schools, representing 31% of all charter school students, compared to 22% of public school students statewide. According to a 2023 study by the Thomas B. Fordham Institute, ELLs in Texas achieve English language proficiency faster at charter schools than at traditional public schools, despite starting with similar skill levels. On STAAR exams, more ELLs at Texas public charter schools made progress across all subjects (77% vs. 72% statewide) and in reading specifically (82% vs. 77%). If Texas public charter schools were their own state, their ELL students would rank first in the nation on NAEP reading exams for both fourth and eighth graders. ELL graduates from Texas public charter schools are twice as likely to complete rigorous AP, IB, and dual credit courses—and twice as likely to meet the state standard for college readiness and enroll in higher education. Those academic gains also translate into real earnings: ELLs and former ELLs who attended Texas public charter schools in high school earn about $2,000 more annually in their early careers than comparable peers.

The Texas Public Charter Schools Association—a statewide advocacy and member organization based in Austin—actively tracks and publicizes this ELL data as part of its mission to strengthen diverse, high-quality charter schools and ensure every student in Texas has access to ever-improving public school options. Their published research makes the case that serving English learners well isn't incidental to the charter model; it's a defining feature of it.

Why Are Charter Schools Outperforming for ELL Students?

Several structural factors help explain the gap:

  • Mission-driven culture. Many charters are explicitly founded to serve immigrant and low-income communities, building bilingual instruction and deep family engagement into their model from day one—not as an add-on, but as a core design principle.
  • Instructional flexibility. Without the rigid curricular constraints that often bind district schools, charters can adopt research-backed language acquisition models more quickly and tailor them to their specific student population.
  • Extended learning time. Longer school days and years, common in high-performing charter networks, give English learners more exposure to academic language and grade-level content—the volume of input that accelerates proficiency.
  • Data-driven responsiveness. Strong accountability cultures mean teachers and leaders track language development metrics closely and adjust instruction in near-real time rather than waiting for annual assessments.
  • Smaller learning communities. Many charters deliberately keep class sizes compact, which supports the relationship-building that research consistently links to faster language acquisition and stronger student engagement.

What ELL Families Should Ask When Choosing a Charter School

Not every charter school serves English learners equally well. The flexibility that enables excellence in some schools can mask neglect in others. Families navigating school choice should ask pointed questions before enrolling:

  1. What percentage of your students are currently classified as ELLs? A school with real experience serving ELL students has built systems; a school with very few may not have them yet.
  2. What language acquisition model do you use—and what's the evidence for it? Structured English immersion, dual language, and transitional bilingual programs each have different research bases and suit different student profiles.
  3. How do you measure and communicate language development progress to families, and in what languages?
  4. What is your ELL reclassification rate, and what happens to students after reclassification? Strong schools track former ELLs to ensure academic gains persist once services end.
  5. Do you have staff who speak the home languages of your ELL population?
  6. What does family engagement look like for non-English-speaking households? Translation services, community liaisons, and home-language communications are practical signals of genuine commitment.

What Charter Leaders Should Take Away

For charter school operators and leaders, the ELL enrollment surge is both an opportunity and a responsibility. Schools that build genuine capacity to serve multilingual learners—hiring bilingual instructional coaches, partnering with community organizations trusted by immigrant families, and designing intake processes that don't create unnecessary barriers—will not only grow enrollment but better serve the students who stand to benefit most from a high-quality school choice.

The data is making something clear that advocates have long argued: the flexibility that defines the charter model isn't just good for academically advanced students or those with specific learning profiles. It's proving especially powerful for children who arrive at school still building the language their entire academic future depends on.

That's not a niche. It's the future of American public education—and charter schools are already showing up to meet it.
