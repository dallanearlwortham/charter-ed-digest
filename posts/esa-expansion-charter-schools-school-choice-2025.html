ESAs Are Booming — What It Means for Charter Schools

# ESAs Are Booming. Here's What It Means for Charter Schools in 2025.

Five years ago, Education Savings Accounts were a niche policy experiment in a handful of states. Today, they are reshaping the ground beneath every charter school leader's feet — and the strategic response cannot wait for next year's planning cycle.

At the beginning of 2020, ESA programs were operating in only five states, while approximately a dozen states offered parents access to private school vouchers. As of the start of 2025, there are now more than 30 states offering ESAs, education vouchers, and other school choice programs like tax credit scholarships. In 2025 alone, five states — Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana, Texas, and Wyoming — enacted brand-new ESA programs. Nearly half of the students participating in private school choice programs are now enrolled in ESAs, and five additional states are expected to add ESA programs by the end of 2026.

The velocity of this policy wave is unlike anything the school choice movement has seen before. For charter school leaders, the right response is not alarm — it is strategy.

What Exactly Is an ESA, and How Does It Differ from Charter School Choice?

Public school choice generally refers to options such as charter schools, magnet schools, and open enrollment policies that allow families to choose public schools outside their residential zones. Private school choice — the category that includes ESAs — provides families with funds to cover private school tuition or other approved educational expenses. Policymakers sometimes express concerns that expanding one type of choice may come at the expense of the other, but evidence suggests these options can not only coexist but thrive simultaneously, offering a broader spectrum of opportunities for families.

In practical terms, an ESA redirects a portion of a student's per-pupil state funding into a family-controlled account that can be spent on private school tuition, tutoring, curricula, therapies, or some combination of all of these. Families must generally withdraw from public or charter school once they sign an ESA contract — meaning every ESA enrollee is a student who has actively chosen to exit the public system entirely.

Utah — home to over 137 charter schools — is already living this dual reality. Utah families have access to a range of education options, including three private school choice programs, charter schools, magnet schools, homeschooling, and inter-district open enrollment. The choice ecosystem there is not hypothetical. It is fully operational, and charter schools in the state are already competing for families who know they have multiple legitimate pathways available to them.

The Surprising Headline: Charter Enrollment Keeps Growing Anyway

Here is the data point that surprises most people outside the sector: even with the growth of other educational options like ESA programs, enrollment at charter schools defies expectations by continuing to grow. Charter schools are experiencing consistent, nationwide growth, adding over half a million students over the last six years — a 15 percent increase — even as the overall school-age population shrinks.

Charter school enrollment has increased by about 492,000 students from 2019–2020 to 2024–2025, and in 84 percent of states, the growth in charter school enrollment has outpaced the growth in the school-aged population. Charter schools are not just surviving the ESA surge — they are, in most states, still gaining ground within it.

But surviving and thriving strategically are two different postures. The ESA boom is permanently changing what informed, activated families expect from any school they consider. Charter leaders who adapt their messaging and model now will be the ones families find first — and choose most often.

What Do Families Actually Want — and Where Does That Put Charter Schools?

What factors drive parents to choose charter schools over other options, including ESAs? The research is specific. A National Alliance for Public Charter Schools survey found that safety is by far the highest concern — 77 percent of parents said it was absolutely essential to their child's education — followed by quality of instruction at 58 percent, with individualized support for their child cited as absolutely essential by 41 percent of parents. Separately, EdChoice research found that charter school parents prioritized a safe environment (37 percent), academic quality or reputation (36 percent), and morals, character, or values instruction (23 percent) as their top three factors when choosing a school.

These are precisely the same dimensions that ESA-funded private options compete on. The overlap is not coincidental — it is the terrain where the enrollment competition is now being fought, in Utah and in every other state where ESA programs have taken hold. A well-run charter school has a genuine, structural argument to make on every single one of those priorities.

How Smart Charter Schools Are Differentiating in an ESA Environment

The charter networks holding their own — and growing — in high-ESA states are doing three things deliberately:

Sharpening a specific identity. Families empowered by the ESA conversation now arrive with sharper questions than families did a decade ago: What makes your school different? What is your educational philosophy? Why should I choose you over assembling my own program with ESA funds? Generic answers lose enrollments. A clear, mission-driven identity wins them. Charter schools operate under performance-based contracts that require them to meet specific academic, financial, and organizational goals — and that built-in accountability is itself a competitive differentiator that private ESA-funded arrangements cannot match.

Expanding access points to meet families where they are. Hybrid models — a physical campus paired with full online enrollment — make charter schools reachable for families who might otherwise be drawn toward an ESA-funded home-based or private online program. Freedom Prep, a tuition-free public charter school network in Utah serving students from PreK through 12th grade, illustrates this approach directly: the network operates physical campuses in Provo, Vineyard, and St. George while also enrolling students fully online statewide — giving Utah families a single, coherent, publicly accountable option rather than a patchwork of ESA-funded vendors they must research, coordinate, and manage themselves.

Owning the "free and fully comprehensive" message. ESAs come with fund caps, administrative requirements, and the real burden of assembling a complete education from multiple sources. A high-performing charter school is tuition-free, publicly accountable, and delivers a full educational experience under one roof. Many families weighing ESA options do not fully appreciate this advantage until a school communicates it plainly and confidently — which means the communication gap is both the problem and the opportunity.

The Bigger Picture: A Choice Ecosystem, Not a Zero-Sum War

The most useful frame for charter school leaders is not ESAs versus charter schools. In January 2025, President Donald Trump issued an executive order instructing the Secretary of Education to release guidance about how states can use federal formula funds to support K-12 educational choice initiatives — a clear signal that the federal policy environment will continue amplifying family awareness of alternatives to assigned district schools for years to come.

States that invest heavily in school choice of any kind normalize the idea that families should have options. That normalization benefits charter schools as much as it benefits private schools. The families now researching ESA programs are the same families who, a generation ago, might never have looked beyond their assigned neighborhood school. They are engaged, motivated, and actively searching for the right fit.

A charter school with a clear identity, a strong digital presence, and a multi-pathway enrollment model is exceptionally well-positioned to make the case that the best choice does not require leaving the public system at all. The ESA boom did not create the demand for better schools. It made that demand visible, urgent, and impossible to ignore. Charter schools were built to answer it — and 2025 is the moment to make that case as loudly and specifically as possible.
