46 Million Parents Are Searching. Most Still Don't Switch Schools.

# 46 Million Parents Are Searching. Most Still Don't Switch Schools.

A record number of American families are actively looking for something better than the school their zip code assigned them. According to a January 2026 national survey by the National School Choice Awareness Foundation — covering 3,960 U.S. parents of school-aged children — 75% of parents considered a new school for at least one child in 2025, representing more than 46 million families and the highest level of school searching recorded in five years. Looking ahead into the 2026-27 school year, 62% of parents say they are likely to search for a new school this year.

And yet: 31% of parents — nearly 20 million people — searched for a new school and still did not enroll a child.

That gap between intent and action is one of the most important, and least discussed, structural problems in K–12 education today. Understanding why it exists — and what it reveals about how families actually navigate school choice — matters enormously for charter school leaders, education advocates, and any family currently stuck in the research-but-not-enrolled phase.

Why So Many Families Search But Don't Switch

The NSCAF survey identifies the most common barriers families hit after they start searching but before they commit. The findings are revealing:

  • Concerns about disruption (33%) — Families worry that switching schools mid-stream will harm their child socially, academically, or emotionally. The fear of making things worse stops action even when the status quo is clearly unsatisfactory.
  • Uncertainty about making the wrong choice (26%) — With more options available than ever before, paradox-of-choice paralysis is real. Parents who don't have a clear framework for evaluating schools default to inaction.
  • Limited availability or waitlists (26%) — High-demand schools, particularly classical charters and specialized programs, regularly run lotteries. A family that discovers their preferred school has a two-year waitlist often abandons the search entirely rather than planning ahead.
  • Transportation challenges (25%) — In many states, charter schools are not required to provide busing. For working-class families, a school that requires a 45-minute drive twice a day is effectively inaccessible regardless of its quality.

These aren't irrational fears. They are predictable friction points that the school-choice ecosystem has not yet solved at scale. And they compound each other: a parent who fears disruption, is uncertain about how to evaluate options, discovers a waitlist at the school they liked, and has no transportation solution is very likely to close the browser tab and stay put.

The Awareness Problem Underneath the Action Gap

Before families can even hit those enrollment barriers, many face a more fundamental obstacle: they don't know what's available. According to new national polling data from EdChoice, parents broadly support education choice policies, but awareness of specific options remains low — parents can identify whether their state has a school choice program only slightly better than they would by randomly guessing.

This is striking. Thirty-four states have expanded school choice through ESAs, tax credits, vouchers, open enrollment, or charter growth over the past five years — and in states like Arizona, Indiana, North Carolina, and Ohio, between one-third and nearly half of school parents report being unaware of their state's choice program. Support is high. Awareness is not.

The practical result is that millions of families are exercising school choice by default — staying in an assigned school not because they've weighed their options and chosen it, but because they don't know there are options to weigh.

What This Means for Charter Schools Right Now

For charter school leaders, the search-to-enrollment gap isn't just a marketing problem. It's a design problem and a communication problem with real equity consequences.

Families who successfully navigate school choice tend to be those with more time, more social capital, and more access to informal networks where school options get discussed. A first-generation immigrant family, a single working parent, or a family who recently moved to a new state is far less likely to stumble onto the right charter school through word of mouth. The awareness gap disproportionately affects exactly the families that high-quality charter schools most want to serve.

Several practical responses are worth considering:

Simplify the entry point. Every step a family has to take before they can learn whether a school is right for their child is a step at which they may leave. Information nights, virtual open houses, and clear online enrollment timelines reduce friction and signal that the school is designed to welcome families, not filter them.

Address the disruption fear head-on. Families who fear they'll harm their child by switching need concrete evidence — not promises. Testimonials from families who made a mid-year or inter-grade transition, data on how new students typically perform in their first semester, and explicit onboarding support all reduce the perceived risk of switching.

Build waitlist awareness earlier. A family that learns about a school in April and discovers a lottery closed in February will not enroll that year. Charter schools that proactively market lottery timelines — especially to families whose children are still in the younger grades — convert more interested families into actual applicants over time.

Make transportation explicit. For fully online charter schools, this barrier evaporates entirely. For in-person schools, providing clear guidance on carpool networks, public transit options, or existing family communities in various neighborhoods can meaningfully reduce the transportation friction that stops real families from enrolling.

The Online Charter Advantage in Closing the Gap

Fully online public charter schools are structurally positioned to address several of the most common search-to-enrollment barriers simultaneously. There is no transportation problem. There are no geographic attendance zones. Disruption risk is lower because a student can often begin mid-semester without losing their social footing or academic continuity. And the enrollment process can be completed from home, on a family's schedule, without requiring a campus visit during working hours.

This is part of what makes schools like LANV — the Leadership Academy of Nevada, a free accredited online public charter school serving Nevada students in grades 4–12 — a structurally accessible option for families who have done the searching but kept hitting walls. LANV's mission is to provide highly motivated and self-directed students with a classical education so they can become principled leaders, and its fully online model means that the logistical barriers that stop so many families from acting on their school-choice intentions simply don't apply. A Nevada family that has spent months researching classical education options, found a waitlist, worried about a commute, and stalled — has a concrete path forward that doesn't require any of those tradeoffs.

What Searching Families Actually Need

The January 2026 survey notes that as school choice becomes mainstream, parents want more information — not less. The families who searched but didn't enroll weren't indifferent. They were stuck. The difference between a family that searches and acts and one that searches and stalls is rarely motivation. It's almost always information, timing, or a specific logistical barrier that nobody helped them solve.

For charter schools, that means the work doesn't end at building a great academic program. It extends to every touchpoint a searching family encounters before they ever speak to an admissions coordinator: the school website, the reviews on third-party platforms, the social media presence, the clarity of lottery and enrollment dates, and the ease of getting a real question answered by a real person.

Sixty-two percent of U.S. parents say they plan to search for a new school in 2026. That's an enormous pool of families who are open to something better. The schools that close the gap between search and enrollment won't necessarily be the ones with the best programs — they'll be the ones that make it easiest for a motivated family to say yes.
