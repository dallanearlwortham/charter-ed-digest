High-Dosage Tutoring: Charter Schools' Edge in Recovery

# Half a Grade Level Behind: Why High-Dosage Tutoring Is the Academic Recovery Move Charter Schools Can't Afford to Skip

Five years after the pandemic shuttered classrooms, the numbers are still uncomfortable reading. As of spring 2024, the average U.S. student remained nearly half a grade level behind pre-pandemic achievement in both math and reading—and in reading, students are actually further behind than they were in 2022. No state improved in both subjects on the most recent Nation's Report Card. Federal relief dollars poured into schools, and achievement gaps widened anyway: the highest-income districts were nearly four times more likely to recover in both math and reading than the lowest-income districts.

For charter school leaders serving high-need communities, this is not background noise. It is the central challenge of the next several years—and the evidence now points clearly to one of the most powerful tools available to address it.

What High-Dosage Tutoring Actually Is

High-dosage tutoring (HDT) is not the ad hoc extra help a student might get after school once a week. Researchers define it as frequent, structured, small-group or one-on-one instructional support—typically three or more sessions per week—delivered by trained tutors during or closely connected to the school day. The "dosage" is the point: intermittent help rarely moves the needle; sustained, intensive support does.

The research is remarkably consistent. A 2024 meta-analysis of 89 studies of tutoring in literacy and math found academic gains equal to about three to four months of extra learning for early elementary students who received high-dosage tutoring. Johns Hopkins researchers found that one-to-one high-dosage tutoring is more effective than other reading interventions, including computer-driven tutorials and small-group approaches. And the National Student Support Accelerator has documented that elementary students gain more than four months' worth of reading instruction through well-implemented HDT programs.

That kind of effect size is rare in education research. When a strategy reliably produces three to four months of accelerated learning, it deserves to be treated as infrastructure—not a pilot or a one-year grant experiment.

The Implementation Gap (and Why Charter Schools Can Close It)

Here is the direct answer to a question many school leaders are asking right now:

Why aren't more schools using high-dosage tutoring if the evidence is this strong? The honest answer is logistics. Traditional district schools face scheduling constraints, union agreements, per-pupil funding formulas, and procurement timelines that make embedding tutoring into the school day genuinely difficult. Each minute of tutoring that students received in recent national programs appeared to be as effective as pre-pandemic research predicted—but students simply weren't getting enough minutes altogether, because building the systems to deliver consistent dosage proved harder than expected.

Charter schools are not exempt from these challenges, but they carry a structural advantage: the autonomy to redesign schedules, hire interventionists, and build tutoring into daily operations without waiting for district-level approval. A charter school that decides Tuesday that every struggling third-grade reader will receive three 20-minute one-on-one sessions per week can often build that model into the master schedule within weeks. A comparable district school may take years.

States are beginning to recognize this. South Carolina established a $15 million pilot program that explicitly allows school districts and charter schools to implement high-dosage, low-ratio tutoring in mathematics and reading. The evidence base is mature enough that policymakers are now funding it at scale—which means schools that have already built the internal capacity will be positioned to access those resources first.

Starting Early Is the Multiplier

The research on when HDT delivers the greatest returns points in one direction: earlier is almost always better. Foundational literacy and numeracy skills compound—students who decode fluently in second grade access content more easily in every subsequent subject, every subsequent year. Students who fall behind in phonics and phonemic awareness in kindergarten and first grade face steeper and steeper remediation curves the longer intervention is delayed.

This is precisely why Charter School of the Dunes, a tuition-free PreK–8 public charter school serving Gary and Northwest Indiana, operates an Early Learning Center dedicated to PreK through second grade. Embedding structured support at that developmental window—before reading difficulties calcify into identity and habit—is where high-dosage tutoring can do its deepest, most durable work.

Charter schools with early childhood programs have a genuine first-mover advantage here. The youngest learners are the highest-leverage targets for intensive intervention, and schools that own the PreK–8 pipeline can design a continuous support arc rather than inheriting students at grade 3 already two years behind.

What Effective HDT Programs Have in Common

Across the strongest programs in the research literature, several features recur:

  • Trained, consistent tutors — students benefit most from working with the same tutor session after session, building rapport and continuity
  • Alignment with classroom instruction — tutoring sessions that reinforce and extend what happens in the core classroom outperform disconnected remediation
  • Embedded scheduling — programs delivered during the school day, not as opt-in after-school add-ons, produce higher attendance and more consistent dosage
  • Frequent progress monitoring — short cycle assessments allow tutors to adjust focus rather than running through a fixed curriculum regardless of student response
  • Focus on foundational skills first — particularly in early grades, phonics and phonemic awareness targeted through HDT show the strongest effect sizes in literacy

One-on-one tutoring consistently outperforms small-group delivery in the research, but high-quality two-on-one or three-on-one models still produce meaningful gains and are more scalable in most school settings.

The Funding Cliff Is Real—But the Urgency Is Bigger

Federal pandemic relief funding (ESSER) has expired, and many tutoring programs built on that money are now scrambling. Only 23 states currently provide competitive grant or formula funding that allows schools to direct dollars toward high-impact tutoring. This is a genuine challenge.

But the appropriate response to a funding cliff is not to abandon the strategy that works—it's to build sustainable, lower-cost versions of it into regular operations. Peer tutoring programs, embedded interventionists funded through Title I, partnerships with university teacher preparation programs, and community-based tutoring organizations are all levers charter schools can pull without depending on one-time federal dollars.

The pandemic did not create America's learning gaps—it exposed and deepened ones that already existed. For schools in high-poverty communities, the recovery window is not infinite. Students who were in kindergarten in 2020 are now in fifth and sixth grade. Every year that passes without intensive, targeted intervention is a year of compounding disadvantage that becomes harder and harder to reverse.

High-dosage tutoring is not a silver bullet. But among the tools education research has evaluated at scale, few have delivered as consistently. Charter schools willing to treat it as a permanent feature of their academic model—not a temporary patch—are the ones most likely to look back on this period as a turning point rather than a lost decade.
