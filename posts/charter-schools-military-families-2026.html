Charter Schools & Military Families: What 2026 Changes Mean

# Charter Schools and Military Families: Why 2026 Is a Turning Point for Enrollment Access

For military families, the question "Where will my kid go to school next year?" is rarely rhetorical—it's one of the most stressful logistics of every PCS move. Charter school waitlists, which don't pause for deployment orders, have long made that stress worse. In 2026, that dynamic is beginning to shift in meaningful ways, driven by new state laws, federal attention, and a growing number of charter schools that are treating military family enrollment as a design priority rather than an afterthought.

The PCS Problem in Plain Terms

Military-connected kids experience far more school transitions than their civilian counterparts. It's not unusual for a military child to attend five or more schools between kindergarten and their senior year of high school. Every move means new enrollment paperwork, new graduation credit requirements, new social ecosystems to navigate—and, if a family has been eyeing a high-demand charter school, the very real possibility of starting over at the bottom of a waitlist.

Idaho's Rep. David Leavitt framed the structural problem clearly when arguing for his state's 2026 military charter preference bill: military members are often ordered to move between duty stations, making it difficult to enroll in charter schools that frequently have long waitlists. By the time a military child climbs to the top of a list, it may already be time for new orders. That scenario plays out across the country, in every state with popular charter schools and active military installations nearby.

That's not an edge case. It's a structural barrier embedded in how most charter lotteries operate—and one that disproportionately disadvantages the families who have the least control over when and where they live.

What States Are Actually Doing About It in 2026

So far in 2026, six states have passed legislation that either increases transparency or changes access to public charter schools. Among the most consequential changes for military families:

  • Idaho passed HB 726, allowing charter schools to give enrollment preference to students from military families in lottery and waitlist processes. The law covers both active-duty military families and Reserve/National Guard families, and went into effect on July 1, 2026.
  • South Carolina authorized charter schools to extend enrollment preference to dependents of active-duty military members residing or stationed in the state—without requiring participation in a standard lottery—provided enrollment within that preference category stays within defined limits.
  • At the federal level, a 2026 executive order directed the Secretary of Defense to review mechanisms under which military-connected families may use Department of Defense funds to attend schools of their choice, including private schools. That directive signals that military school choice is now a priority well above the state legislative level.

Together, these moves reflect a recognition that the school choice framework, for all its expansion over the past decade, has not been purpose-built for the realities of military life.

Why Charter Schools Specifically?

Charter schools sit at a compelling intersection for military families: they are tuition-free public schools offering specialized curricula or distinctive school cultures that families can deliberately choose—not simply be assigned to based on a zip code. For families who relocate every two or three years, the ability to research and select a school with a known identity and consistent mission is significant. A charter's defined instructional philosophy—whether STEM-focused, classical, arts-integrated, or project-based—travels with a family's expectations even when the home address changes. When a student arrives mid-year at a school whose values and structure are already understood, the adjustment curve shortens for everyone: student, parents, and teachers alike.

What does a quality charter school actually offer a military family? In a word: predictability. Not rigidity—but the reassurance that a school has a coherent identity, communicates it clearly, and runs the same way in November as it does in August. For families who have cycled through assignment-based schools in four states, that consistency is not a minor amenity. It's a primary reason to choose.

There are currently about 7,800 charter schools in the U.S. serving approximately 3.8 million children—around 7.2% of students nationwide—and that number has more than doubled over the last ten years. As the charter sector grows, so does its relevance to military families looking for reliable options in unfamiliar cities.

The Waitlist Tension: Access vs. Fairness

The Idaho legislative debate also surfaced a tension that charter leaders should understand clearly, not just observe from a distance. Mountain Home Superintendent James Gilbert raised a pointed objection: that military preference bills could allow a proposed charter school to sweep up military families currently enrolled in the district, effectively targeting a specific demographic. "We're now building or proposing charter schools to target certain student demographics," Gilbert said. "To me, that certainly seems less than fair."

That concern deserves a thoughtful response from the charter community. Preference tiers in lotteries—founders' children, siblings, now military dependents—stack. A charter school navigating multiple preference categories in 2026 should be able to articulate the rationale for each tier clearly and communicate that rationale transparently to all applicants. The equity argument for military preference is genuine and well-grounded. So is the responsibility to ensure those preferences don't inadvertently destabilize the public schools serving the same communities.

What Charter Schools Can Do Right Now—Without Waiting for Legislation

Charter leaders don't need a new state law to begin serving military families better. Several practical changes are available immediately:

  • Publish your lottery timeline clearly, early, and in plain language. Military families often have short windows—sometimes just weeks—to research schools before a PCS move. A clear, findable timeline is the most basic form of welcome.
  • Name a dedicated point of contact for incoming military families. One staff member who understands PCS timelines, knows how to expedite records requests, and can answer questions without routing families through four departments removes enormous friction.
  • Clarify your mid-year enrollment policy explicitly. Families relocating in October or February shouldn't encounter a wall of "applications only open in January" language with no explanation of alternatives.
  • Consider a provisional seat-hold policy for waitlisted families with PCS orders. Some schools are piloting provisional enrollment for families who can document an imminent move to the area—allowing them to plan school transitions with more certainty.
  • Proactively communicate with local installation School Liaison Officers (SLOs). SLOs are the primary point of contact for PK–12 school-related matters on military installations and actively guide families toward local school options. A charter that has a relationship with its nearest SLO is a charter that appears on more families' radars.

Schools that treat the military family enrollment experience as an intentional design problem—rather than an occasional exception managed ad hoc—tend to become the schools military families recommend to each other, across installations and across years.

A School That Already Gets It

eStem Charter School Seconday, part of the eStem Public Charter Schools network in Little Rock, Arkansas, is a concrete example of a charter that has built military family support directly into its admissions infrastructure. The network lists a dedicated "Support for Military Families" page within its admissions navigation—placing it alongside standard enrollment information rather than burying it in a resource footnote. The network has served more than 3,000 students across elementary, junior high, and high school campuses since opening in 2008, and manages high demand through a random, anonymous lottery each March that is open to any Arkansas resident. That open-enrollment model, combined with proactive military family outreach, is exactly the kind of approach the 2026 policy conversation is pushing more charter schools to adopt.

The Bigger Picture

In a January 2026 parent survey, 75% of U.S. parents of school-aged children engaged actively with school choice for the 2025-2026 school year. That level of engagement isn't a blip—it reflects a sustained shift in how families understand their options. Military families are among the most motivated, well-researched choosers in that group, precisely because assignment-based schooling has consistently failed to account for the realities of their lives.

So far in 2026, eleven states have created new or expanded existing school choice programs. The legislative momentum around military-family charter access is part of a broader reckoning with who the school choice system is actually built to serve. For charter schools, the opportunity here is larger than compliance with new preference laws. It's the chance to become the institution that military families seek out by name, trust on short notice, and stay loyal to long after the next set of orders arrives.

The families who move the most are often the ones paying the closest attention to what a school actually stands for. That's a brief worth earning.
