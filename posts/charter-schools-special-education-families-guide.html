Charter Schools & Special Ed: What Families Must Know

# Charter Schools and Special Education: What the Data Shows—and What Every Family Should Ask Before Enrolling

If you have a child with a disability and you're considering a charter school, you're likely running into a frustrating paradox: the schools that tend to offer the most flexible, personalized learning environments also have a well-documented history of serving fewer students with disabilities than traditional public schools. Understanding why that gap exists—and what your rights are regardless of which school your child attends—is one of the most practical things a family can do before enrollment season begins.

The Enrollment Gap Is Real, But the Story Is More Complicated

About 83.1% of special education students in charter schools spend 80% or more of their school day in general education settings, compared to 67.5% of students with disabilities in traditional public schools, according to research from The Center for Learner Equity. That is a meaningful difference in inclusion rates—and it favors charter schools.

At the same time, the overall share of charter school students who qualify for special education services tells a different story. The percentage of students in charter schools qualifying for services under IDEA grew from 10.4% in 2012 to 11.5% in 2021—still lower than the 14.1% enrollment rate in traditional public schools that same year.

Why does the gap persist? Research points to a perception problem as much as a policy problem. Students with identified disabilities—particularly those with autism and those with a speech or language impairment—are less likely to enroll in charter schools, and the gap grows considerably as students progress from kindergarten through third grade. Families' beliefs that charters cannot or will not appropriately serve their child's needs are sticky and, in essence, become self-fulfilling. Traditional public schools, meanwhile, enroll more students with autism and intellectual disabilities—students who typically need more intensive support and services.

Your Child's IDEA Rights Don't Change at the Charter School Door

One of the most important things for any family to understand before making a charter school choice: the legal protections that come with a disability do not disappear at enrollment. Charter schools must follow IDEA and Section 504 just as traditional public schools do. They must provide a free and appropriate public education and honor all legal rights tied to special needs education. The way services are delivered may vary, but the obligation to provide support is the same under federal law.

In practical terms, that means several things families should hold onto:

  • If your child has an IEP when they enroll, the charter school must implement it.
  • Every charter school must have a system for screening children to determine whether they should be referred for a special education evaluation.
  • Parents are afforded all rights and protections specified in IDEA that they would have received if the child were enrolled in any other public school program.
  • If a school and a family cannot reach agreement on which services to implement, the family can request mediation or an impartial special education hearing.

Where Charter Schools Are Falling Short—And What Is Being Done About It

Knowing your rights is a starting point, not a finish line. Charter schools have failed to consistently address the needs of students with disabilities or, perhaps more importantly, to close the gap in educational outcomes between students with disabilities and their peers. This can have long-term negative consequences on students' academic, mental, and behavioral development if their charter schools are not prepared to support them.

A multi-year Center for Learner Equity study found that charter school authorizers could better leverage their oversight to help close opportunity gaps. Recommended actions include requiring corrective action when schools do not meet benchmarks for students with disabilities, publicly reporting outcome data, and building state-level guidance on the true costs of providing appropriate special education in charter settings.

One data point that deserves particular attention from families doing their homework: students with disabilities attending charter schools were secluded 2.11 times more than their peers without disabilities—compared to 1.65 times more in traditional public schools. Disproportionate discipline is a problem across both sectors, but the disparity is wider in charters and is worth asking about directly before you enroll.

There are genuine bright spots. The percentage of students with disabilities enrolled in Advanced Placement courses in charter schools grew from 3.3% in 2018–19 to 4.4% in 2020–21, compared to just 2.8% at traditional public high schools. Higher inclusion rates and growing AP access suggest that for students with disabilities who land in the right charter, the experience can meaningfully exceed what a traditional district school offers.

What Integrated Early Support Can Look Like Before Kindergarten

One place the broader school choice ecosystem is doing right by families of children with disabilities is at the pre-K level—where some providers are directly solving the forced choice families have always faced between therapy and academic preparation. Miami-based Prism is a concrete example of this integrated model: serving children under 6 with autism by combining 1:1 ABA therapy delivered by a Registered Behavior Therapist with group school readiness instruction, all in the same setting. A Board Certified Behavior Analyst personally assesses each child and creates an individualized treatment plan, so the clinical and the academic work are aligned from day one rather than siloed across different providers and schedules. Children who arrive at kindergarten with both behavioral foundations and school readiness skills are far better positioned to succeed in a charter—or any school—once they age in. Early investment at this level is what makes later inclusion possible.

6 Questions Every Family Should Ask a Charter School Before Enrolling a Child With a Disability

What is the single most important thing a family can do when evaluating a charter school for a child with a disability? Ask pointed, specific questions before you commit—not after a placement goes wrong. Here are six that matter most:

  1. How many students with IEPs are currently enrolled, and what are their primary disability categories? A school with no current IEP students almost certainly lacks the staffing infrastructure your child will need.
  2. Who is your special education coordinator, and what are their qualifications? You want a named person with a clear role—not a vague reference to a support team.
  3. How are related services—speech, OT, ABA—delivered? On campus or through outside providers? Off-campus services frequently get deprioritized once the school day gets busy or transportation becomes complicated.
  4. What is your process if my child's current IEP requires something your school does not currently offer? The candor of the answer tells you more about the school's true readiness than any brochure will.
  5. Can I speak with a current family whose child has a similar disability profile? Schools that are genuinely confident in their special education program will say yes without hesitation.
  6. What does your discipline data look like for students with IEPs? Disproportionate suspension or seclusion rates are a concrete, measurable warning sign worth taking seriously.

The Bottom Line

As competition grows among private, traditional, and nontraditional schools, charter schools that want to lead on equity need to double down on enrolling and supporting students with disabilities—with real staffing, transparent data, and genuine accountability from authorizers. Schools that communicate their special education capacity clearly and cultivate strong connections to early intervention providers will be better positioned to serve families who have too often assumed there is no seat at the table for their child.

The data increasingly supports what should be a motivating truth for charter leaders and families alike: for students with disabilities who do enroll in a well-prepared charter school, inclusion rates and academic access can exceed what traditional public schools provide. The remaining work is closing the perception gap, building real capacity, and making sure every family has the specific information they need to find the charter that will genuinely deliver on its promise.
