The Charter Funding Gap: What's Still Broken in 2026

# The Charter Funding Gap: What's Still Broken in 2026

A charter school can outperform every district school in its city and still be funded like a second-tier institution. That mismatch sits at the center of one of the most persistent—and underreported—problems in public education finance: the per-pupil funding gap between charter schools and traditional public schools.

In 2026, that gap is finally getting serious legislative attention in several states. But for charter leaders navigating tight budgets, the fight is far from over.

How Large Is the Gap, Really?

Research across major U.S. cities has consistently found that charter school students receive substantially less public funding than peers in traditional district schools. One widely cited multi-city analysis found that, on average, public charter schools received approximately $5,828 less than traditional public schools in per-pupil funding—a gap of roughly 27 percent. Local funding showed an even sharper disparity, averaging $8,094 per charter student across eighteen cities studied.

The core driver is local property tax revenue. District schools routinely access local bond levies and capital construction dollars that flow through local tax bases—revenue streams most charter schools are legally or structurally excluded from. The result is a chronic shortfall, especially on the facilities side.

Why the gap matters beyond the balance sheet:

  • Facilities costs consume a disproportionate share of charter operating budgets, crowding out instructional spending
  • Smaller per-pupil allocations make it harder to offer competitive teacher salaries, directly compounding recruitment and retention challenges
  • The inequity falls hardest on the students charter schools disproportionately serve: low-income families, students of color, and children with disabilities

The Facilities Problem Is the Funding Problem

Facilities funding is where the inequity is sharpest and most structural. Most charter schools must lease or purchase building space on the open market while receiving little or no state help to cover those costs—costs that district schools never face because they occupy publicly owned buildings maintained through separate capital budgets.

Only 18 states offer a per-pupil facilities allowance to charter schools at all, leaving the majority of charter operators to absorb real estate expenses from the same per-pupil operating dollars intended to pay teachers and run classrooms.

Some states are changing this in 2026. Indiana, Florida, and Idaho have expanded facilities-sharing policies and strengthened state revolving loan programs for charters—models that ExcelinEd's January 2026 policy trends report identified as likely to be replicated elsewhere this year.

Ohio offers a useful case study in incremental progress. Excluding facilities aid, high-quality charter schools in Ohio now receive roughly 90 percent of what local districts receive, while other brick-and-mortar charters sit at approximately 75 percent. Those numbers represent real improvement—Ohio charters received only about 68 percent of comparable district funding as recently as fiscal years 2015–17—but they also illustrate how much ground remains to close even in states actively working on equity.

When Formula Fixes Get Quietly Undone: The D.C. Example

Even where strong per-pupil formulas exist, budget pressures can erode them without making headlines. A June 2026 fight over Washington, D.C.'s fiscal year 2027 education budget demonstrated exactly how fragile formula-based equity can be.

The mayor's proposed budget moved $85 million out of the Uniform Per Student Funding Formula (UPSFF)—the mechanism used to allocate dollars equally to both traditional public schools and charter schools—redirecting those funds exclusively to D.C. public schools. The DC Charter School Alliance calculated that the resulting per-pupil gap came to approximately $1,850 in favor of DCPS students, affecting more than 48,000 children who attend public charter schools across the District.

The Alliance's executive director noted that for a smaller charter school serving 300 to 400 students, that per-pupil difference could mean $2 million in lost funding in a single year—enough to trigger teacher layoffs or eliminate specialty programs. It was a vivid reminder that funding equity isn't secured once and held forever. It requires organized advocacy at every budget cycle, in every state capital.

Pennsylvania: A State in Active Reform—and Active Debate

Pennsylvania has been a flashpoint for charter funding debates for years, particularly around cyber charter schools. The 2025-26 state budget introduced reforms to how much districts must pay in cyber charter tuition, with the legislature estimating statewide savings for public school districts of around $178 million. New accountability requirements were added as well—including attendance verification via webcam during synchronous instruction and mandatory weekly wellness checks on students learning remotely.

Those reforms followed a 2025 state auditor's report finding that five Pennsylvania cyber charter schools had legally increased revenues by $425 million and grown their reserve funds by 144 percent between 2020 and 2023, driven largely by a tuition formula untethered from actual instructional costs. Advocates on both sides acknowledged the changes as a meaningful step while disagreeing sharply about whether they went far enough.

This is precisely the terrain where statewide advocacy organizations become indispensable. The Pennsylvania Coalition of Public Charter Schools (PCPCS) serves as the unified voice for Pennsylvania charter schools across these funding and policy debates, equipping member schools with advocacy toolkits, legislative resources, grant programs such as the Pennsylvania Charter School Program (PACSP) Grant, and professional training—so that charter schools and the families they serve have organized, sustained representation when funding formulas are being rewritten in Harrisburg.

What Charter Leaders Can Do Right Now

Waiting for legislatures to act isn't a funding strategy. Charter leaders who understand the policy landscape can take concrete steps today:

1. Quantify your school's specific gap. Calculate your actual per-pupil revenue against the comparable figure for your local district. A school-specific number is far more persuasive with legislators and local media than a national average.

2. Map your facilities cost structure. Know exactly what percentage of your operating budget goes to lease or mortgage payments. This data is the foundation of any credible case for facilities aid in states that don't yet provide it.

3. Engage your statewide coalition. State charter associations aggregate school-level data, coordinate legislative testimony, and maintain relationships with lawmakers across party lines. Individual schools rarely move policy on their own.

4. Monitor formula changes proactively—not just at year-end. As D.C. demonstrated in 2026, per-pupil formulas can be restructured in ways that harm charter students without any announcement. Budget season requires active monitoring from the first proposal through final passage.

5. Frame the argument around students, not operators. Families who choose public charter schools are still public school families. Their children are still public school students. Funding equity framed as a student-level fairness question tends to be a more durable political argument than one framed around the interests of charter management.

The Bottom Line on Charter Funding Equity

Why do charter schools receive less funding than traditional public schools? The structural answer: most states route local property tax revenue and capital construction dollars exclusively through school district systems, leaving charter schools dependent on state per-pupil allocations that typically don't fully account for facilities costs. The resulting gap can reach thousands of dollars per student per year—and it falls most heavily on the schools serving the highest-need communities, in cities where property values and local tax bases are often weakest.

The 2026 policy environment offers real reasons for optimism. More states are treating the facilities gap as a solvable problem. Ohio's incremental equity supplements are demonstrating a replicable playbook. Pennsylvania's contentious cyber charter reforms are pushing the conversation toward cost transparency and accountability. And ExcelinEd's 2026 trend analysis points to a growing wave of specialized charter models—healthcare-focused schools, microschool collaboratives—that will further pressure states to modernize how they calculate what education actually costs to deliver.

But optimism doesn't balance a budget. The gap will close only through persistent, data-driven advocacy—school year after school year, legislative session after legislative session—until public funding actually follows the public school student, regardless of which public school they choose.
