The Charter School Funding Gap: What Leaders Must Know in 2026

# The Charter School Funding Gap: What Leaders Must Know in 2026

Charter school leaders routinely deliver strong academic results with one hand tied behind their backs. While they are held to the same public accountability standards as traditional public schools, they are rarely funded like them. In the average major city, public charter schools receive roughly 27–30% less in per-pupil funding than traditional public schools — a gap that has persisted for more than a decade and that, in 2026, is sparking legislative fights from North Carolina to Georgia to Washington, D.C.

Understanding this gap — where it comes from, what's driving change in 2026, and what school leaders can do right now — is one of the most practical things a charter leader or informed charter family can do this school year.

---

How Large Is the Funding Gap, Really?

Charter schools received, on average, 30% less in funding than traditional public schools — or $7,147 less per pupil — during the 2019-20 school year, according to a study of charter schools across 18 major cities and 16 states. That figure has held relatively steady across multiple subsequent studies.

The gap is sharpest in urban districts, where most charter schools — and most low-income Black and Brown students — are concentrated. Thirteen of fourteen cities studied received a "C" or lower grade for funding equity; the outlier earning an "A" was Houston.

The root driver is local funding. Researchers have found that the disparity is driven primarily by inequitable local revenues rather than state and federal allocations. Traditional public schools routinely benefit from local property tax levies and capital bond elections that charter schools are legally excluded from accessing.

The Facilities Problem Is the Biggest Piece

Of all the components that make up the funding gap, facilities is where the inequity bites hardest.

Many charter schools receive no facilities funding at all, instead paying for rented space out of per-pupil formula allocations. In plain terms: dollars meant to pay teachers and run programs get redirected to cover rent and building maintenance instead.

In Georgia, advocacy groups warn that many charter schools do not receive local facility tax funding, leaving schools to cover construction and maintenance expenses out of operating budgets — with some schools spending nearly 20% of their budgets on rent, maintenance, and building costs alone. Without additional financial support, some charter schools are forced to delay repairs, scale back programs, or limit teacher pay just to keep the lights on.

The ripple effects are real and predictable: reduced instructional staff, thinner programming, and suppressed teacher compensation all flow directly from facilities underfunding.

---

What's Happening on the Policy Front in 2026

This is not a stale policy debate. Legislatures across the country are actively wrestling with charter funding equity right now — with mixed results.

States making progress: In 2025, states generated $251 million in new funding for charter school facilities, marking what advocates describe as a significant shift toward more equitable funding. Several states extended that momentum into 2026:

  • Florida's state budget (SB 2500) increased charter school capital outlay funding to $248.6 million, up from $230 million, supporting facilities for more than 400,000 charter students.
  • Tennessee established an Equitable Facilities Fund providing revolving loan financing options, backed by $20 million in per-pupil facility aid.
  • Idaho and several other states began actively examining their funding formulas to help charter schools save on facility costs and direct more dollars toward student learning.

Where the fight continues: In Washington, D.C., despite the mayor's budget proposal allocating $1.45 billion to public charter schools, the D.C. Charter School Alliance reports a remaining gap of $2,000 per student between the district and charter sectors — and puts the total resource disparity for the proposed FY27 budget at roughly $9,675 less per child attending a D.C. charter school, even as charter students make up nearly half the city's public school population.

In North Carolina, the Coalition for Charter Schools' 2026 legislative agenda lists "fair funding for charter schools at both the state and local level" as its top priority — including removing caps on funding for students with exceptional needs and securing uniform local funding payments spread across all 12 months of the school year.

---

What This Means for Schools Serving High-Need Communities

The funding gap lands hardest on the schools doing the heaviest lift — networks serving predominantly low-income students and students of color. A $5,000–$9,000 per-pupil shortfall doesn't just affect facility aesthetics; it determines whether a school can staff a social worker, keep class sizes manageable, or sustain after-school programming that keeps families enrolled.

This is why the funding question is inseparable from the equity question. Networks designed to serve Black and Brown families — addressing not just academics but health, housing, and long-term economic mobility — are stretched thinnest by a financing system that was never structured around their success.

BRICK Rochester is one example of a charter network trying to close that gap through model design rather than waiting for policy to catch up. According to its website, BRICK operates K–12 schools paired with comprehensive holistic supports — covering education, health, housing, and career pathways — under a "pre-cradle to career" framework explicitly built for Black and Brown families. Running that kind of integrated model on a per-pupil budget that may be 20–30% below a neighboring district school's is a structural challenge that no amount of mission alone can fully absorb.

---

Practical Strategies for Charter Leaders Facing the Funding Gap

Leaders don't have to wait for Legislatures to act. Several approaches can meaningfully reduce the gap's impact:

1. Quantify your gap in human terms — then make it public. Most school communities, including families, don't know the funding differential exists. Translating the dollar figure into concrete stakes ("that gap is equivalent to one full-time counselor per 80 students") builds the community understanding and political will needed to drive change.

2. Pursue mission-aligned lenders and CDFIs. Charter-focused nonprofit lenders can provide technical and financial support beyond what traditional banks offer, including access to federal tax credit programs — such as the New Market Tax Credit — that help schools leverage long-term facility financing at below-market rates.

3. Engage state-level advocacy coalitions actively. In 2026, momentum is building as more state leaders recognize that a strong public education ecosystem requires fairly funded charter schools — but that momentum depends on sustained, organized advocacy from school operators, not just policy groups.

4. Explore energy and green infrastructure tax credits. Several states offer solar and energy-efficiency incentives that charter schools can use to cut long-term facility operating costs — freeing up per-pupil dollars for instruction and staffing.

5. Align your multi-year budget to your lease and facilities cycle. Matching long-term commitments — leases, facilities debt, managed services — to realistic revenue forecasts reduces the risk of structural deficits that compound quietly year over year.

---

The Bottom Line

What is the charter school funding gap in 2026?

Charter schools currently receive between 20% and 30% less per-pupil funding than traditional public schools in most major U.S. cities, driven primarily by exclusion from local tax revenues and facilities funding. In some cities, the total per-pupil resource disparity exceeds $9,000. In 2026, multiple states are passing or actively debating legislation to close the facilities portion of that gap, but the core operating inequity remains largely unresolved nationwide.

The funding gap is not a footnote — it is the operating reality shaping every hiring decision, program choice, and facilities trade-off a charter school leader makes. Knowing how it works, where it is being challenged, and what levers exist to address it is foundational knowledge for anyone leading or choosing a charter school right now.
