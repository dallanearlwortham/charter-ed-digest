Title IX Compliance: Charter Schools' Hidden Liability

Most Charter Schools Are Behind on Title IX — and the Stakes Are Higher Than Ever

A single unanswered complaint. A coordinator who didn't know their role. A grievance procedure that exists only as a PDF no one has ever used. These are the scenarios that turn a Title IX gap into a federal investigation — or a lawsuit — and they are far more common at charter schools than most leaders realize.

Title IX training in K-12 schools is governed by the 2020 Department of Education regulations (34 CFR Part 106), following the January 2025 federal court vacatur of the 2024 rule. Under the 2020 rule, K-12 schools must respond whenever any employee has notice of sexual harassment. That regulatory whipsaw has left many charter schools — especially lean operations running on small administrative teams — uncertain about exactly what they are required to do. The answer is unambiguous: the 2020 rule is fully in force, and it carries real consequences for every public school that accepts federal funds, including every charter.

What the 2020 Rule Actually Requires of Charter Schools

Is Title IX mandatory for charter schools? Yes, without exception. All districts and charter schools must have a designated Title IX Coordinator with their contact information prominently posted in visible areas of the school, including handbooks, and on the school's website. That requirement is not optional and not contingent on school size, enrollment, or governance model.

Under the operative 2020 framework, the core obligations for K-12 charter schools include four non-negotiables.

First, every school must designate a Title IX Coordinator — at least one employee appointed specifically to coordinate compliance efforts and handle complaints. That person's name, title, office address, and contact information must be publicly accessible, not buried in a staff-only shared drive or printed once in a handbook that families never see.

Second, every staff member must be trained. Under the 2020 rule, all K-12 employees serve as responsible reporters. There is no confidential resource exception and no "it wasn't my job to report" defense. A teacher, a coach, a front-office staffer — if any of them receive notice of sexual harassment, the school's obligation to respond is triggered.

Third, the school must adopt written grievance procedures that actually function in practice. The procedures must cover how reports are received, how investigations are conducted, what evidence standards apply, and how determinations are made and communicated. A generic template that has never been reviewed by school leadership is not a compliant procedure.

Fourth, training materials must be publicly available on the school's website and retained for seven years under 34 CFR 106.45(b)(10)(i)(D). This is one of the most frequently overlooked requirements — and one of the first things OCR asks for.

The Compliance Gaps That Keep Surfacing

OCR compliance reviews and Title IX self-audits keep surfacing the same recurring deficiencies: Title IX Coordinator authority, training documentation, supportive measures, grievance procedures, athletics equity, and retaliation handling. For charter schools specifically, three of these gaps appear with particular frequency.

The first is a coordinator who exists only on paper. Many charter schools do not have a dedicated Title IX Coordinator, even though federal regulations require that all recipients of federal financial aid designate and well-publicize one. At smaller charters, the role gets assigned to an already-stretched assistant principal or HR generalist who has never received role-specific training. Anecdotally, 95 percent of smaller schools lack a true dedicated coordinator. The person named in the policy is also managing federal programs, discipline, athletics, and a dozen other responsibilities — which creates the conditions for missed reports and inadequate responses.

The second gap is undocumented or inaccessible grievance procedures. Schools that schedule interviews informally, by phone or hallway conversation, leave themselves with no record that notice was properly handled. Every step of the process — from initial report through investigation to final determination — must be documented in a way that is defensible if the school is ever asked to demonstrate compliance.

The third gap is training content stuck in the wrong regulatory era. When the 2024 Title IX rule was vacated in January 2025, schools that had updated their policies and training materials to reflect the new framework needed to roll those changes back. Any policy updates made in anticipation of the 2024 rule should be reviewed and, where applicable, reconciled with the 2020 framework that is currently operative. Many schools — particularly those that outsourced training to vendors who proactively updated their content in 2024 — have not completed this reconciliation.

Why the Risk Is Amplified Right Now

The enforcement environment shifted significantly in 2026. The U.S. Department of Education's Office for Civil Rights faces serious challenges processing Title IX violation complaints following the March 2025 closure of seven regional offices and the layoff of nearly half its staff. These changes are expected to severely hinder OCR from investigating and resolving complaints related to sex discrimination under Title IX.

It might be tempting to interpret reduced federal enforcement capacity as lower compliance risk. That reading is wrong. When OCR's reach contracts, state agencies, advocacy organizations, and plaintiff attorneys expand to fill the gap — often more aggressively than OCR ever did. For charter schools, whose authorizer contracts frequently include civil rights compliance as a charter renewal condition, an unresolved complaint does not need to reach a federal finding to become a school-threatening event.

The reduction in OCR staffing signals an important shift toward state-level enforcement. Charter leaders should expect state education agencies to increase their own compliance scrutiny precisely because the federal backstop has weakened. This is not the moment to treat Title IX as a low-priority administrative item.

The Structural Challenge Charter Schools Face

Districts typically have centralized legal counsel, HR departments, and compliance officers whose full-time work includes staying current on civil rights obligations. Single-site charter schools do not have that infrastructure. The compliance burden falls on a school leader already managing enrollment, facilities, instruction, finance, and board relations simultaneously.

This structural gap is real, and it is precisely why state-level charter associations are operationally critical for Title IX compliance — not just as a resource of last resort, but as an ongoing partner. The Colorado League of Charter Schools provides its member schools with a dedicated Title IX training suite and practical guidance on where most charters fall short, built specifically for the charter context. Generic district-focused compliance frameworks often miss key governance dynamics unique to independently operated charter schools — particularly the fact that a charter's Title IX Coordinator reports to a board of directors rather than a district superintendent, which affects how authority, accountability, and complaint escalation actually work in practice.

If your school's Title IX infrastructure was designed by or borrowed from a district, it is worth asking whether it accurately reflects your school's governance structure. Coordinator authority, board notification protocols, and the chain of accountability in a formal complaint all function differently when there is no district layer above the school.

A Practical Action List for Charter Leaders

A meaningful Title IX compliance review does not require outside counsel or a formal audit to get started. Charter leaders can close the most critical gaps by working through five concrete steps.

Confirm that your Title IX Coordinator is designated, trained, and publicly listed — by name and contact information — on the school's current website and in both the student handbook and the staff handbook, not just in an internal policy document.

Review your grievance procedures against the 2020 rule, not the 2024 rule. If your policy was last substantively revised after April 2024, it may need to be reconciled with the 2020 framework that is currently operative.

Document all staff Title IX training — who completed it, when, and which materials were used. This documentation is typically the first thing OCR requests at the outset of any compliance inquiry.

Publish your training materials on your school website and keep those records for a minimum of seven years. This requirement appears simple but shows up as a failure point in compliance reviews with notable regularity.

Brief your board on the school's Title IX obligations, the current status of your compliance infrastructure, and what a functioning grievance process looks like. If board members do not understand what compliant looks like, they cannot hold school leadership accountable for maintaining it — and Title IX is squarely a governance-level responsibility, not just an operational checkbox.

The Trust Dimension

For families choosing a charter school — often specifically because they expect it to be more responsive and accountable than their assigned district school — knowing that the school has clear, functioning processes for protecting students from sex-based harassment and discrimination is part of what makes the choice feel safe and justified.

Getting Title IX right is a signal that your school takes student welfare seriously, documents what it does, and operates with the kind of institutional integrity that earns and sustains family trust. That is not incidental to the charter school mission. In a meaningful sense, it is the charter school mission made visible in day-to-day operations.

The 2020 Title IX Final Rule is the operative regulation as of late 2026. New rulemaking may emerge. But the underlying obligation — protect students, train staff, maintain accountability, document everything — does not shift with any administration or any regulatory cycle. For charter schools, building durable Title IX infrastructure now is both a legal requirement and a declaration of institutional values that families, authorizers, and communities will notice.
