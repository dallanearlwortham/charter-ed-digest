Will Your Charter School Still Be Open in 5 Years?

# Will Your Charter School Still Be Open in 5 Years?

You've toured the campus, reviewed the test scores, and loved the culture. But here's the school-choice question most families never think to ask: Will this school still exist when my child reaches graduation?

In 2026, families making school-choice decisions should also weigh a less obvious but increasingly important factor: how stable is the school itself? Charter schools earn their autonomy by delivering results — but that same autonomy means they can close far more quickly than a traditional district school when finances or enrollment go sideways. For families committing their child's K-12 years to a school, stability isn't a footnote. It's a core criterion.

---

Why Charter School Stability Matters More Than Ever

Chicago's charter schools saw enrollment dip about 4.5% in a recent year, compared to 1.7% at district-run schools — and citywide enrollment losses hit charter budgets hard. When enrollment falls at a charter school, the financial consequences are direct and swift: when a charter school sees a significant enrollment decline, its assets can drop below its liabilities, and it may be unable to cut costs enough to make up the difference — forcing it to close.

Families want to know whether their child's school is performing well, whether it will remain open, and what they should do if it does not. Families who choose a charter school expecting to stay through graduation deserve to know whether that school has the financial and operational foundation to actually be there.

---

What Causes Charter Schools to Close?

Understanding why charter schools close helps families know what to look for. Indicators of distress often start in one core area — governance, operations, or finance — and, like a domino, affect others. Researchers have documented the reasons for charter closure as financial, operational, and/or academic. Common root causes include:

  • Financial mismanagement — operating over budget, missing payroll, or running multi-year deficits
  • Enrollment decline — lower enrollment means less per-pupil funding, triggering a downward spiral
  • Governance breakdown — board members who lack capacity, fail compliance requirements, or clash with the school's authorizer
  • Leadership instability — frequent turnover in principals or executive directors with no succession plan

Red flags show up in the data as multiple years of net losses, where expenditures consistently exceed revenues. Real-world examples are instructive: the Colorado Skies Academy closed its doors just 16 days before the 2026-27 school year was set to begin, blindsiding families and leaving teachers unpaid — with financial instability publicly flagged by its prior authorizer more than a year before the closure.

---

How to Assess a Charter School's Stability Before You Enroll

What does a stable charter school look like? A well-established charter school with consistent enrollment, clean audits, an engaged board, and a productive relationship with its authorizer is very unlikely to close unexpectedly. Here's how to evaluate each dimension:

1. Check Enrollment Trends

Is the school growing, stable, or declining? Ask the admissions office for enrollment figures over the past three to five years. A school with a healthy waitlist and steady or growing enrollment has strong financial footing. Schools that fail to enroll close to their projected student capacity raise serious concerns about financial viability.

2. Look at Financial Transparency Documents

Most states require charter schools to post annual audits, budgets, and financial reports publicly — often on the school's website or through the state education department. Look for:

  • Clean auditor opinions (not "qualified" or "adverse")
  • Positive net assets year over year
  • Reasonable reserve funds
  • No pattern of deficit spending

3. Understand the Authorizer Relationship

Throughout a charter term, authorizers monitor academic performance, financial health, governance, compliance with laws and the charter agreement, and equity. Ask: Who authorizes this school — a local school district, a state charter board, or a university? When was the charter last renewed, and for how long? A recent, multi-year renewal is a strong stability signal.

A deteriorating relationship between the governing board and the authorizer — when that relationship was previously characterized as positive and problem-solving — often emerges as an early indicator of distress.

4. Evaluate the Board's Track Record

Many parents who send their children to public charter schools don't realize that the school has its own board — and that the charter agreement is granted to that board, not to parents or the school leader. Ask whether the board holds regular, open public meetings. A board that is accessible, posts meeting minutes, and maintains a publicly listed membership is operating transparently. A board that is hard to find, rarely convenes, or turns over its membership constantly is a warning sign.

5. Ask About the School's Age and Renewal History

A school that has operated for a decade or more and survived multiple authorizer renewals has a proven track record. Longevity signals that the school has consistently met its academic and financial obligations — the precise conditions authorizers evaluate at renewal time.

---

A Practical Question List for Families

Before enrolling, ask the school directly:

  • How many students are currently enrolled, and how has that changed over the past five years?
  • When is the charter next up for renewal, and when was it last renewed?
  • Are your annual financial audits posted publicly? May I see the most recent one?
  • Who is your authorizing agency, and how would you describe your relationship with them?
  • How long has your current leadership team been in place?

The answers — and the confidence with which school staff provide them — tell you a great deal about institutional health.

---

Stability as a Differentiator in School Choice

A school's longevity isn't separate from its quality. Schools that remain financially healthy, grow their enrollment, and maintain strong authorizer relationships year after year are, almost by definition, doing something right. That track record deserves real weight in your decision.

The Vanguard School, a tuition-free K-12 charter school in Colorado Springs, Colorado, is a concrete example of this kind of institutional staying power — its website notes the school is celebrating 30 years of excellence, a milestone that reflects the sustained community trust and operational consistency that families evaluating long-term school choice should look for.

---

The Bottom Line

Can you predict whether a charter school will close? Not with certainty — but you can dramatically reduce the risk by checking enrollment trends, financial audits, board meeting cadence, authorizer renewal history, and leadership continuity before you commit. The best charter schools are transparent about all of these things, because they have nothing to hide and everything to prove.

Choosing a school is an investment in your child's future. Make sure the school is investing just as seriously in its own.
