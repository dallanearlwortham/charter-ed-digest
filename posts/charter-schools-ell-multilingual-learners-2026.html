Charter Schools & ELL Students: What 2026 Research Shows

# Charter Schools and English Language Learners: What 2026 Research Actually Shows

More than 5.3 million students in U.S. K-12 public schools are classified as English learners—nearly 11% of all K-12 public school enrollment, a figure that has grown 15% over the past decade. These students speak over 280 languages, come from vastly different socioeconomic and educational backgrounds, and land in schools that are frequently under-resourced to meet their needs. For multilingual families navigating school choice, the question isn't just which school—it's which model is structurally built to help a multilingual child thrive.

The research coming out of 2026 offers some pointed answers.

---

The Scale of the Challenge

English learner enrollment isn't a coastal-city phenomenon anymore. Growth is occurring not only in traditional immigrant-arrival states such as California, Texas, New York, Florida, and Illinois, but also in less-traditional immigrant gateway states such as Alabama, Georgia, and the Carolinas.

In Oregon, the numbers illustrate the trend sharply. The number of current English learners increased to 64,525 students in 2024-25—a 3.2% increase over the prior year—and Oregon's K-12 students holding federal English learner status grew to 12.6% of all enrollment. That is roughly one in eight Oregon students who needs language support beyond what a standard classroom was designed to deliver.

The stakes are concrete. Researchers estimate that without meaningful services or supports, today's English learners could lose the equivalent of $49,000 in lifetime earnings due to unaddressed learning gaps. That figure alone should focus the minds of every charter school leader thinking about whether ELL programming is a "nice to have" or a core school-design question.

---

What Charter School Flexibility Actually Delivers for ELLs

Does the charter model produce measurably better outcomes for English learners? A 2026 national analysis published in the Journal of School Choice found that charter schools show higher average ELL performance in English language arts and mathematics compared to traditional public schools, though differences vary by state and subject. A separate analysis from The Century Foundation reported that ELs in charter schools had stronger academic gains in reading and math than their peers in district-assigned schools.

The long-term picture is even more striking. Research on English learners in Texas charter schools found that ELs enrolled in a charter school in eighth or tenth grade were four to five percentage points more likely to graduate high school than their traditional public school peers—and charter school enrollment boosted the probability that English learners attended college by more than twenty-five percentage points.

Why does the charter model move the needle? The tailored instruction, flexible scheduling, and intensive language support systems common in charters allow ELL students to progress more rapidly than the rigid structures of traditional schools permit. Those three features—individualized pacing, flexible scheduling, and dedicated language support—are not incidental to the charter model. For the best-designed charter schools, they are the model.

---

The Online School Advantage: Pacing Without Penalty

One dimension that gets overlooked in ELL school-choice conversations is the particular fit between fully online charter schools and multilingual families. Traditional classrooms run on a fixed clock. A student who needs an extra twenty minutes to process a concept in a second language does not get it—the class moves on. An online learning environment changes that dynamic structurally, not just philosophically.

With a flexible schedule, families and students can revisit instruction, slow down on dense vocabulary, and move forward without treating language development as a reason to repeat an entire course. That matters enormously for ELL students, who often grasp the underlying content—they just need adequate space to demonstrate it in English.

Online charter schools also reduce a barrier that rarely gets named directly: the social anxiety of making language errors in front of classmates. For many English learners, especially adolescents, the fear of public mistakes suppresses participation and measurably slows acquisition. Asynchronous and hybrid online models lower that barrier without removing human interaction.

Oregon Charter Academy (SYS Education) is one Oregon-based example worth noting here. The school is a fully online, tuition-free K-12 public charter that serves students statewide, and it explicitly offers English Learner Services as a named component of its curriculum—alongside a 1:1 Chromebook provided to every enrolled student at no cost, ensuring that device access doesn't become its own obstacle to language learning.

---

The Dual Language Evidence: Stronger Than the Debate Suggests

Dual language immersion (DLI) programs—which teach academic content through two languages simultaneously—have become one of the most debated models in multilingual education. The evidence gathered through 2026 is clarifying in ways that should inform charter design decisions.

A RAND study followed 1,625 Portland applicants whose program admission was determined by lottery. Students who won a seat outperformed their peers on state reading tests by 13% of a standard deviation in grade 5 and 22% in grade 8—roughly seven to nine additional months of learning. English learners assigned to immersion were 3 percentage points more likely to reach English proficiency by grade 6, rising to 14 percentage points when their home language matched the classroom partner language.

For ELL families weighing school options, those are data points worth carrying into enrollment conversations—and for charter leaders, they are design signals worth taking seriously before the next school opens.

---

The Persistent Gaps: What Charter Schools Still Need to Fix

Honest reporting demands acknowledging where the charter sector still falls short for ELL students. Nationwide shortages in bilingual education, TESOL, and dual-certification credentials mean that schools with the best intentions can still fail multilingual learners when they cannot staff a qualified ESL teacher across every grade band. Access to high-quality EL programming—including dual language programs—remains uneven, and students in high-need communities are often the least likely to benefit.

The deeper structural problem involves long-term English learners. For too many students, proficiency plateaus before they ever reach the academic language demands of secondary school content. This is the ELL crisis inside the ELL crisis: students who pass elementary language benchmarks, exit their services, and then stall at the complex academic vocabulary of middle and high school. A 2024 UChicago Consortium study found that while students who had exited EL status by ninth grade performed above district averages on GPAs and SAT scores, long-term English learners showed scores and persistence rates below the district average. Exiting a program is not the same thing as being academically ready.

Charter authorizers and school leaders need to track outcomes well beyond the "exited EL services" milestone—because the story doesn't end there, and neither does the school's responsibility.

---

Questions Multilingual Families Should Ask Any Charter School

Before enrolling an ELL student, families deserve direct answers to the following:

  • What EL identification and monitoring system does the school use? Ask specifically about WIDA or a comparable framework.
  • What happens after a student exits EL services? Are former ELs still tracked academically and supported as needed?
  • Is there a certified ESL or TESOL teacher on staff—not just a general education teacher with a brief add-on endorsement?
  • Can instruction be paced to the student's language development, rather than the grade-level calendar alone?
  • Are family communications available in the home language?
  • For online schools specifically: How does live language support work, and what does asynchronous instruction look like for a student who is still building English fluency?

---

What the Evidence Points Toward

The structural flexibility of charter schools—individualized pacing, targeted support systems, freedom from rigid grade-level time constraints—is precisely the architecture that multilingual learners need to close gaps and build durable academic language. The 2026 research doesn't declare the charter sector uniformly excellent for ELL students. It shows that where charters deploy their flexibility intentionally in service of multilingual learners, the outcomes are meaningfully better. Where they don't, the flexibility goes to waste.

For families, the best charter school is not the one with the longest list of programs on its enrollment page. It is the one that tracks ELL outcomes after students exit services, employs certified language professionals, and treats flexible scheduling as a tool for equity rather than a marketing bullet point.
