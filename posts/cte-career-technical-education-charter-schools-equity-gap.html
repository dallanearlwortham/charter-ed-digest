CTE in Charter Schools: Closing the Equity Gap

# CTE in Charter Schools: Why the Equity Gap Is Real—and What Leaders Can Do About It

Career and technical education has never been more relevant. With AI reshaping entry-level work, student loan anxiety at a peak, and employers loudly signaling that they'll hire credentials over degrees, CTE programs are suddenly the most talked-about reform in K–12. But for the urban charter schools serving the students who most need these pathways, CTE remains stubbornly out of reach—underfunded, under-built, and, in many cases, misunderstood as a detour away from college rather than a road toward it.

That's a problem charter leaders can't afford to ignore heading into 2026-27.

What the Research Actually Shows About CTE

The evidence for high-quality CTE is strong and getting stronger. Research shows that CTE programs improve employment opportunities and that students who participate can increase their earnings after graduation. More than half of CTE completers go on to earn a college degree—demonstrating that career-focused education is a pathway to both higher education and employment, not a substitute for either.

CTE participants are also more likely to take AP and STEM courses in high school and less likely to drop out than peers who don't take CTE courses.

The employer signal is equally clear. A national survey of more than 300 employers found that 77 percent had hired someone in part because of their CTE experience, while more than 90 percent supported increased public investment in CTE programs. And interest from students is surging: Department of Education data show a 10 percent increase in enrollment for K–12 CTE programs between the 2022–23 and 2023–24 school years.

Six CTE trends to watch in 2026 include AI literacy becoming a baseline skill requirement for entry-level jobs, CTE programs aligning more tightly with national reindustrialization priorities, and a sharp rise in dual enrollment as students pursue industry credentials alongside college credits. States and employers are increasingly integrating CTE into broader workforce and talent development strategies—making it less of a school program and more of an economic infrastructure investment.

The Equity Gap Charter Leaders Must Confront

Here is the uncomfortable part: most of this CTE momentum is flowing past the schools serving low-income urban students.

A 2025 peer-reviewed study published in AERA Open found that income inequality between school districts significantly influences which students can access and participate in CTE programs—with students in lower-income districts substantially less likely to participate. The study highlights how existing structural inequalities shape not just who enrolls in CTE, but which types of programs are even available to them.

The funding structure makes this worse. In Ohio—one of the states investing most aggressively in CTE expansion—urban districts and charter schools received less than 4 percent of the funding the state made available in 2024 and 2025 for expanding CTE offerings. Urban schools also received less than a tenth of the state's incentive funding for enrolling CTE students in high-priority fields.

At the federal level, the Strengthening Career and Technical Education for the 21st Century Act (Perkins V), the primary law for funding CTE in the United States, has not been reauthorized by Congress since 2018. Existing funding has been eroded by inflation and population growth, leaving a more than $230 million gap between what was appropriated in 2024 and what is needed to simply keep pace.

Access barriers compound the funding problem. Some students face challenges reaching CTE programs due to scheduling conflicts, a lack of transportation, and child care responsibilities—factors that can shut out certain students, lower program completion rates, and deepen equity gaps further.

What does this mean for charter schools specifically? It means that the "college-for-all" mission most urban charters were built around and the CTE opportunity aren't mutually exclusive—but they do require deliberate design to coexist.

What a Strong CTE-Plus-College Model Looks Like

The good news is that CTE done well doesn't compete with college readiness. It reinforces it. Here is what research and practitioner experience point to as the core elements of effective implementation.

1. Structured Career Pathways, Not Elective Add-Ons

Effective CTE programs offer structured sequences of courses tailored to a student's career interests—bridging high school coursework, college credits, industry certifications, and career opportunities into a coherent arc. The key word is sequence. A single elective in graphic design isn't CTE. A three-year pathway with industry mentors, stackable credentials, and a senior capstone is.

Charter schools that begin pathway planning in 8th or 9th grade—mapping out how coursework connects to a credential and a career cluster—give students and families a compelling reason to stay enrolled and stay engaged through graduation.

2. Work-Based Learning as the Connective Tissue

Growing evidence shows that CTE functions as an early talent pipeline for employers, not just a preparation program. Comprehensive job shadowing, worksite tours, and internships help students build skills, social capital, and informed career choices. For charter schools in urban centers—surrounded by hospitals, tech firms, financial institutions, and small businesses—this is a real geographic advantage waiting to be activated through intentional employer outreach.

Schools should map the employer landscape within a realistic commute of their campus before designing pathways. The strongest CTE programs align their offerings to where local hiring actually happens.

3. Industry Credentials That Signal Real Value

Credentials matter when employers and colleges recognize them. Charter leaders should vet the credential market carefully and prioritize certifications in high-demand sectors: health care, IT, advanced manufacturing, logistics, and financial services. Some of the most practical credentials—CompTIA certifications, OSHA safety cards, Google IT Support Professional Certificates, CNA prep—can be earned without expensive lab infrastructure, making them genuinely accessible for schools with tight capital budgets.

CTE offers students the opportunity to earn industry-recognized credentials and develop skills that can lead directly to employment, apprenticeships, or further education after graduation. That on-ramp to multiple futures is exactly the value proposition families in under-resourced communities deserve.

4. Integrating CTE With College Counseling, Not Sidelining It

Noble - Baker College Prep, part of Noble Schools' 17-campus Chicago network serving grades 6–12, has built its identity around college access—with the network's stated mission being to equip every student to complete college and lead choice-filled lives. That mission is entirely compatible with CTE. Research showing that more than half of CTE completers go on to earn college degrees makes the case that career-connected learning and college aspiration reinforce each other rather than compete. The challenge for college-focused charters is to expand the definition of "college and career ready" without diluting either commitment—and that starts with how counselors frame pathways to students and families from day one.

5. Building the CTE Educator Pipeline

Today, 1 in 8 teaching positions nationally is unfilled or filled by an uncertified professional. CTE roles are among the hardest to fill: 32 states and Washington, D.C., reported a shortage of CTE educators in recent years, and nearly 1 in 3 public schools hiring for a CTE teaching role struggled to fill that vacancy.

Charter schools have more flexibility than district schools to hire industry professionals as instructors—especially in states with alternative licensure pathways. A working IT professional who teaches one section of cybersecurity, or a licensed nurse who leads health sciences, brings credibility no textbook can replicate. Building hybrid educator pipelines takes investment and creativity, but it is one of the clearest structural advantages the charter sector holds over traditional public schools.

Practical First Steps for Charter Leaders

If your school hasn't launched a formal CTE program, here is where to begin:

  • Audit your current course catalog for career-connected content that could be sequenced into a formal pathway
  • Survey your families and alumni to understand which career sectors resonate most in your community
  • Map your neighborhood's employer landscape to identify realistic internship and advisory board partners
  • Research your state's Perkins V allocation process — many urban charter schools leave federal CTE dollars on the table simply because they haven't applied
  • Explore regional CTE consortia — some states allow charter schools to access CTE courses through inter-district agreements
  • Identify one industry credential in a high-demand field that your students could realistically earn within the next school year, then build backward from there

The Bottom Line

Is CTE worth it for urban charter schools? The research says yes—decisively. Students in quality CTE programs graduate at higher rates, earn more after graduation, and are just as likely to pursue college as non-CTE peers. The equity gap in CTE access isn't evidence that career-connected learning doesn't work for low-income students. It's evidence that the systems designed to fund and deliver CTE haven't worked for those schools yet.

Charter leaders have the flexibility, the community relationships, and the urgency to change that from the inside. The most important move isn't waiting for Perkins V reauthorization or a state grant—it's deciding that your students deserve these pathways now, and building toward them one structured sequence at a time.
