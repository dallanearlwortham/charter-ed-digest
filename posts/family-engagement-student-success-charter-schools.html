Why Family Engagement Is the Hidden Engine of Student Success

# Why Family Engagement Is the Hidden Engine of Student Success

Five years out from the pandemic, school leaders are still chasing recovery on test scores, attendance, and student behavior. Most conversations focus on instructional minutes, tutoring programs, and curriculum quality — all legitimate levers. But a growing body of 2024–2025 research keeps pointing to a factor that gets far less airtime in policy debates: what families do, and whether schools are genuinely designed to bring them in as partners.

The evidence is harder to ignore than ever.

What the Research Actually Shows

Greater parental involvement is consistently associated with higher academic achievement, increased motivation, and better social-emotional outcomes. That much has been true for decades. What is newer is precision around which kinds of involvement move the needle most — and which do not.

School-based involvement, such as participation in parent-teacher conferences, open houses, and other school events, has a positive impact on academics in preschool, middle school, and high school, but the size of the impact is much lower in high school than in the early grades. That finding matters enormously for elementary school leaders: the window when school-based family engagement yields its greatest academic return is precisely the K-6 years.

At-home discussions and encouragement surrounding school also have a positive impact on students' academic achievement at all developmental stages. And one common assumption does not hold up under scrutiny: helping kids with their homework was shown to have little impact on students' academic achievement, though it does have positive effects on student motivation and engagement in school.

The behavioral data is striking, too. Schools reporting high levels of parent engagement see a 35 percent drop in disciplinary incidents, and 78 percent of teachers say parental support improves classroom behavior.

The Post-Pandemic Trust Deficit

The pandemic did not just disrupt learning — it frayed the relationship between many families and their schools. Almost 1 in 5 school communicators reported a lack of trust in district communications among families in 2024. Meanwhile, in late 2024, 72 percent of surveyed teachers, principals, and district leaders reported that student behavior was worse than it had been in 2019 — a higher percentage than in 2021 and 2023.

These two trends are connected. When families do not trust or feel connected to a school, the informal accountability that community partnership creates — the expectation that school norms matter, that teachers and parents are on the same team — erodes. Rebuilding it requires more than a back-to-school night. Schools that embed strong family engagement as a core strategy are better positioned to respond to post-pandemic learning gaps and evolving student needs.

What "Embedded" Family Engagement Actually Looks Like

There is a meaningful difference between schools that invite parent involvement and schools that architect it into their daily operations. Research shows that schools must intentionally and consistently design family engagement activities, events, and communication that empower families to communicate high expectations for learning, monitor learning progress, support learning at home, guide their child's educational path, and advocate for their child's needs.

That is a demanding list. It implies that family engagement is not a program you bolt onto a school — it is a design choice baked into culture and calendar from day one.

Teachers are the primary bridge between families and schools, yet many educators feel underprepared to navigate the complexities of partnering with families. A 2025 WestEd analysis identified the most common pain points: finding time to communicate with families, engaging multilingual families, having crucial conversations, explaining report cards versus actual performance, and reaching families who work during the day. Schools that address these barriers structurally — through dedicated planning time, professional learning, and two-way communication systems — see the most durable gains.

The federal government's own 2024 Charter Schools Program Impact Report underscores this point: many charter schools make sure to create opportunities for parents to engage in school regularly and help connect their families to critical community services, with some running parent advisory committees and regularly surveying families to understand what they need.

Why Charter Schools Have a Structural Advantage Here

Does the school model matter for family engagement? Yes — and the mechanism is straightforward. Charter schools are schools of choice. Families opt in, which means they arrive with a baseline level of investment. But more importantly, the best charter schools use that voluntary relationship intentionally, designing their models around ongoing family partnership rather than treating it as a bonus feature.

With close ties to local communities, charter schools are well-positioned to engage and collaborate with families and tailor activities and decisions based on direct feedback. That community embeddedness is a genuine structural asset — one that large, geographically assigned district schools often cannot replicate in the same way.

Jefferson Academy, a tuition-free public charter school serving grades K-6 in Kaysville, Utah, makes the family-community partnership explicit at the level of its founding mission: "Engaging all students in partnership with family and community to become informed, compassionate, global citizens." The school maintains dedicated parent involvement programming, student-led parent-teacher conferences, a volunteer program, and a Military Child Support Program — structures that treat families as active participants in the educational process, not just recipients of report cards. That kind of institutional intentionality is precisely what the research says separates high-engagement schools from low-engagement ones.

What Families Should Look for When Choosing a School

For parents evaluating schools — charter, district, magnet, or otherwise — family engagement infrastructure is worth scrutinizing as carefully as test scores or curriculum. A few questions worth asking:

  • Does the school use student-led or two-way conferences, or one-directional updates from teachers?
  • Is parent volunteering organized and encouraged, or treated as an afterthought?
  • Does the school solicit family feedback and visibly act on what it hears?
  • Are family engagement events tied to student learning, or mostly social occasions?
  • Does school leadership communicate proactively, or only when something goes wrong?

Research consistently shows that active parental involvement correlates with higher academic performance, improved behavior, and a more positive attitude toward school among students. That correlation holds across income levels and racial demographics — making family engagement one of the most equitable levers available to any school.

The Equity Dimension No One Should Skip

One caution the 2025 research surfaces: engagement gaps are real. A 2025 paper on school-based parental involvement found that parents with higher education levels or greater financial resources tend to be more engaged — highlighting equity gaps in access to meaningful participation. Schools that design engagement around the default — parents who can attend daytime events, who are already comfortable navigating institutional settings — will inadvertently reinforce those gaps.

Parent involvement is often considered a pathway through which schools enhance the achievement of underperforming children. That is the real promise: done well, family engagement does not just benefit already-advantaged kids — it is one of the strongest tools schools have for narrowing gaps between student groups. The benefits cut across demographic lines, helping students regardless of background when schools put genuine structures in place to support it.

The best family engagement models meet families where they are: linguistically, logistically, and culturally. That means flexible meeting times, multilingual outreach, home-learning resources, and communication tools families actually use — not just open houses that work for families who already feel at home in school buildings.

---

The schools making the most meaningful post-pandemic recoveries are not doing it by luck. They have recognized that the classroom is only part of the equation — the families behind those students are either partners in the work or they are not. Building the structures that make that partnership real, consistent, and accessible to all families is among the highest-leverage investments a school leader can make right now.
