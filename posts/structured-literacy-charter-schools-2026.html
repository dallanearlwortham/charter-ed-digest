Structured Literacy: What Charter Schools Must Get Right in 2026

# Structured Literacy: What Charter Schools Must Get Right in 2026

America's reading crisis did not appear overnight — but in 2026, the evidence of its depth is impossible to ignore. According to the National Assessment of Educational Progress, U.S. students' reading test scores have continued their downward slide, a trend that began before the pandemic and has yet to reverse. For families comparing schools and for charter leaders evaluating curriculum, understanding where your school stands on the science of reading may be the single most consequential academic question of this decade.

What the Science of Reading Actually Is — and Why It Matters Now

The science of reading is not a single curriculum or a passing trend. It is a vast body of research drawn from cognitive science, linguistics, and neuroscience that shows reading must be taught explicitly and systematically. Its practical classroom application is called structured literacy: direct, sequenced instruction in phonemic awareness, phonics, vocabulary, fluency, and comprehension — in direct contrast to balanced literacy, which blends elements of whole language learning with only incidental phonics instruction.

The evidence strongly favors structured literacy. Research indicates that only about 35% of U.S. students are currently reading proficiently, yet studies suggest that 95% of learners can reach proficiency when instruction is genuinely grounded in the science of reading. Structured literacy approaches tend to yield larger positive effects on student learning than balanced literacy approaches — a gap that has quietly widened over decades while many schools remained committed to methods the research had already left behind.

For many families, this is new information. Reading instruction is one of those areas where marketing language — "we meet every child where they are," "we use a balanced approach" — has long substituted for specific, verifiable answers about what actually happens in classrooms.

Why Charter Schools Have a Structural Advantage

Traditional public schools face a genuinely difficult transition to structured literacy. District-wide curriculum adoption, negotiated professional development calendars, budget cycles, and multi-year approval pipelines all slow implementation — sometimes by years. Charter schools operate on a fundamentally different model.

Charter schools hold independent authority over curriculum design, instructional strategy, staffing, and resource allocation. That means a charter board that is serious about structured literacy can vote to adopt a high-quality, evidence-based reading program and begin intensive teacher training within a single school year — no waiting on a district curriculum committee, no multi-year adoption pipeline, no competing priorities set by a central office serving hundreds of schools at once.

This flexibility is one of the most significant structural advantages charter schools hold. And in 2026, with state legislatures across the country mandating evidence-based literacy instruction and pushing schools to abandon balanced literacy frameworks, charter schools that have already made the transition are well-positioned to demonstrate exactly why that autonomy matters.

Ohio's 2026 Science of Reading Report illustrates what committed implementation looks like at scale: the state's Science of Reading Champions program expanded significantly this year, recognizing 109 schools across all 16 regions for exemplary implementation of evidence-based literacy practices. Schools that move purposefully — rather than waiting for a mandate to force their hand — are the ones producing measurable gains.

The High School Blind Spot Every 9th-Grade Charter Must Address

Most of the structured literacy conversation in 2026 centers on early elementary, and for good reason: foundational decoding is built in the primary grades. But there is a critical and under-discussed blind spot — adolescent students who passed through the balanced literacy era and arrived at middle or high school still unable to read fluently.

Most reading interventions are designed for elementary-age students, leaving few structured literacy options available to older learners and even fewer that can accelerate progress fast enough to close the gap before graduation. This is a problem every 9th-grade charter school must confront directly, because students who cannot read proficiently by the time they enter high school face compounding disadvantages across every subject — history, science, math word problems, and career and technical coursework all demand strong reading ability.

A randomized controlled trial of a structured literacy intervention in Chicago public high schools found that the program increased reading scores by 0.20 standard deviations across more than 2,700 students — a meaningful effect for learners who had already spent years in an inadequate instructional environment. A newer adolescent literacy program called LIFT (Literacy Intervention for Teens) showed even sharper early results: in intensive intervention pilots, 58% of students improved their reading level by more than half a school year, and 45% accelerated by one or more full grade levels.

This is precisely why the choice of a high school charter deserves as much literacy scrutiny as the choice of an elementary school. Career Path High, a tuition-free charter school serving grades 9–12 in Kaysville, Utah, is a clear example of the kind of focused high school environment where these questions about incoming reading levels and foundational skill gaps have direct stakes — because a student who cannot fluently decode text in 9th grade is a student whose career and college readiness goals are already at risk.

What Families Should Ask When Evaluating Any School's Literacy Approach

Here is a direct answer to a question many families are searching right now: How do I know if my child's school is using evidence-based reading instruction?

Ask these specific questions — at any school, charter or otherwise:

  • What core reading curriculum do you use, and is it on your state's approved structured literacy or high-quality instructional materials (HQIM) list?
  • How is phonics instruction sequenced across grade levels? Is it explicit and systematic, or embedded and incidental?
  • What universal screening tool do you use — such as DIBELS, AIMSweb, or mCLASS — and how often do you screen every student?
  • What does reading intervention look like for a student identified as behind grade level in decoding, and how quickly does it begin?
  • For high schools specifically: Do you screen incoming 9th graders for reading deficits, or assume literacy was handled in elementary school?

A school that cannot answer these questions with specifics — one that defaults to phrases like "we use a research-based approach" without naming the curriculum or the screening tool — deserves more scrutiny before you enroll.

The 2026 Mandate for Charter Leaders

In 2026, the case for delay has evaporated. State mandates are catching up, national reading scores continue to fall, and families are increasingly informed. The charter schools that will differentiate themselves on academic outcomes over the next five years will be the ones that treated structured literacy as a non-negotiable curriculum priority — not a pilot, not a supplement, and not something to revisit during the next strategic planning cycle.

Literacy is the foundation. Every content area, every career pathway, and every post-secondary outcome your school promises ultimately depends on whether students can read proficiently. Charter leaders who are honest with themselves — and with the families they serve — know that good intentions and a mission statement are not enough. The science has been clear for decades. The only remaining question is whether your school has acted on it.
