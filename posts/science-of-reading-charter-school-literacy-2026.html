The Reading Crisis Charter Schools Are Solving

# The Reading Crisis Charter Schools Are Solving

More than 40 percent of American fourth graders cannot read at even a basic level. That is not a prediction or a fear — it is the finding from the 2024 National Assessment of Educational Progress (NAEP), and it landed like a fire alarm across K–12 education. Students scoring below the NAEP Basic threshold likely cannot identify the main idea of a simple passage. They are not merely behind. They are in crisis.

For charter school leaders, this data is both a moral call and a strategic one. The families you serve are watching. And in 2026, a growing number of them are choosing schools — or leaving them — based specifically on how a school teaches reading.

What the NAEP Numbers Actually Mean

The 2024 NAEP results confirmed a long-term stagnation in literacy that goes well beyond pandemic disruption. Reading proficiency improved slightly from 1992 to 2013, peaking at 35 percent for fourth graders — but by 2024, scores had regressed to early 2000s levels, with just 31 percent of fourth graders and 30 percent of eighth graders performing at or above the proficient level in reading. No state saw gains in NAEP reading at either grade compared to 2022.

Critically, experts note this is "a major concern that can't be blamed solely on the pandemic." The problem is structural and predates COVID-19 by decades. That structural problem has a name: the way most American schools have taught reading for a generation simply does not work for a large share of students. Balanced literacy — a philosophy that de-emphasized explicit phonics instruction and trusted children to absorb reading patterns through exposure to text — dominated classrooms nationwide. The science says otherwise, and states are finally catching up.

What the Science of Reading Actually Is

The Science of Reading (SoR) is not a single program or a branded product. It is a body of converging research — from cognitive science, linguistics, and neuroscience — on how the human brain learns to decode written language. Schools implementing it rely on five core components: phonemic awareness, phonics, vocabulary, fluency, and comprehension, delivered in a structured, explicit, and cumulative sequence. This practical classroom application is called Structured Literacy.

The evidence base is compelling: research shows that 95 percent of learners can achieve reading proficiency when given instruction grounded in the Science of Reading. That figure reframes the entire conversation. The reading crisis is not primarily a poverty problem or a parenting problem or a post-pandemic hangover. It is, in large part, a solvable instructional problem — and that means the schools willing to change how they teach reading have an enormous opportunity to move the needle.

Why Charter Schools Are Built for This Shift

Charter school autonomy is a genuine operational edge when it comes to curriculum adoption and instructional change. District schools frequently face multi-year procurement cycles, constraints on instructional methods embedded in collective bargaining agreements, and layered bureaucratic approval chains. A charter school can evaluate the evidence, choose a high-quality structured literacy curriculum, train its teachers, and begin implementation within a single school year.

That agility matters because Structured Literacy instruction is inherently suited to the diverse learners charter schools were designed to serve. Students who benefit most from explicit, systematic phonics instruction are disproportionately low-income students, English language learners, and students with dyslexia or related language-based learning differences — exactly the populations charter schools have historically committed to reaching.

In 2026, Lexia hosted a dedicated Charter School Science of Reading Summit, bringing together charter leaders to share evidence-based approaches to literacy instruction and practical implementation strategies. The existence of a summit specifically targeting charter school leaders signals that the sector is treating early literacy as both a student outcome imperative and a defining school identity question — not a compliance checkbox.

Schools like Moreno Valley High School in Angel Fire, New Mexico — a 9th–12th grade charter that has earned consistent placement on the U.S. News & World Report, Newsweek, and Washington Post Challenge Index national rankings — demonstrate what's possible when a small, mission-driven charter commits fully to academic outcomes. Students who arrive at high school as confident, proficient readers are students who can access rigorous coursework, engage with complex texts, and complete the college-preparatory sequence that leads to real post-secondary options. The culture of academic achievement that earns national recognition at the secondary level is built, necessarily, on a strong foundation of early literacy.

States Are Mandating It — Charters Can Get There First

State policy is now accelerating what the research has long supported. Michigan Governor Gretchen Whitmer signed Public Act 26 of 2026, requiring that by the 2031-32 school year, certified teachers in local school districts, intermediate school districts, and charter schools complete common Science of Reading training for K–5 instruction. Ohio's Science of Reading Champions program expanded significantly in 2026, recognizing 109 schools across all 16 regions for exemplary implementation of evidence-based literacy practices — with charter schools among the honorees.

For charter leaders, the mandate is coming regardless of readiness. The schools that act before the deadline — adopting strong curriculum, investing in teacher training, and building screening and intervention systems — are the ones that will develop reputations as the places families seek out when a child is struggling to read.

What Families Should Ask When Evaluating a Charter's Literacy Approach

How can a family tell whether a charter school is genuinely using the Science of Reading? Most schools are not used to being asked directly, which makes the question itself a useful filter. Here is a practical checklist for school visits and information nights:

  • Ask what curriculum they use for reading instruction. A school with a real SoR commitment should name a specific structured literacy program — not describe a "balanced approach" or a reassuring "variety of methods."
  • Ask how they screen for reading difficulties. Evidence-based schools assess all students for foundational skill gaps and dyslexia risk at the start of each year — not only after a child has visibly struggled for months.
  • Ask what happens when a student falls behind in reading. Look for a specific intervention protocol: structured small-group instruction, regular progress monitoring, and a clear threshold at which further evaluation is triggered.
  • Ask about teacher training. LETRS — Language Essentials for Teachers of Reading and Spelling — is one of the most respected SoR professional development programs available. Schools that have invested in it are signaling institutional commitment, not just a curriculum swap.

A school that answers these questions with specifics has done the work. One that responds with generalities likely has not.

The Literacy Gap Is an Enrollment Signal Too

Charter leaders sometimes underestimate how urgently the reading crisis is driving family decisions right now. Parents of children who are struggling to decode text are not passive — they search, they ask neighbors, they visit campuses. When a charter school can demonstrate with curriculum specifics, screening data, and a clear intervention track record that it has a credible, evidence-based answer to the reading problem, that school becomes a destination rather than an option on a list.

The reading crisis is real. The science to address it has been settled for years. Charter schools that move with urgency — adopting strong curriculum, training teachers deeply, and communicating that commitment clearly to families — will find that literacy is not just a program. It is a differentiator, a promise, and ultimately the most powerful enrollment story a school can tell.
