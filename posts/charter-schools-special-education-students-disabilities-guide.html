Charter Schools & Special Ed: What the Evidence Shows

# Charter Schools and Special Education: What New Research Reveals — and What Families Should Ask

For families raising a child with a disability, choosing a school is one of the highest-stakes decisions they'll ever make. Charter schools can feel like an uncertain option: Will they honor my child's IEP? Do they have qualified special education staff? Will my child be welcomed — or quietly pushed out?

Those are exactly the right questions. And in 2026, the research is clearer than it has ever been. The picture is more nuanced than critics claim and more promising than many families realize.

The Enrollment Gap Is Real — and Context Matters

Roughly 400,000 children with disabilities currently attend charter schools nationwide. Yet students with disabilities still enroll in charters at a lower rate than in traditional public schools — approximately 11.5% of charter enrollment compared to 14.1% in traditional public schools, according to the Center for Learner Equity's analysis of federal civil rights data.

Critics have long pointed to that gap as evidence that charters screen out or push out students with disabilities to protect academic metrics and per-pupil budgets. But the gap alone doesn't tell the full story. Research from the National Council on Disability found that parents of students with disabilities are simply less likely to apply to charter schools — often because they have less information about their rights and options, not because charters are turning families away at the door.

Legally, charter schools cannot look at an application, see an IEP or a 504 plan, and choose not to enroll that child. They also cannot engage in "counseling out" — discouraging a family from enrolling to avoid serving a child with higher needs. As public schools, charters are bound by the same federal disability law as any district school.

What a Major 2026 Study Actually Found

The most significant recent research on this question was published in January 2026 by the National Bureau of Economic Research. The study analyzed records from more than 1.7 million Michigan K-8 students who switched from a district school to a charter school between 2013 and 2018. Its central finding: students with disabilities who enrolled in charter schools saw their standardized test scores improve alongside those of their general-education peers — even though they spent more time in general-education classrooms and received less intensive special education services than they had in traditional public schools.

The academic gains were meaningful. By the third year after enrollment, achievement gains reached approximately 0.2 standard deviations in both math and reading. Student attendance improved as well, with absence rates falling by 3.3 percentage points in the first year after charter enrollment and continuing to improve modestly after that.

On the question of services: after switching to a charter school, students were more likely to participate in resource programs — spending most of their school day in general education classrooms and receiving targeted instruction in a resource room for part of the day — while participation in more intensive, costlier cognitive programs declined. The researchers concluded that charter schools tend to deliver IEP-based services in more inclusive settings, without sacrificing academic outcomes.

One of the study's authors put it plainly: "Charter schools can be a useful educational tool for parents with students with disabilities. They shouldn't be quick to rule it out, because it does seem that, for at least a substantial segment of disabled students, charter schools are helpful for them — at least for their academic performance."

A similar earlier study in Boston found that children with disabilities who were admitted to a charter school through a lottery were more likely to meet college-ready benchmarks than special education students in traditional public schools — adding further weight to the pattern.

Do Charter Schools Have to Follow IDEA and Section 504?

The short answer: yes, fully. Public charter schools are public schools and must comply with the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA), Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act, and the Americans with Disabilities Act. That means a charter school must:

  • Identify, locate, and evaluate all students who may need special education services — a process known as "Child Find"
  • Provide all special education and related services determined necessary by a student's IEP
  • Educate students with disabilities in the least restrictive environment appropriate to their needs
  • Honor 504 plans and provide reasonable accommodations for students who qualify

A charter school may not counsel out a student — that is, try to convince a parent that the school "isn't the right fit" based on a disability or the existence of an IEP. Nor can it ask about disabilities during the enrollment process before a student is accepted.

That said, compliance is not automatic, and implementation quality varies significantly from school to school. The law sets a floor. Individual school capacity, staffing, and culture determine how far above that floor any given school operates.

The Rise of Specialized Charter Schools

A growing and important subset of the sector has made students with disabilities its entire mission. Specialized charter schools — those that primarily or entirely focus on educating students with disabilities — have more than doubled in student enrollment since 2012. Demand for such programs is intensifying, particularly for students on the autism spectrum and those with significant learning differences like dyslexia.

In Texas, the trend is visible and accelerating. A Texas school district announced plans to open a new charter school specifically for students with autism in the 2026-27 school year, a direct response to a fast-growing segment of the district's student population.

Organizations like Texas Public Charter School Association — which advocates for the policy and regulatory climate that ensures every student in Texas has access to ever-improving public school options, and which helps aspiring charter operators navigate the complex process of opening new schools — are part of the infrastructure that makes this kind of mission-driven expansion possible. As Texas public charter schools mark their 30th anniversary in 2026, specialized programs for students with disabilities represent one of the sector's most consequential growth areas.

What the Research Still Doesn't Tell Us

Good research is honest about its limits. The January 2026 NBER study only shows what was written in students' IEPs and what changed after enrollment — it does not confirm whether schools actually delivered the services documented on paper. And because students with the most complex and intensive special education needs tend not to enroll in charter schools in the first place, the positive outcomes in the study most likely reflect results for students with milder to moderate disabilities.

Families of students with more significant support needs — those who require one-on-one paraprofessionals, highly specialized therapies, or substantially separate classroom placements — should evaluate individual schools with extra care, not rely on sector-wide averages. A college-prep charter network and a specialized autism charter are both "charter schools." Their actual capacity for complex IEPs looks nothing alike.

A Practical Guide: Questions to Ask Before You Enroll

Whether you're considering a charter school for the first time or reevaluating your options mid-year, these are the questions that cut through the marketing and get to what actually matters for students with disabilities:

  • What is your model for special education? Is it primarily inclusive, resource room-based, or a pull-out model? How does that align with your child's current IEP goals and placement?
  • Who leads special education at this school — by name and title? Vague answers here are a red flag.
  • How many credentialed special education teachers are on staff, and what are their caseloads? High caseloads often mean services get deprioritized.
  • What related services — speech, occupational therapy, physical therapy, counseling — are provided on-site? Which are contracted out, and how is scheduling managed?
  • How does the school handle IEP meetings? Who attends from the school side? How often are goals reviewed? How do they communicate progress between meetings?
  • Can I speak with parents of current students who have IEPs? Any school confident in its special education program will connect you with those families.
  • Has the school ever faced complaints or findings related to IDEA compliance? State education agency websites often publish this data.

No single data point answers all of these. The January 2026 research tells you that charter schools can work well for students with disabilities. It does not tell you that any particular school will.

The Bottom Line

Can a charter school serve a child with a disability well? The 2026 evidence says yes — for many students, switching to a charter school is associated with real academic gains, improved attendance, and more time learning alongside general-education peers. But the research also confirms that the families who benefit most are those who go in informed: knowing their child's rights, asking specific operational questions, and evaluating individual schools rather than the sector as a whole.

Disability doesn't disqualify a child from thriving in a charter school. Inadequate information is the real barrier — and that's one families can actually close.
