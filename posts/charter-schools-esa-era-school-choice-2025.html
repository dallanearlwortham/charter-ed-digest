Charter Schools in the ESA Era: How to Thrive, Not Just Survive

The School Choice Boom That Was Supposed to Hurt Charter Schools — Hasn't

When state after state began passing universal Education Savings Account (ESA) programs, many observers predicted charter schools would feel the squeeze. If families could take public dollars to private schools, tutors, or homeschool curricula, why would they choose a charter? The data through 2025 tells a different story — and the lessons in that data are ones every charter school leader should understand.

Enrollment at charter schools increased by nearly 492,210 students — about 14.69 percent — nationwide from the 2019–2020 school year to the 2024–2025 school year, according to preliminary data from the National Alliance for Public Charter Schools. For the 2023–2024 school year alone, charter schools added more than 80,000 new students, making them the only segment of the public school ecosystem experiencing consistent growth, in contrast to district public schools, which have seen a steady decline in enrollment.

That growth is happening during the biggest expansion of private school choice in American history. Understanding why charter schools are holding — and gaining — ground is the most important strategic question in K-12 education right now.

---

How Big Is the ESA Wave?

As of March 2025, nine Republican trifecta states had enacted universal ESAs since 2021. In 2025 alone, Idaho, Indiana, New Hampshire, Tennessee, Texas, and Wyoming passed universal private school choice legislation. By the start of 2025, twelve states had enacted universal school choice programs — including ESAs — giving nearly all K-12 students in those states access to public funds for private education options.

Utah, where charter schools have long been a pillar of the education landscape, sits at the center of this shift. Signed into law in 2023 and first available in the 2024–25 school year, the Utah Fits All scholarship program is the state's ESA. Beginning in 2025–26, scholarship amounts are $8,000 for students enrolled full-time in a qualified private school, $6,000 for home-based students ages 12–18, and $4,000 for home-based students ages 5–11. From charter schools to universal education savings accounts, Utah now offers one of the broadest school-choice landscapes in the country.

That is a genuinely significant new set of options for Utah families — and it means every charter school in the state is now operating in a more competitive environment than it was two years ago. That pressure is something charter leaders can no longer afford to treat as background noise.

---

Why Charter Enrollment Keeps Growing Anyway

What do charter schools offer that an ESA check simply cannot buy on its own? The clearest answer is a complete, coherent, tuition-free public education — no tuition bills to stretch an ESA award to cover, no piecing together a curriculum from a dozen different vendors, no scrambling to find credentialed instructors for every required subject. The National Alliance for Public Charter Schools' 2025 enrollment data makes the case plainly: demand for charter schools is as strong as ever even as ESA programs expand, because today's families expect choices and they expect excellent public schools — and charter schools deliver both.

That demand signal matters. Families are not choosing charter schools by default. They are choosing them because charters provide structured, accountable, mission-driven programming that a patchwork of ESA vendors typically cannot replicate — especially for high school students who need coherent course sequences, college counseling, extracurricular opportunities, and a real diploma pathway under one roof.

There is also the accountability argument, which tends to be underappreciated in the current school choice debate. Charter schools operate under public oversight, report academic outcomes, and can be closed for chronic underperformance — a level of transparency that private schools receiving ESA dollars are often not required to match. For many families navigating a suddenly crowded marketplace of options, that accountability is not a bureaucratic burden. It is a meaningful assurance.

---

The Smarter Move: Treat ESAs as an Opportunity, Not a Rival

The most forward-thinking charter leaders are not playing defense. They are seeing ESA expansion as both a revenue and a mission opportunity.

At the National Alliance's 2025 annual conference, charter school leaders hailed the opportunities created by the rise of private school ESAs, which have skyrocketed in popularity in states that have passed them. Leaders in Texas and Florida discussed how to seize those opportunities by offering a la carte courses to students with ESAs — essentially opening the school's strongest programs to families who may not be enrolled full time.

Florida is the furthest along this path. The state's 2023 House Bill 1 converted all K-12 scholarship programs into universally available ESAs and created the Personalized Education Program, which allows parents to use $8,000 per student to build a fully customized education for children not enrolled full time in a public or private school. Charter schools in Florida have begun listing individual courses on state platforms, letting ESA families select classes the way they might browse an online marketplace — earning per-course revenue while introducing the school to entirely new student populations who might enroll full time later.

In Texas, where the ESA program will not launch until 2026, a coalition of charter leaders already started a pilot for private-pay students at four schools, offering a la carte classes online and in person, including some after school. The message from those leaders is clear: this is an opportunity, not a threat. For charter schools in states earlier in the ESA adoption curve, this is the playbook worth studying now — before competitive habits are already set.

---

What This Means for Tuition-Free Charter Schools Specifically

The tuition-free label has always been a powerful differentiator for charter schools, and it becomes more valuable — not less — as ESA programs proliferate. Here is why: ESAs transfer purchasing power to families, but they do not eliminate cost or complexity. Private school tuition frequently exceeds ESA award amounts, especially at high-performing schools. Homeschool and hybrid arrangements require significant daily time investment from parents who are also managing jobs and households. Microschool pods, while growing fast, remain largely unproven at scale for academic outcomes.

A well-run, tuition-free charter school offering a full academic program, extracurriculars, credentialed teachers, and a clearly defined school culture remains one of the most complete education values available in any state — particularly for working families who want genuine quality without the financial stress or the logistical burden of assembling a school experience from scratch.

Career Path High, a tuition-free charter school serving grades 9–12 in Kaysville, Utah, is operating in exactly this kind of expanded-choice environment — where Utah families now have more publicly funded education options than at any point in state history, and where a school's ability to clearly articulate its distinct value proposition is no longer optional. It is existential.

---

Practical Takeaways for Charter Leaders in 2025

Whether your state is a mature ESA market or just entering one, the strategic implications are consistent. Charter schools that grow through this period will be the ones acting on these realities now:

  • Sharpen your value narrative. What specific outcomes, culture, or programs does your school offer that a family simply cannot assemble with an ESA check and a vendor list? "Free and good" is the floor, not the ceiling.
  • Map your full competitive landscape. In ESA states you are competing not just with other charter and district schools, but with private schools that are suddenly affordable to more families and with the entire ed-tech and microschool marketplace.
  • Explore a la carte partnerships. If your state permits ESA students to access individual courses at charter schools, consider piloting that model. The revenue is real, and it puts your school's strengths in front of families who may enroll full time later.
  • Lead with accountability as a selling point. Transparent academic data and public oversight are charter advantages in a marketplace where many alternatives operate with far less scrutiny. Do not assume families already understand this distinction — make the case explicitly.
  • Track legal developments in your state. A district court judge ruled on April 18, 2025, that Utah's Fits All program violated the state constitution, but allowed it to continue operating while the case is appealed. The ESA landscape remains legally unsettled in several states, and major rulings can shift the competitive picture quickly.

---

The Bottom Line

The rise of universal ESAs has not weakened charter schools — it has raised the bar. Families now have genuine, publicly funded alternatives, which means the charter schools that will thrive are the ones doing what great charters have always done: operating with a clear mission, delivering measurable outcomes, and being specific and confident about what makes them worth choosing over everything else on the menu.

Charter schools now serve nearly 4 million students in more than 8,100 schools nationwide and have added over half a million students over the last six years, even as the overall school-age population shrinks. That is not an accident. It is evidence that public, tuition-free, mission-driven education still has a powerful case to make in any school choice environment — broad or narrow. The only question is whether each individual school is making that case clearly enough to be heard above the noise.
