Teacher Stability: A Charter School Quality Signal

# Why Teacher Stability Is One of the Most Underrated Quality Signals in K-8 Charter Schools

When families evaluate K-8 schools, they typically weigh test scores, safety ratings, and program offerings. What they rarely check — and arguably should — is how long teachers actually stay.

Teacher stability is not a soft, feel-good metric. It is a measurable proxy for whether a school is working: for its students, its staff, and its mission. In 2026, the data on teacher turnover makes a compelling case for why families choosing a charter school should ask pointed questions about staff retention before they ever sit down for an enrollment tour.

The National Turnover Picture — and Why Charter Schools Face a Steeper Hill

A 2026 Learning Policy Institute report drawing on the most recent federal teacher and principal survey data found that charter schools have a teacher turnover rate of 17.8%, compared to 14.9% in traditional public schools. That roughly three-point gap compounds over time. If a K-8 school loses nearly one in five teachers every year, a student who enrolls in kindergarten may face a near-complete rotation of classroom teachers before they reach fourth grade.

The same report found that turnover is disproportionately concentrated in the schools that can least afford it. Schools serving the highest concentrations of students from low-income households lose teachers at rates 35–37% higher than schools serving the least economically vulnerable students. A separate 2026 analysis by Education Resource Strategies (ERS), examining nine large school districts, found that schools with at least 70% of students experiencing poverty lost 19% of their teachers between October 2024 and October 2025 — compared to 14% at lower-poverty schools. During the 2025–26 school year, nearly half of teachers in the lowest-poverty schools had been at their school for at least four years, versus only about one-third of teachers in the highest-poverty schools.

For K-8 charter schools that serve predominantly under-resourced communities, this is not an abstract policy problem. It is the daily reality of continuity — or the lack of it — in children's academic lives.

What Turnover Actually Costs Schools and Students

Replacing a teacher is not cheap. The Learning Policy Institute estimates that teacher replacement costs an average of $25,000 in large districts and $12,000 in small districts (in 2024 dollars). Every dollar spent on recruitment, onboarding, and substitute coverage is a dollar not spent on instructional materials, family programs, or professional development for the teachers who stayed.

But the costs that don't appear in a budget line are more consequential. A 2026 study published in Developmental Psychology tracked teacher-student relationship quality and its associations with academic competence, social competence, and behavioral outcomes from kindergarten through sixth grade. Findings showed consistent within-year associations with school adjustment, and longitudinal results suggested that fluctuations in relationship quality predict downstream changes in academic functioning.

Put simply: when a child loses a teacher they have built trust with — mid-year, between grades, or because a school simply cannot retain staff — the disruption lands not just emotionally but academically.

The Revolving Door Hits Early-Career Teachers Hardest

The ERS analysis also found that one in four teachers with zero to two years of experience left their school after the 2024–25 school year — far higher than the one-in-seven rate for teachers with eight to fifteen years of experience. New teachers are often assigned to the students who need the most support, precisely when those teachers are least equipped to provide it without mentorship and a stable environment. When a rookie teacher leaves, the school frequently cycles in another rookie — and the pattern repeats.

This is where mission-aligned charter schools can either break the cycle or perpetuate it. Schools that hire deliberately for cultural fit, invest in early-career mentoring, and build tight professional communities tend to retain newer teachers at higher rates than those that treat staffing as a logistics problem to be solved each August.

What the Research Says Actually Reduces Turnover

The Learning Policy Institute analysis identified three factors most strongly associated with whether teachers stay or go:

  • Compensation. For every $1,000 increase in salary (adjusted for cost of living), the probability of turnover decreases by approximately 0.34 percentage points. Teachers earning around $70,000 had turnover rates nearly 40% lower than those earning around $50,000.
  • School leadership. Turnover rates were cut in half among teachers who reported effective, supportive leadership — 9.0% among those with strong leadership versus 18.7% among those without it.
  • Job and workplace satisfaction. Teachers with high workplace satisfaction left at a rate of just 8.0%, compared to 22.0% among those with low satisfaction.

The takeaway for charter school leaders is that turnover is not random attrition. It is a signal — and largely a preventable one — that responds directly to intentional investment in people and culture.

How Mission-Driven Schools Approach Stability Differently

This is where well-designed charter schools hold a structural advantage. Unlike large district systems, they can hire explicitly for mission fit, build tight professional cultures, and maintain the relational intimacy — among staff, between teachers and students, and between school and family — that makes people want to stay.

Solare Collegiate Charter School, a tuition-free K-8 college preparatory school in Southwest Albuquerque, New Mexico, grounds its model in individualized support, character development, and academic rigor. That kind of explicit, unified mission creates natural alignment between what the school asks of teachers and what educators motivated by purpose-driven work are actively seeking. Schools designed around a coherent identity give teachers a reason to stay that goes beyond a salary line — they stay because the work matches their values, and because they are surrounded by colleagues who chose the same thing deliberately.

What Families Can and Should Ask Before Enrolling

What does it mean when a K-8 school has low teacher turnover? It means students are more likely to be taught by educators who know the school's culture, know the curriculum deeply, and — critically — know them. Longitudinal relationships between teachers and students are one of the most powerful and underappreciated levers in elementary education. Low turnover is a signal that those relationships are being protected.

If you are comparing K-8 charter schools, teacher stability is a fair and important question to raise. A school worth choosing should be able to answer it with specifics. Consider asking:

  • What is your average teacher tenure, and how has it trended over the past three years?
  • What percentage of teachers who started last year are returning this fall?
  • How do you specifically support teachers in their first and second years?
  • What does your leadership team do day-to-day to make this a school where educators want to build a career?

Vague answers — or visible discomfort with the questions — are data points in themselves. Schools that have thought carefully about retention will have concrete answers and, often, genuine pride in what those numbers reflect.

Stability Is a Feature, Not an Accident

Teacher retention does not happen by default. It is built through leadership, culture, compensation, and a school design that makes the daily work of teaching sustainable and meaningful. For K-8 families, understanding that reality — and asking about it directly — turns an overlooked variable into one of the most useful filters in a school search.

A school where teachers are known, supported, and choosing to return year after year is a school where your child will be known too. And at the K-8 level especially, being known by a teacher who has watched you grow across multiple years is one of the most powerful things a school can offer.

Ask the questions. Look at the patterns. Stability, it turns out, is not a happy accident — it is a design choice, and it shows.
