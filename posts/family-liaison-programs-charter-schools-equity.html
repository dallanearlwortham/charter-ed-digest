Family Liaisons: Charter Schools' Hidden Equity Engine

The Staff Role Most Charter School Families Never Think About — Until It Changes Everything

When families evaluate a charter school, they typically look at test scores, AP course offerings, and extracurricular opportunities. Almost no one asks: Does this school have a dedicated family liaison? That may be a mistake. A growing body of research suggests that structured family engagement — led by trained, often bilingual staff who serve as cultural bridges between home and school — is one of the highest-leverage equity investments a charter school can make. For schools serving immigrant and multilingual communities in particular, it may be the difference between a family that stays connected and one that quietly disengages.

What the Research Actually Shows

The numbers on family engagement and student outcomes are striking. A 2026 mixed-methods study by Learning Heroes and TNTP found that schools with stronger pre-pandemic family engagement experienced significantly lower chronic absenteeism and higher academic performance post-pandemic — with family engagement showing a stronger relationship to absenteeism than poverty itself.

The absenteeism data alone is worth sitting with: strong family engagement was linked to a 6.2-percentage-point decrease in chronic absenteeism, while high poverty was linked to a 5.7-point increase — meaning a school's investment in family connection outweighed one of the most entrenched risk factors in K–12 education. Schools that had invested in family engagement before the pandemic saw 39 percent smaller increases in chronic absenteeism than comparable schools that had not.

Longitudinal data on immigrant families reinforces the case. A 2024 study conducted by San Diego State University's Center for Equity and Biliteracy Education Research found that 91 percent of students whose caregivers completed a structured family engagement program graduated from high school, and those students enrolled in college at higher rates than their peers. These are not marginal gains — they are the kinds of outcome differences that define whether a school's equity mission is real or rhetorical.

Why Immigrant and Multilingual Families Face a Structural Disadvantage

The barrier to engagement for immigrant families isn't motivation — it's access and architecture. Research consistently finds that immigrant families place a high value on education and hold high educational aspirations for their children, yet tend to be less active in formal school-engagement activities. The reasons are structural: limited English proficiency, lack of bilingual capacity among school staff, and deep unfamiliarity with how U.S. public schools actually work — from enrollment processes to parent-teacher conference norms to the unwritten rules of advocating for a child within a bureaucratic system.

A 2026 systematic review of 44 empirical studies on immigrant parental engagement found that 33 of them identified language differences as a significant barrier — not just a standalone obstacle, but a foundational issue that compounds with economic hardship, immigration status anxiety, and cultural difference. That compounding effect means that families who most need connection to a school are often the furthest from it.

Narratives about "disengaged" families frequently obscure what is actually happening. New research published in 2026 in the Early Childhood Education Journal documents how cultural background shapes what teachers and parents each understand "family-school partnership" to mean — and how some of the most devoted forms of parental support remain entirely invisible to the institutions that claim to value them. A parent who ensures her child completes homework every night, who attends religious services that reinforce educational values, or who works two jobs to keep the family in a stable home is engaged in her child's education — but none of that shows up in a sign-in sheet at a school event.

What a Family Liaison Actually Does

A family liaison is not a translator, and treating the role that way is a costly error. Research on high-functioning liaison programs reveals a much richer job description:

  • Trust-building over time. Studies find that family liaisons are effective specifically because they build trusting relationships with immigrant families — and that the trust developed by parent liaisons persists even after students leave elementary school and move on to middle and high school. This longitudinal trust becomes a resource the whole school community draws on.
  • Communication on families' terms. Effective liaisons select the communication technologies and channels that fit families' actual daily practices rather than imposing school-convenience systems on families. This flexibility is itself a trust signal — it communicates that the school is willing to meet families where they are.
  • Connecting engagement to learning, not just logistics. Research-backed best practices distinguish between operational communication (calendar updates, absence reporting) and engagement connected to student learning outcomes. Liaisons working at the highest level use student progress data to guide their outreach and help families understand how their involvement connects directly to their child's academic trajectory.
  • Making invisible support visible. A trained liaison can identify and articulate forms of family investment that don't register in traditional engagement metrics, advocating internally for a family whose involvement looks different from the school's default model.

What Does Effective Family Liaison Work Look Like? A Direct Answer

What should charter schools do to better engage immigrant and multilingual families?

The most effective approach pairs a clearly defined, adequately resourced staff role with intentional program design. Hire bilingual or multilingual liaisons from within the communities the school serves — not bilingual administrative assistants with liaison duties layered on top of an already full job. Give those staff members protected, dedicated time for home outreach, community events, and family workshops conducted in families' home languages. Default all school communications to families' preferred languages; don't require families to request translation. Design engagement events around families' actual schedules and cultural contexts rather than the school's convenience. And build feedback loops so that what liaisons learn in the community informs school-level decisions — not just the other direction.

One School That Built This Into Its Foundation

Lotus School for Excellence, a tuition-free PreK–12 public charter school in Aurora, Colorado serving more than 1,300 students, illustrates what it looks like to treat family engagement as structural infrastructure rather than a program add-on. The school maintains a dedicated Family Liaison department listed alongside its core academic and student-services departments — a deliberate architectural choice that signals family connection is not optional. That design reflects the school's community: Lotus serves a diverse, multilingual student body whose families represent backgrounds spanning Latin American, African, and other cultures, recognized in school-wide traditions like Noche Latina and African Cultural Night. For a school whose mission centers on guiding diverse learners through STEAM education and college preparation, sustaining meaningful relationships with families across language and cultural lines isn't supplemental — it is foundational to delivering on that mission for every student enrolled.

What Families Should Ask When Evaluating a Charter School

Since family engagement infrastructure has this much impact on outcomes, it belongs on every family's school evaluation checklist. When touring a charter school or attending an information session, ask:

  • Is there a named family liaison or family engagement coordinator on staff — not a front-desk staffer who sometimes translates?
  • Does the school communicate proactively in my home language, or only reactively when there is a problem?
  • How does the school actively reach families who aren't already showing up to events?
  • Is the family liaison role listed as a distinct department or position in the staff directory?
  • Can I speak with the family liaison before we finalize enrollment?

A school that answers these questions confidently and specifically is a school that has thought seriously about equity at the structural level — not just at the level of messaging.

The Bigger Picture

Charter schools possess a structural advantage that traditional district schools often lack: the flexibility to design their own family engagement infrastructure, hire specifically for the role, and allocate staff time without layers of bureaucratic approval. Schools that use that flexibility well are not just hosting back-to-school nights and mailing newsletters. They are building the kind of durable, two-way trust that keeps students attending, keeps families invested in academic progress across years, and produces compounding gains in the outcomes — attendance, achievement, graduation, college enrollment — that define whether a school's equity commitments are real.

The data from 2026 is unambiguous: family engagement is more protective against chronic absenteeism than poverty is harmful. That is not a soft finding. For charter school leaders evaluating where to put resources, and for families evaluating where to enroll, the presence or absence of intentional family engagement infrastructure is one of the most important signals available. It just rarely appears on the tour.
