Can Charter Schools Survive the School Choice Surge?

# Can Charter Schools Survive the School Choice Surge?

Charter schools spent decades fighting to become a choice. Now they're facing a new problem: they're no longer the boldest option on the menu. As education savings accounts expand across the country and families gain the ability to direct public dollars to private schools, microschools, and even homeschool co-ops, the charter sector faces an enrollment threat it was not built to anticipate.

A new report published in August 2026 by the American Enterprise Institute makes the pressure concrete—and the implications for charter leaders are worth taking seriously right now, at the start of the 2026-27 school year.

The AEI Report: What Charter Leaders Are Telling Researchers

The AEI report, authored by Ian Kingsbury and David Marshall, draws on survey data from 285 charter school leaders in Arizona, Florida, and Utah—three states with well-established, universal ESA programs that represent ideal case studies for what happens to the charter sector when school choice expands broadly.

The findings are sobering:

  • Nearly half of charter leaders surveyed reported enrollment losses due to students using an ESA for non-charter options such as private school tuition or subsidized homeschooling.
  • Nearly the same proportion anticipated a future decline in total enrollment.
  • Enrollment impacts were most pronounced in Arizona, where the ESA program is most mature.
  • Charter leaders reported a growing perception among families that private and microschool models now offer superior flexibility and autonomy.

The report's bottom line is blunt: "Charter schools, long celebrated for innovation and choice, risk marginalization unless they undergo a fundamental shift away from a system-centric approach toward a parent-centric model."

That's not a distant warning. It's a description of what is already happening in markets where ESAs have been operating for several years.

The National Picture: 205 Bills, 36 States, and a Federal Push

Arizona, Florida, and Utah are ahead of the curve—but the curve is moving fast. According to FutureEd, which is tracking 205 private school choice bills across 36 states in the 2026 legislative session, lawmakers across the country are proposing new programs, expanding existing ones, revising eligibility rules, and strengthening oversight. Heading into this session, 17 states have, or soon will have, programs that make public funds available for private educational expenses to all students, regardless of income.

A new federal tax credit scholarship program is also scheduled to take effect in January 2027, and state legislatures are already moving to shape how their states participate. West Virginia is expected to open its ESA program to all students in 2026-27. Texas launched its own universal program. This is the most significant structural shift in K-12 funding in a generation.

For charter schools—which are free by design and publicly funded—the threat isn't tuition competition. It's perception competition. Families who now have genuine choices are asking what charter schools offer that a flexible private school, a small microschool, or a personalized homeschool co-op cannot. Charter leaders who cannot answer that question crisply will see enrollment erode regardless of quality.

What Families Are Actually Looking For

The EdChoice 2026 Schooling in America Survey found that ESAs remain the most popular school choice policy among the general public, with 78% support—and 84% among school parents. That level of enthusiasm reflects something real: parents want control, flexibility, and a sense that their child's education is built around their child, not around institutional convenience.

Interestingly, the same survey found that charter school support among parents sits at 75%—strong, but slightly below ESA and voucher programs. The implication: charter schools enjoy significant goodwill, but families increasingly want the kind of flexibility and autonomy they associate with parent-directed choice programs.

This is actually an opportunity, not just a threat. Charters that successfully communicate their own flexibility—in scheduling, curriculum, learning environment, and family engagement—can make a compelling case that they offer the best of both worlds: public accountability and parental responsiveness, at no cost.

How Online Charter Schools Are Positioned to Compete

Among charter models, fully online schools may be the best-positioned to weather the ESA era. They already offer the structural flexibility that families cite as their primary reason for pursuing alternative education: schedule autonomy, individualized pacing, learning from home, and freedom from a fixed geographic assignment. And unlike private ESA-funded options, they are entirely tuition-free.

Oregon Charter Academy (SYS Education), a fully online K-12 public charter school in Oregon, illustrates what a differentiated, parent-responsive charter model looks like in practice. The school delivers 100% online instruction through a Canvas-based platform, provides a Chromebook to every enrolled student, offers a dedicated CTE pathway program called ASCEND, includes built-in counseling services, and runs a dual credit program through which 1,700 students have already earned college credit. That combination—tech-ready infrastructure, career pathways, academic supports, and zero tuition—is a genuinely competitive package in a marketplace that's getting more crowded.

What Charter Leaders Must Do Differently in 2026-27

The AEI report found that charter leaders are calling for regulatory reform: 65% cited the need to reduce regulation and bureaucracy, 58% referenced funding equity and facilities support, and 42% called for relief around special education compliance. Those systemic issues require advocacy. But there are things charter leaders can act on right now, without waiting for policy to change:

1. Lead with outcomes, not identity. "We're a charter school" is not a differentiator anymore. What are your graduation rates? College placement? Career credential attainment? Make the numbers visible and front-facing.

2. Redesign your enrollment conversation around parent priorities. The shift the AEI report calls for—from system-centric to parent-centric—starts with how you talk to prospective families. Ask what they need. Show how your school meets it.

3. Find your curricular signature. Whether it's AI literacy, project-based learning, dual enrollment, CTE pathways, or something else, families choosing in a crowded market are drawn to schools with a coherent, distinctive instructional identity. Generic doesn't compete.

4. Make flexibility visible. If your school has scheduling flexibility, competency-based elements, or personalized pacing, say so explicitly. Don't assume families know these features exist. Many are still mentally comparing you to a traditional district school when the more relevant comparison is a microschool or ESA program.

5. Invest in family communication as a strategic priority. The single biggest differentiator between charter schools that are growing and those that are shrinking in ESA states is how actively they engage families—not just at enrollment, but month to month throughout the year.

The Stakes Are Real—But So Is the Charter Advantage

It would be a mistake to read the AEI data as a death knell for charter schools. The charter sector's core strengths—public funding, credentialed teachers, accreditation, accountability structures, and the ability to serve all students including those with IEPs and 504 plans—are not matched by most ESA-funded alternatives. Microschools and homeschool co-ops often cannot accommodate complex learning needs. Private schools can be expensive even with a partial ESA subsidy. Online charter schools, in particular, combine the flexibility families now expect with the safety net of a licensed, accountable public institution.

But advantages only matter if families know about them. The charter sector's challenge in 2026 is less about what it is and more about how clearly it communicates what it is—and whether it's willing to evolve fast enough to stay ahead of a school choice market that is moving at a pace few anticipated.

The schools that treat this moment as a wake-up call will be better positioned in 2030. The ones that don't may not make it there.
