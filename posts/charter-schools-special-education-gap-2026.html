Charter Schools & Special Education: Close the Gap in 2026

# Charter Schools and Special Education: The Gap Is Widening — and Leaders Need to Act

Special education is the area where charter school accountability most visibly collides with charter school autonomy. The legal obligations are non-negotiable — every charter school that accepts public funds must provide a free appropriate public education to students with disabilities, implement IEPs in compliance with federal law, and admit students without regard to disability status. But the data in 2026 tells a consistent story: charter schools are enrolling fewer students with disabilities than traditional public schools, the gap between the two sectors is widening in several states, and the staffing crisis making all of this harder to address is not improving.

For charter school leaders, this is not just a compliance matter. It is a mission matter.

What the Data Shows in 2026

The numbers are stark and getting starker. In Texas, a February 2026 report from the University of Texas at Austin found that in the 2024-25 school year, students with disabilities made up 12 percent of charter school enrollment versus 15.8 percent in traditional public schools statewide. The University of Texas researchers also found twice the enrollment growth of students with disabilities at traditional public schools versus charter schools from 2009 to 2025 — meaning the gap has not been stable, it has been accelerating.

Texas is not an outlier. National data from prior years shows a similar pattern: traditional public schools consistently enroll a greater proportion of students with disabilities than charter schools do. The causes are complex. Research consistently shows that a significant share of the gap exists not because charter schools push students out, but because students with certain disabilities — particularly autism and speech or language impairments — are less likely to apply to charter schools in the first place, often because families assume (sometimes correctly) that the specialized supports they need will not be available. Charter schools are also less likely than district schools to classify students as needing special education services, and more likely to declassify students who came in with IEPs.

Both of those patterns carry legal and ethical implications that charter leaders cannot afford to ignore.

The Staffing Crisis Underneath the Data

Even charter schools that are genuinely committed to serving students with disabilities are facing a structural obstacle: there are not enough qualified special education teachers. A 2025 national scan from the Learning Policy Institute reported that at least 411,549 teaching positions were unfilled or filled by underqualified staff — with special education representing a disproportionate share of those vacancies. Special education staffing shortages have reached what experts are calling "crisis" levels at both the state and national levels, with high burnout rates, excessive caseloads, outdated staffing models, and credentialing bottlenecks driving chronic turnover.

Why is special education staffing so hard for charter schools specifically?

Charter schools typically operate with leaner administrative structures and smaller budgets than large district schools. They may not have the dedicated special education coordinators, related service providers (speech-language pathologists, occupational therapists, psychologists), or specialized classroom infrastructure that students with significant disabilities require. When a charter school is also trying to recruit and retain general education teachers, fund a flexible schedule, and manage a growth-phase budget, special education staffing often loses out in resource allocation decisions — even when the intent is to serve all students.

That is a leadership and governance failure, not an inevitable structural constraint.

What Charter Leaders Are Legally Required to Do

The legal floor is clear, even when the practical path is hard. Charter schools must:

  • Admit students without screening for disability status. An application process cannot require disclosure of an IEP or 504 plan, and disability status cannot be a factor in admission decisions.
  • Implement IEPs as written. Once a student with a disability enrolls, the charter school is responsible for implementing the services specified in the IEP — including arranging for related services, even if that means contracting with outside providers.
  • Participate in IEP development. Charter school staff should participate in IEP meetings, and the school's instructional context should be considered when developing service recommendations.
  • Not counsel families out. Suggesting that a school is "not the right fit" for a student because of their disability — or making the enrollment process burdensome in ways that functionally discourage families with IEPs from applying — exposes a charter to serious legal liability.

Families of students with disabilities have the right to choose a charter school. The charter has an obligation to make that choice viable.

Practical Strategies That Work

The charter schools making real progress on special education access are not waiting for more funding or more staff to appear. They are building smarter systems with what they have.

Contract strategically for related services. A charter school that cannot afford a full-time speech-language pathologist on staff can contract with a licensed provider — or use telehealth-based therapy services — to deliver IEP-mandated services without carrying the full cost of a salaried specialist. Breaking the role into components (one contractor handles IEP meetings, another delivers therapy) can stretch a limited budget further while maintaining compliance.

Build paraeducator pipelines. With training, observation, and ongoing coaching, paraeducators can effectively deliver highly structured interventions and evidence-based practices. Charter schools that invest in developing their paraeducators — including funding pathways to special education certification — create a more stable and aligned workforce than those relying on external hiring.

Use a "grow your own" approach for special education teachers. Financial incentives for teachers in surplus areas to transition into special education roles can reduce staffing shortages by leveraging the existing workforce. Charter networks with multiple campuses are especially well positioned to create cohort-based residency tracks for aspiring special education teachers, spreading the cost across the network.

Audit your enrollment data annually — before your authorizer does. Charter schools should routinely compare their percentage of enrolled students with disabilities against their district and state benchmarks. If the gap is large and growing, the leadership team needs to ask why — and be prepared to answer that question publicly.

A Texas Illustration of the Broader Challenge

The special education access challenge is especially pronounced in states where charter enrollment has expanded fastest. Texas is the clearest example: charter enrollment has grown dramatically over the past two decades, but the proportion of students with disabilities enrolled in those charter campuses has not kept pace with the growth in the overall student population with disabilities. Texas charter networks serving high-need, at-risk student populations — like this one, which welcomes students ages 14 to 21 who need flexible scheduling and accelerated graduation pathways — face the full weight of the special education challenge: their students often arrive with unmet or unidentified needs, their budgets are constrained, and the state's overall charter-sector special education gap creates a context of heightened scrutiny from authorizers and advocates alike.

Getting special education right in that environment is hard. It is also non-negotiable.

What Families of Students with Disabilities Should Know

If you are a family considering a charter school for a child with an IEP or 504 plan, you have rights — and you should use them.

  • A charter school cannot deny admission based on your child's disability.
  • You can ask the school directly: How many of your current students have IEPs? What related services do you provide on-site?
  • You can request to see the school's most recent special education compliance report if your state requires one.
  • If a school tells you it "can't really serve" your child's needs, consult with a special education advocate before accepting that answer at face value.

The right charter school, with the right supports, can be an excellent environment for a student with a disability. But families should go in with clear information, not assumptions.

The Bottom Line

The charter sector's special education enrollment gap is real, it is documented, and in several states it is getting wider — not narrower. The staffing crisis makes it harder to close, but it does not make closure impossible. Charter school leaders who treat special education as a compliance burden to be minimized will eventually face the consequences: legal liability, authorizer action, and reputational damage in the communities they serve.

Charter leaders who treat it as a core commitment — and build the staffing, systems, and culture to back that up — will be the ones whose schools deserve to keep growing.
