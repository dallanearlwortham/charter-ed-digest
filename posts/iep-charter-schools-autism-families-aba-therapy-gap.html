IEPs at Charter Schools: What Autism Families Must Know

# IEPs at Charter Schools: What Autism Families Must Know in 2026

Your child has an autism diagnosis. You've found a charter school you love — smaller class sizes, a focused mission, a community that feels right. Then the questions start: Will they actually deliver the services in my child's IEP? What happens to the ABA therapy we've been doing? Is a charter school the same as a public school when it comes to special education?

These are exactly the right questions — and the answers matter more than most charter school guides acknowledge.

Charter Schools Are Public Schools — With a Catch

The foundational fact every autism family needs to know: charter schools are legally public schools, which means they are covered by the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA). A charter must provide your child with a Free Appropriate Public Education (FAPE) — including evaluating students suspected of having a disability, developing an Individualized Education Program (IEP), and delivering the services written into it.

It's illegal for a charter school to deny admission because of a disability. It's also illegal for a charter to "counsel out" a family — that is, to discourage them from applying or enrolling because the school assumes it can't meet a child's needs. Simply saying "we can't serve your child here" is generally not a sufficient legal reason to turn a family away.

Charter schools are also subject to Child Find obligations in a similar way to traditional public schools. If staff or a family suspect a disability, the charter itself is typically responsible for arranging the evaluation — not the family, and not an outside agency.

So far, so good. But here is where the practical catch comes in.

Why IEP Delivery at Charters Can Look Very Different

Many charter schools are small and independently run, which means they may not have the same bench of in-house specialists — behavior analysts, speech-language pathologists, occupational therapists — that a larger school district can deploy across multiple buildings. When that's the case, a charter typically contracts out for those services rather than skipping them. But contracted services can affect how consistently and how intensively they are actually delivered.

This is not a loophole — a charter school is legally obligated to provide everything in the IEP. But "legally obligated to provide" and "provides it well" are not always the same thing in practice. Contracted services can mean waiting periods, part-time providers shared across multiple campuses, or a therapist who sees your child once a week in a repurposed storage room rather than a proper therapy space.

How formal responsibility is divided also matters. Whether the charter operates as its own local education agency (LEA) or as part of the authorizing school district's LEA can affect who is ultimately accountable for IEP compliance — and who a family goes to when something isn't being delivered. It is worth asking that question directly before enrollment, not after.

Whether special education teachers at a charter must be state-certified also depends on state law. Some states hold charters to identical certification standards as traditional public schools; others allow flexibility. In practice, the qualifications of the adults implementing your child's IEP are worth verifying, not assumed.

The Gap That Opens Before Kindergarten

For families of young autistic children — particularly those still in the birth-to-six window — there is a structural issue that precedes the charter school decision entirely.

Early intervention (EI) supports children under age 3 with developmental delays or disabilities. When a child turns 3, they age out of EI and must transition to other services — typically school-based special education or private therapies like ABA. This transition is governed by a legal shift from IDEA Part C (which covers birth to age 3) to Part B (which covers ages 3 to 21). By law, an EI service coordinator must initiate transition planning at least 90 days before a child's third birthday, and if the child qualifies for school-based services, an IEP must be in place and services must begin by their third birthday — not after.

But even when the paperwork goes smoothly, families frequently discover that the IEP services their child receives in a public or charter preschool program do not fully replace the intensity of the early intervention they had before age 3. For children with autism whose behavioral and communication development requires high-dosage, individualized support, a part-time IEP in a preschool setting is often not enough on its own to sustain momentum.

This is the gap where outside ABA therapy — funded through private insurance, Medicaid, or family resources — becomes essential rather than optional for many families. And it persists well into the kindergarten years for children who are still building foundational skills.

What IEPs Don't Cover — And Why Outside ABA Still Matters

Here is a question autism families search constantly: If my child has an IEP at a charter school, do we still need private ABA therapy?

The honest answer is: often, yes — for several distinct reasons.

First, IEP services are calibrated to educational necessity, not clinical intensity. A school is required to provide what your child needs to access their education and make meaningful progress — not to maximize every possible developmental outcome. The standard is "appropriate," not "optimal."

Second, insurance-funded ABA therapy and school-based IEP services are required to operate in separate lanes. Insurance cannot pay for ABA delivered in school for the same goals the school's IEP is already covering, because doing so would constitute improper double billing. Outside ABA and school services must be coordinated carefully, with distinct goals, to avoid that conflict.

Third, the hours differ substantially. Intensive ABA programs for young autistic children can involve 20 to 40 hours per week of structured, individualized intervention. An IEP, even a robust one, rarely approaches that dosage across all providers combined.

For families navigating the preschool-to-kindergarten transition, keeping outside ABA therapy in place alongside a school program — or choosing a center that deliberately integrates ABA with school readiness preparation — is often what bridges the gap between what a charter's IEP can legally offer and what a young child with autism clinically needs during those critical early years. In the Miami area, families pursuing exactly that integrated model have worked with Prism, an ABA therapy and early advancement center serving children under 6 with autism, where 1:1 therapy from Registered Behavior Therapists is paired with group school readiness instruction — built for families whose goal is to have their child ready for a neighborhood school, whether that school turns out to be a traditional public school or a charter.

Questions to Ask Any Charter School Before You Enroll

If your child has an autism diagnosis or an active IEP, the following questions are not optional — they belong in every conversation with a charter school before you commit:

  • Is the charter its own LEA, or part of the authorizing district's LEA? This determines who is directly and legally responsible for IEP compliance.
  • Who actually provides your IEP-related services — in-house staff or contracted providers? If contracted, how frequently do they come, and how is continuity monitored?
  • What happens if the IEP calls for a service you don't currently have on staff? A school with real special education infrastructure will have a practiced answer to this.
  • Can we see the space where services are delivered? A hallway pull-out or shared corner is not equivalent to a dedicated therapy room.
  • How do you coordinate with outside providers — ABA therapists, private speech therapists — if our child continues those services outside school hours? Some charters actively coordinate. Others have simply never been asked.
  • What is your protocol if a specialist leaves mid-year? Under IDEA, a school cannot legally use a staffing shortage as an excuse to stop delivering IEP services — but you need to know what that looks like in practice at this specific school.

What Florida Families Should Know

Florida has autism-specific charter schools that deliver ABA-based instruction and therapeutic services, including programs serving Miami-Dade and Broward counties. Florida's Empowerment Scholarship Account (ESA) programs also give families of children with disabilities additional flexibility in funding private educational placements and supplemental services.

But the existence of autism-specific charters does not mean that every general-enrollment charter school in Florida is well-equipped to serve a young autistic child. Even excellent general-enrollment charters range widely in their special education staffing, their experience with behavior support plans, and their familiarity with coordinating outside clinical providers. Asking hard questions up front — and keeping clinical support in place while you assess whether the school's answers hold up in the actual school year — is not a sign of distrust. It is sound planning.

The Bottom Line

Charter schools occupy an unusual position in the special education landscape: legally, they carry the same obligations as any public school. Day-to-day, they often operate with far more independence — and far more variability — than a traditional district school. For autism families, that independence cuts both ways. It can mean a more focused mission and a community that genuinely fits your child. It can also mean thinner special education resources, less institutional experience with complex IEPs, and a steeper burden on families to monitor and supplement what the school provides.

The families who navigate this most effectively tend to be the ones who stop asking "are they required to serve my child?" — they are — and start asking "how, specifically, will they do it, and what will we maintain outside of school to make sure the gaps are covered?" Those are the questions worth answering before the first day of school, not after.
