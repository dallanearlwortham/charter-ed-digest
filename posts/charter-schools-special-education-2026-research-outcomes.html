Charter Schools and Special Ed: What the 2026 Research Shows

# Charter Schools and Special Education in 2026: What the Research Actually Shows

Few criticisms of the charter school sector land harder than this one: charter schools underserve students with disabilities, either by discouraging their enrollment or failing to provide adequate services once they're in the door. It's a charge that has shaped policy debates and parent decisions for years. But the research landscape in 2026 looks significantly more complicated — and more favorable to charters — than the conventional narrative suggests.

For charter school leaders and families of students with Individualized Education Programs (IEPs), understanding both the real gaps and the emerging evidence matters more than ever.

---

The Enrollment Gap Is Real — but Shrinking in Context

Start with the most-cited statistic: students with disabilities are underrepresented in charter schools relative to traditional public schools.

According to a February 2026 report from researchers at the University of Texas at Austin, students with disabilities made up 12 percent of charter enrollment in the 2024–25 school year, compared to 15.8 percent in traditional public schools statewide. That roughly four-point gap has been a persistent fixture of charter sector criticism, and it is real.

But context matters. Research from the Manhattan Institute has found that students with autism or speech and language impairments are statistically less likely to apply to charter schools in kindergarten than are students without disabilities — suggesting the enrollment gap reflects family decision-making and application patterns, not simply charter gatekeeping. Families of students with significant needs often make conservative choices, staying in schools with established, well-known special education departments rather than risking an unfamiliar environment.

That is a pipeline problem and a communication problem as much as it is a policy problem — and it has different implications for how charters should respond.

---

What Happens to Students with Disabilities Who Do Enroll in Charters?

Here is the direct answer to what most families and policymakers actually want to know: When students with disabilities switch from traditional public schools to charter schools, their academic outcomes tend to improve — often as much as those of students without disabilities.

That finding comes from a major January 2026 study published by the National Center for Research on Educational Access and Choice, authored by economists Scott Imberman and Andrew Johnson and reviewed by the Thomas B. Fordham Institute. The researchers analyzed data from roughly 60,000 Michigan K–8 students who switched from traditional public schools to charter schools between 2013 and 2018.

Key findings:

  • Math and reading scores improved for students with disabilities and general education students at similar rates after enrolling in a charter school
  • By the third year after enrollment, achievement gains reached approximately 0.2 standard deviations in both subjects — a meaningful improvement
  • Attendance improved significantly, with absence rates falling by 3.3 percentage points in the first year after charter enrollment
  • Students with disabilities in charter schools were more likely to be placed in general education classrooms — resource-style inclusion programs rather than intensive self-contained settings
  • These inclusive placements did not come at a cost to academic performance

A 2019 Boston study reached a similar conclusion, finding that charter schools were better than traditional public schools at preparing students with disabilities to meet college-level standards. The author concluded that "schools can boost the academic outcomes of special-education students without traditional specialized services."

---

The Inclusion Finding Is Particularly Significant

One nuanced result from the Michigan study deserves attention: upon entering charter schools, students with disabilities initially spent less time in separate special education settings — roughly half as much as they had in their prior district schools. By their third year, that time had returned to pre-charter levels.

What this suggests is that charters tend to rely more on resource-room and inclusion-based models — where students spend most of their day in general education classrooms and receive targeted pull-out instruction — rather than intensive, full-day self-contained programs. Critics have pointed to this as a potential red flag. The research, however, finds no academic penalty. Achievement and attendance improved regardless.

The Fordham analysis notes that this may actually reflect a more appropriate use of services: traditional public schools may rely "too heavily on disability classification, intensive placements, or accommodations for students with relatively mild special needs, inadvertently lowering expectations."

---

What Charter Schools Are Legally Required to Do

A common misconception, especially among families of students with disabilities, is that charter schools can decline to serve children with IEPs. They cannot.

As public schools, charter schools are bound by the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA). They must identify, evaluate, and serve all students with disabilities. They must implement IEPs. They cannot screen or exclude students based on disability status during the admissions process.

The New York City Charter Schools Collaborative's 2025–26 special education guide states plainly: "Charter schools do not select students based upon their academic abilities" — and the same applies to disability status.

---

What This Means for PreK–12 Charter Networks

The special education conversation is particularly consequential for charter schools that serve students across the full PreK–12 spectrum. When a school enrolls a child in kindergarten with a disability diagnosis, it is committing to serve that student through graduation — across all the complexity that IEP evolution, transition planning, and secondary services entail.

Freedom Prep, a tuition-free public charter school network in Utah serving students from PreK through 12th grade — with physical campuses and a statewide online option — exemplifies the scope of that commitment: a network spanning the full K–12 pipeline must build systems for serving students with disabilities at every grade level and in every format, from early childhood through college and career readiness.

That kind of comprehensive special education infrastructure is a genuine operational challenge, and it's one reason why charter networks with strong PreK–12 continuity often cite special education program development as one of their most significant ongoing investments.

---

What Families of Students with Disabilities Should Ask

If you're a family considering a charter school for a child with an IEP, the enrollment gap data should not be your only reference point. The more useful questions to ask during enrollment or open house:

  • What is the school's special education staffing model? (Resource room, co-teaching, self-contained, or a combination?)
  • How does the school handle IEP meetings and progress monitoring?
  • What related services (speech, OT, PT, counseling) are provided on-site versus contracted out?
  • What is the school's experience with your child's specific disability category?
  • How does the school communicate with families about IEP implementation?

The answers will tell you far more than enrollment statistics.

---

The Bottom Line for Charter Leaders

The 2026 research offers charter school leaders both encouragement and a clear imperative. The evidence that charters can improve outcomes for students with disabilities — even while using less intensive service models — is growing stronger. But the enrollment gap is real, and closing it requires active outreach, transparent communication about services, and a willingness to invest in the special education infrastructure that families of students with disabilities need to feel confident making a choice.

The sector's strongest argument is not that it is already perfect on special education. It's that the data increasingly shows charters can serve students with disabilities well — and that families deserve access to that evidence when making decisions.
