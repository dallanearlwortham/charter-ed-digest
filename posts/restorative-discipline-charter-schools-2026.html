Restorative Discipline in Charter Schools: What the Research Shows

# Restorative Discipline in Charter Schools: What the Research Shows in 2026

When families research charter schools, they often ask about academics, extracurriculars, and mission. Increasingly, they're also asking a quieter but consequential question: How does this school handle conflict? Discipline philosophy, once buried in the back of a student handbook, is now a real enrollment factor—and charter schools that have moved away from zero-tolerance punishment toward restorative practices are seeing the payoff in culture, retention, and outcomes.

Why Zero-Tolerance Discipline Failed Schools

For decades, the default response to student misbehavior in U.S. schools was automatic punishment: detention, suspension, or expulsion. The logic seemed straightforward—consequences deter misconduct. But the evidence caught up with that logic, and it wasn't favorable.

Research from the American Psychological Association found that zero-tolerance approaches often remove students from learning without improving behavior, creating a counterproductive cycle. Students who are suspended fall behind academically, disengage from school community, and face a higher risk of future disciplinary incidents. For students already navigating difficult circumstances at home, a suspension can become the first step toward long-term disengagement.

The approach also produced starkly unequal outcomes. Suspension rates fell disproportionately on Black students, students with disabilities, and students from low-income families—a pattern documented in both traditional public schools and in charter networks that over-relied on strict punitive codes.

What Restorative Practices Actually Look Like

Restorative practices (RP) represent a fundamental shift in how schools respond to conflict and misconduct. Rather than asking "What rule was broken and what is the punishment?", a restorative approach asks "Who was harmed, and how do we repair it?"

In practice, this can look like:

  • Restorative circles — structured group conversations where affected students, peers, and staff work through an incident together
  • Guided reflection conversations — one-on-one dialogues between a student and a trusted staff member after a behavior incident
  • Peer mediation programs — trained student mediators helping resolve conflicts before they escalate
  • Tiered behavior support plans — individualized supports for students showing recurring patterns, rather than repeated suspensions
  • Community accountability agreements — student-led commitments about how to repair harm and move forward

The goal across all of these is skill-building: students learn conflict resolution, empathy, and accountability rather than simply learning to avoid getting caught.

What the Research Says: Promising and Measurable Results

Do restorative practices actually reduce suspensions in schools? Yes—across multiple rigorous studies, the answer is a consistent yes, particularly when schools invest in genuine training and implementation quality.

One of the most compelling datasets comes from Chicago Public Schools (CPS), where researchers studied an RP rollout across 73 high schools from 2014 to 2019. Published findings showed that schools implementing restorative practices saw an 18% overall decline in suspension days and a 19% decrease in student arrests in the four years following implementation. Crucially, these gains didn't come at the cost of classroom learning—there was no evidence that peer test scores declined, and students in RP schools reported improvements in school safety, sense of belonging, and perceptions of classroom behavior on the district's annual survey. Black male and female students saw reductions in suspension days more than double the average reductions observed across all students—directly addressing the equity gap that punitive policies had widened.

A RAND Corporation study of restorative practices in Pittsburgh Public Schools reinforced those findings, showing that RP reduced suspension rates most significantly among elementary students, Black students, and students from low-income families.

A 2026 review of 34 studies found that restorative practices improve school climate, reduce suspensions, and build social-emotional skills across school contexts. Research from the Learning Policy Institute similarly found that restorative discipline improves school climate and reduces repeat behavior issues when used consistently and backed by staff training.

The Charter School Advantage—and Responsibility

Charter schools occupy a unique position in this conversation. Charter flexibility gives schools the ability to design and implement restorative systems from the ground up—building staff training into professional development calendars, embedding restorative values into the founding mission, and creating a culture of accountability that doesn't depend on exclusion.

On the other hand, some charter networks built their early reputations on strict behavioral codes and zero-tolerance policies. In 2026, that model faces increasing scrutiny from families, researchers, and authorizers alike. The question is no longer whether restorative practices work—the research base is substantial—but whether schools are willing to invest in the training and relationship infrastructure that makes them work.

Career Path High, a tuition-free grades 9–12 charter school in Kaysville, Utah, represents the kind of mission-driven model where a positive, purposeful school culture is foundational—because preparing students for careers and life after graduation requires more than academic content. It requires a school environment where students feel respected, accountable, and genuinely part of a community.

The most effective charter discipline systems in 2026 share a few characteristics: they teach behavioral expectations explicitly at the start of the year rather than assuming students arrive knowing them; they invest in counselor and staff capacity before problems arise; and they use data—attendance patterns, repeat referrals, demographic breakdowns—to identify where the system is working and where it is failing students.

What Families Should Ask When Evaluating a School's Discipline Approach

If you're a parent choosing between schools, don't just skim the code of conduct. Ask these questions directly:

  1. What happens the first time a student has a serious conflict? The answer tells you whether punishment or repair is the school's first instinct.
  2. What staff training supports restorative practices? Without trained adults, even well-written policies fall apart in the moment.
  3. What does your suspension data look like, and how has it changed over the past three years? A school that tracks this and can explain trends is one that takes accountability seriously.
  4. How are families involved when behavior incidents occur? Effective restorative systems treat parents as partners, not just recipients of bad news.
  5. What support is in place for students with emotional or behavioral needs? Tiered intervention—not just tiered punishment—is the marker of a mature discipline system.

Schools with strong answers to these questions tend to be the ones where students actually want to stay.

Discipline Philosophy Is Culture

The deeper lesson from the research is that discipline philosophy and school culture are inseparable. Schools where students feel known, respected, and genuinely accountable to their community don't need to rely on exclusionary punishment—because students have more reason to stay engaged and fewer reasons to act out.

For charter leaders, restorative practices aren't just a policy choice. They're a culture-building strategy, a retention strategy, and increasingly, an enrollment strategy. Families in 2026 are paying close attention to how schools treat students when things go wrong.

Suspension data will never make your school's marketing brochure. But how your school handles conflict tells prospective families everything about who you really are.
