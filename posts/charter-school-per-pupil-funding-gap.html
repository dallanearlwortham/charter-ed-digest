The Charter School Funding Gap Families Must Know

# The Charter School Funding Gap: Why Your Public School Gets Less Money Than the One Next Door

Here is a number worth sitting with: the average charter school in a major U.S. city receives roughly $7,000 to $8,000 less per student each year than a nearby traditional public school. Both schools are public. Both are tuition-free. Both serve children who live in the same neighborhoods. Yet one operates at a structural financial disadvantage that shapes every hiring decision, every classroom resource, and every program it can or cannot offer.

This is the charter school funding gap — and in 2025 and 2026, it has moved from a background frustration to a front-burner policy fight across the country.

What the Research Actually Shows

The evidence is consistent across more than two decades of independent study. A widely cited analysis by the University of Arkansas School Choice Demonstration Project found that charter schools receive, on average, 30% less in total funding than traditional public schools — roughly $7,147 less per pupil — based on 2019–20 data covering 18 major cities and 16 states. Follow-up studies found the gap has remained stubbornly persistent, barely narrowing even as charter enrollment has surged to all-time national highs.

In some cities, the inequity runs far deeper. Charter students in Camden, New Jersey received more than $16,000 less per pupil than their district counterparts in one study period — a 46% funding inequity. In Little Rock, Arkansas, the gap represented a 57% difference. Nationally, one Fordham Institute analysis found that the charter funding gap had more than doubled in fifteen years, growing from roughly $3,266 per pupil in 2002–03 to over $7,800 per pupil by 2017–18.

Only one location in a major multi-city study — Shelby County (Memphis), Tennessee — came close to equitable per-pupil revenue between charter and district schools.

The Root Cause: Local Property Taxes

Why do charter schools receive so much less? The answer comes down almost entirely to one funding source: local revenue, and specifically, local property taxes.

Traditional public school districts are tied directly to local property tax bases. When a district passes a bond measure or a mill levy, those dollars flow to district-managed schools. Charter schools — even though they are public schools enrolling the same students from the same communities — are almost universally excluded from direct access to those local revenues.

Research confirms the pattern: in one major-cities analysis, the local funding disparity alone averaged $8,094 per charter student. State and federal gaps, while real, are comparatively small. The local revenue exclusion is the primary engine driving the inequity.

This means a charter school serving 400 students in Indianapolis, Denver, or Atlanta may be operating on more than $3 million less annually than a same-size district school a few blocks away — before a single instructional choice is made. That gap doesn't evaporate. It shows up in teacher salaries, facility conditions, technology, electives, and the depth of student support services.

2025: States Begin to Act

The funding gap is no longer just a research talking point. In 2025, several states moved meaningful legislation to begin closing it:

  • Indiana passed landmark legislation requiring traditional school districts to share a portion of local property tax revenue with charter schools, phasing in at 33% in 2026, 66% in 2027, and full implementation by 2028. Advocates noted that in Indianapolis, roughly 40% of students living within district boundaries already attend public charter schools — making exclusion from local tax dollars particularly difficult to justify.
  • Illinois increased its charter facilities fund by $20 million, directly targeting one of the most resource-intensive gaps charter schools face.
  • Idaho raised the cap on its credit enhancement program to $750 million, improving charter access to low-cost facility financing.
  • Tennessee launched a new Equitable Facilities Fund providing revolving loan options alongside $20 million in per-pupil facility aid.
  • Texas removed a longstanding cap on facilities funding for public charter schools, dramatically expanding what schools can access.

Colorado's charter advocates also notched wins in the 2025 session — securing a raised cap on the Moral Obligation Program to $1 billion and locking in full CSI Mill Levy Equalization funding, two moves that directly improve the financial footing of Colorado charter schools. The Colorado League of Charter Schools, the Denver-based statewide advocacy and support organization, works at the Capitol on behalf of Colorado's charter sector on exactly these kinds of legislative battles, while also providing member schools with finance and operations services designed to help schools make the most of the dollars they currently receive. In May 2026, that advocacy yielded another tangible result: Governor Polis signed SB26-145 into law, advancing charter school facility funding and bringing greater transparency to local bond planning.

What the Gap Costs Schools — and Students — in Practice

When a charter school operates on $5,000 to $8,000 less per student than a nearby district school, that shortfall doesn't disappear. It materializes in concrete ways:

  • Teacher pay. Charter schools competing for the same educator talent pool often can't match district salary schedules — a direct contributor to staffing challenges and turnover.
  • Facilities costs. Without access to bond funding or district-owned buildings, many charters pay commercial-rate rent out of per-pupil operating dollars that district schools spend on instruction.
  • Enrichment and electives. Arts, music, sports, and specialty programs are often the first casualties of a constrained budget — and often among the first reasons families chose the school in the first place.
  • Special education. Serving students with disabilities is more expensive, and the funding gap is compounded when state and federal special education allocations don't fully cover actual per-student costs.

The result is a persistent irony: charter schools — which research has consistently found achieve strong academic outcomes for low-income students and students of color, particularly in urban settings — are producing those results while systematically under-resourced compared to their district counterparts. As one Fordham Institute analysis put it, students in public charter schools effectively sacrifice around one-third of their educational resources by opting out of traditional public schools. The gap in outcomes that could exist under equitable funding conditions remains an open and sobering question.

What Does "Funding Equity" for Charter Schools Actually Mean?

Funding equity for charter schools means that every student enrolled in a public charter school receives the same base per-pupil public funding — from state, local, and federal sources combined — as a student enrolled in a traditional public school in the same community. It does not mean identical total spending (charter schools typically do not receive the same categorical add-ons for district overhead, pensions, or centralized services they don't use). It means that the public dollar attached to a public school student does not shrink by 30% based solely on which publicly authorized school that student attends.

Most states are still far from that standard.

What Charter Leaders Can Do Right Now

Legislative change takes time. In the interim, leaders operating under the funding gap have developed a practical toolkit:

  1. Pursue every federal grant available. The Charter Schools Program, Title I, IDEA, and competitive federal streams are accessible and often underutilized by smaller or newer schools.
  2. Build a philanthropic strategy. Foundation grants and individual donors can fund technology, enrichment, and capital improvements that per-pupil dollars won't cover. Schools with strong outcome data and clear community narratives have the most compelling case.
  3. Use purchasing cooperatives and shared services. Vendor networks and bulk purchasing arrangements can meaningfully stretch operational budgets — particularly in areas like insurance, technology, and back-office functions.
  4. Make the gap visible in your community. Many families — and many local elected officials — are unaware that the per-pupil disparity exists. Publishing it clearly, school by school and year by year, is often the first step toward political will.
  5. Engage your state association actively. State-level charter associations are the most effective vehicle for legislative wins on funding equity. Schools that are engaged members, show up at advocacy days, and put constituent faces on the issue are the ones whose stories move policy.

The Equity Argument That's Hard to Dismiss

There is a fundamental inconsistency at the heart of how public education is funded in most states. Charter schools are required to meet the same accountability, reporting, and performance standards as all other public schools. They draw students from the public. They serve communities with public dollars. They are held to public outcomes. Yet the exclusion from local tax revenue — the largest single driver of the gap — is not a technical oversight. It is a structural policy choice that has persisted for more than thirty years, and one that disproportionately harms the low-income students and students of color who are most likely to attend urban charter schools.

As more states begin to acknowledge and legislate against that inconsistency, the question for charter school leaders isn't whether the gap will eventually close — it's whether their schools are positioned to advocate effectively, survive in the interim, and grow stronger when the playing field finally levels.

The funding fight is winnable. It just hasn't been won yet.
