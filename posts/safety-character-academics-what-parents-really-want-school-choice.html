Safety, Character, Academics: What Parents Really Want

# Safety, Character, and Academics: The New School Choice Trifecta

Most school choice conversations still orbit around one question: which school has the best test scores? But survey data from the past two years tells a more complicated, and more interesting, story. Parents choosing charter schools, private schools, and homeschooling aren't just shopping for academic performance. They're searching for something harder to measure — and charter school leaders who understand this shift are better positioned to serve their communities and make the case for enrollment.

What Parents Are Actually Saying

EdChoice's annual Schooling in America survey is one of the most comprehensive gauges of K–12 public opinion in the country. The 2024 edition — drawn from nearly 4,000 respondents including more than 2,300 current school parents — produced a finding that should stop any school leader in their tracks: school safety is now the single top reason families choose charter schools, cited by 37 percent of charter parents. Academic quality and reputation came in at 36 percent, followed closely by morals, character, and values instruction at 31 percent.

Read that again. For parents actively choosing charter schools, safety and character development are essentially tied with academics as the primary drivers of their decision. This isn't a footnote — it's a fundamental reframe of what "school quality" means to the families charter schools are trying to reach.

The broader sentiment data is equally striking. In 2024, nearly two-thirds of all current school parents — 64 percent — said K–12 education in America was headed in the wrong direction. That was the highest level of pessimism recorded in the survey's 12-year history. By 2025, the number had improved only slightly: 59 percent of school parents still said education was on the wrong track.

The Enrollment Picture Confirms the Trend

Parents aren't just expressing frustration in surveys — they're acting on it. Public school enrollment has declined measurably in recent years, driven partly by falling birth rates and partly by deliberate family decisions. Charter schools, meanwhile, reported enrollment growth of roughly 15 percent during this period, while private schooling and homeschooling also expanded significantly.

What are families fleeing toward? According to the data, they're seeking environments where their children feel safe, where adults model and teach integrity and responsibility alongside reading and math, and where the school's culture is small and coherent enough to actually deliver on those promises. The Hunt Institute's analysis of 2024–25 voter and parent priorities put it plainly: school safety, grade-level reading, and workforce readiness dominated the list — not standardized test rankings.

Why "Whole Child" Is No Longer Just a Slogan

What does it mean for a charter school to educate the whole child?

At its core, a whole-child approach means a school takes responsibility not just for academic mastery, but for a student's sense of safety, personal character, and readiness to contribute to the world beyond the classroom. Developmental research consistently shows that students cannot learn effectively when they feel unsafe, unseen, or disconnected from a school's values and community. Academic rigor and a strong school culture aren't competing priorities — they reinforce each other.

For charter schools, this framing is both a philosophical commitment and a practical competitive advantage. A school that can articulate how it builds character — not just assert that it does — answers the question parents are actually asking. Advisory systems, leadership programs, explicit values instruction, and restorative discipline practices are the structural mechanisms that translate mission language into lived student experience.

A School Built Around All Three

Moreno Valley High School, a tuition-free charter serving grades 9–12 in Angel Fire, New Mexico, is an example of a school whose design reflects all three priorities that families now rank at the top of their list. Its homepage explicitly names "Rigorous, Future-Ready Curriculum," "Small Classes, Big Support," and "Safe & Inspiring Campus" as core pillars — and its programs include a dedicated Character & Leadership strand alongside traditional academics. The school's stated mission is to prepare students "for lifelong success as learners, citizens, and leaders," a formulation that deliberately places citizenship and leadership on equal footing with academic preparation. For families in Northern New Mexico who want a school that takes all three dimensions seriously, it represents the kind of coherent, whole-child environment the survey data says they're looking for.

What Charter Leaders Should Do With This

The practical implication is straightforward, even if the execution takes work. Charter schools have always had the structural flexibility to build strong cultures — smaller size, mission-driven enrollment, freedom from some district-level constraints. But flexibility only matters if it's used intentionally.

A few questions worth asking:

  • Can your school articulate its safety culture concretely? Not just "we have a safe environment" — but how do students and staff experience safety day to day?
  • Is character development embedded in the school's structure, or is it an add-on assembly twice a year?
  • Does your enrollment messaging speak to all three priorities, or does it lean exclusively on academic achievement data?

Families making school choice decisions are doing real research. They visit campuses, read mission statements carefully, and ask pointed questions. A school that communicates — and delivers — on safety, character, and academics isn't just satisfying a market preference. It's building the kind of community that retains students, earns parent trust, and produces graduates who are genuinely ready for what comes next.

The Takeaway

The data is clear: academic quality alone is no longer sufficient to win — or keep — families. What today's parents want from a school is an environment where their child feels safe, is taught to be a person of integrity, and is challenged academically. Charter schools with explicit, well-executed whole-child missions aren't behind the curve. They're ahead of it.
