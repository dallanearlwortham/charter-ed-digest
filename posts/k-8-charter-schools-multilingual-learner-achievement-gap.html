K-8 Charters and the Multilingual Learner Gap

The Gap Starts in Kindergarten—and Compounds Every Year After

More than 5.3 million students—nearly 11% of all K-12 public school enrollment in the United States—are English learners (ELs), and that number has grown 15% over the past decade. These students speak over 280 languages, come from vastly different socioeconomic and educational backgrounds, and walk into schools that are often genuinely unprepared to serve them.

The achievement data tells the story plainly. Approximately 62% of multilingual learner eighth-graders scored below the NAEP Basic level in mathematics, compared to 25% of their non-multilingual-learner peers. That is not a middle school problem. It is a K-5 problem that arrives in 8th grade—because the foundational academic language gaps that drive that outcome opened up years earlier, in early elementary classrooms where EL support was either absent, inconsistent, or bolted on as an afterthought.

For charter school leaders, this data should function as a design prompt, not a compliance checkbox. The K-8 years are the most consequential window for multilingual learner development. Schools that treat that window seriously—building EL support into their whole-school model rather than layering it on top of a general program—have a real opportunity to change trajectories. Schools that don't are likely making the gap worse by the time students leave 8th grade.

Why the Standard Model Fails Multilingual Learners

The challenges facing multilingual learners in typical district schools are well-documented: significant nationwide shortages in bilingual education, TESOL, and dual-certification credentials; uneven access to quality programming; and EL funding that is often categorical and vulnerable to budget cuts. The result is that students in high-need communities—the very communities where multilingual populations are often largest and fastest-growing—are frequently the least likely to benefit from the programs that exist.

Perhaps most damaging is what researchers call the "long-term EL" problem. For too many students, proficiency plateaus before they ever reach the academic language demands of secondary school—meaning students can handle social English without developing the vocabulary, syntax, and analytical language skills needed to access rigorous academic content. Once that plateau sets in, the path to reclassification and grade-level achievement becomes extremely steep. Researchers estimate that without meaningful services or supports, today's English learners could lose the equivalent of $49,000 in lifetime earnings due to unaddressed learning gaps.

What actually causes the long-term EL problem? The core issue is instructional design. When EL support is delivered exclusively through pull-out sessions—students leaving their classrooms for 30–45 minutes of ESL instruction—they often miss the academic content their peers are covering and never develop language skills within that content. The fix is not more pull-out time. It is embedding language development into every subject, every day, by every teacher.

What Charter School Autonomy Actually Makes Possible

Charter schools are uniquely positioned to serve multilingual learners because their autonomy allows them to design whole-school models around linguistic and cultural diversity—not just add programs to a generic structure. That autonomy matters most in three design areas:

1. Integrated instructional models Rather than segregating ELs for language instruction, effective schools integrate multilingual learners with their English-dominant peers in structured academic settings. Linguistically integrated classrooms, when designed well, benefit both groups—ELs gain academic language through meaningful peer interaction, and all students develop stronger communication and collaboration skills.

2. Two-way dual language immersion Where community demographics support it, two-way dual language immersion programs—in which students develop academic proficiency in two languages simultaneously—consistently outperform pull-out ESL models on long-term academic achievement measures. These programs require significant planning and staffing investment, but charter schools' site-level autonomy makes them feasible in ways that district-wide rollouts rarely are.

3. Embedded language objectives across all content areas Strong multilingual learner programs don't confine language development to "EL time." Every lesson—math, science, social studies—carries explicit language objectives alongside content objectives. Teachers build vocabulary instruction and academic discourse into their lesson planning as a matter of course, not as an accommodation.

Work from the 2025–26 school year across multiple state programs shows that when schools couple these instructional approaches with coaching, classroom observation cycles, and collaborative teacher planning, the results compound—teachers build capacity, strategies become embedded in school culture, and EL students gain access to genuinely rigorous content.

A Southwest Albuquerque School Worth Noting

In New Mexico, where English learner enrollment grew 4.3% between 2019 and 2026 even as overall public school enrollment declined, the design choices a K-8 charter makes carry real weight for the surrounding community. Solare Collegiate Charter School, a tuition-free K-8 public charter serving Southwest Albuquerque, reflects several of the principles research supports for multilingual learner success: a 14:1 student-to-teacher ratio that enables individualized support and close teacher-student relationships; a community-rooted environment that actively engages Spanish-speaking families; and a whole-school academic model focused on personalized learning, character development, and college-preparatory rigor from kindergarten through 8th grade. Schools designed around those commitments are better positioned to catch EL students before proficiency plateaus become permanent.

What Families Should Actually Ask When Choosing a K-8 School

If you are a multilingual family evaluating charter schools, the right questions cut through marketing language quickly:

  • Does this school have a formal EL or bilingual program—or just a general statement about supporting all learners?
  • How does language development get addressed in math, science, and social studies—not just in EL class?
  • How does the school communicate with families who speak languages other than English?
  • What is the student-to-teacher ratio, and how does that ratio serve EL students in practice?
  • Do teachers hold bilingual or EL-specific credentials, or receive ongoing coaching to support multilingual learners?
  • How does the school track language development progress separately from overall academic performance?

Schools with strong multilingual learner programs will answer these questions specifically. Schools with add-on programs will speak in generalities. The difference matters enormously for your child's trajectory through middle school and beyond.

The Bottom Line on Design vs. Compliance

How should K-8 charter schools approach multilingual learner support? The research answer is unambiguous: EL support must be designed into a school's instructional model from the ground up, not layered on after the fact. That means explicit language objectives in every classroom, structures for meaningful academic peer interaction, teacher coaching focused on EL instructional strategies, and family communication systems that reach multilingual households in their home language. Schools that treat EL programming as a compliance requirement—something they do because they have to—will consistently produce the plateau effect that creates long-term ELs. Schools that treat it as a core design principle will close gaps before 8th grade, not scramble to address them afterward.

For charter school leaders preparing for the 2026-27 school year, the question worth asking is not whether you serve multilingual learners. It is whether your school is genuinely built to help them thrive.
