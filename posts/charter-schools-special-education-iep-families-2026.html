Charter Schools & Special Ed: What IEP Families Must Know

# Charter Schools and Special Education: What Families of Students with IEPs Need to Know in 2026

For families navigating school choice with a child who has an Individualized Education Program (IEP) or a suspected disability, one question comes up again and again: Does my child still get their full special education rights if I choose a charter school?

The short answer is yes — but the details matter enormously, and 2026 has brought new research that should reshape how families, advocates, and charter leaders alike think about this question.

Your Child's Federal Rights Don't Stop at the Charter Door

Students with disabilities who attend public charter schools retain all the rights and protections that Part B of the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) provides, just as they would if enrolled in a traditional public school.

In practice, that means:

  • Free Appropriate Public Education (FAPE) — the central IDEA guarantee that eligible students receive special education and related services at no cost to the family — applies fully to charter schools, even one in its first year of operation or still in the process of hiring dedicated special education staff.
  • Child Find obligations apply. Charter schools, like all public schools, have a legal obligation to locate, identify, and evaluate all students who may qualify for special education services. Every charter school must have a system for screening children to determine whether they should be referred for a special education evaluation.
  • Existing IEPs must be honored. A charter school must carry out the IEP of every eligible student it enrolls, just as any other public school would. If your child transfers mid-year with an active IEP, services cannot simply pause while the new school "gets organized."
  • Lottery admission must be blind to disability status. All students who wish to attend a charter have an equal opportunity for admission regardless of their need for special education and related services. Questions about special education eligibility cannot be sought prior to any blind lottery — doing so would violate federal law.

What the New Research Says About Outcomes

A major study published in January 2026 upended a widely held assumption about charter schools and students with disabilities. Students with disabilities who leave a traditional public school to enroll in a charter school experience improved academic outcomes — and they improve at roughly the same rate as their general-education peers.

The study, published by the National Center for Research on Education Access and Choice, analyzed records from more than 1.7 million Michigan K–8 students who switched from a traditional district school to a charter school between 2013 and 2018. It found that while students with disabilities spent more time in general-education classrooms and received less intensive services than their peers in traditional public schools, their standardized test scores in reading and math increased along with those of classmates who didn't qualify for special education — and attendance improved for both groups as well.

One of the study's authors put it plainly: charter schools "can be a useful educational tool for parents with students with disabilities," and that families "shouldn't be quick to rule it out, because it does seem that, for at least a substantial segment of disabled students, charter schools are helpful for them."

This doesn't mean every charter is automatically the right fit for every student with a disability. It does mean the blanket assumption that charter schools shortchange students with IEPs is not well supported by current evidence.

The Trade-Off Families Should Understand: Inclusion vs. Intensity

One consistent finding across multiple studies is that charter schools tend strongly toward inclusive service delivery models. Compared to traditional public schools, charters use less intensive and less expensive special education programs and service assignments. After switching to a charter, students with IEPs are more likely to participate in resource programs — spending the majority of their day in general-education classrooms and receiving targeted instruction in a resource room for a portion of the day — and less likely to be placed in more restrictive, self-contained settings.

In fact, roughly 84 percent of students with disabilities in charter schools spend at least 80 percent of their school day in general education classrooms — a higher rate of inclusion than is typical in traditional public schools.

For many students — particularly those with mild-to-moderate learning disabilities, attention challenges, or social-emotional needs — this inclusive approach produces strong academic results, and the flexibility charter schools often have around scheduling and instruction can work in these students' favor. For students with more significant or complex needs who require intensive, highly structured, or self-contained support, however, the reduced service intensity common at many charter schools may not be sufficient. Those families need to dig deeper and ask harder questions before committing to any particular school.

A Real-World Example: Charters That Build Supports In from the Start

Charter schools that embed special education as a core part of their model — not an afterthought bolted on after the fact — are best positioned to serve students with IEPs consistently and well. Free Horizon Montessori, a charter school within Colorado's Jefferson County Public Schools serving students from preschool through middle school in Golden, Colorado, illustrates how a distinctive pedagogical model and comprehensive student supports can coexist under one roof. The school's site includes dedicated program areas for Special Education, Academic Support, Social Emotional Learning, and Gifted & Talented services — reflecting a commitment to meeting a wide range of learners within a single, coherent Montessori framework rather than treating exceptional learners as edge cases.

That breadth matters. The Montessori approach — with its emphasis on individualized pacing, mixed-age groupings, hands-on materials, and student-directed learning — can align naturally with the kind of differentiated, student-centered instruction that students with IEPs often need. But families of students with disabilities should still ask direct, specific questions about how any charter school, Montessori or otherwise, staffs and structures its special education delivery before enrolling their child.

6 Questions Every Family Should Ask a Charter School Before Enrolling

Regardless of the school's model or reputation, walk into every prospective charter school visit with these questions ready:

  1. Who is the LEA of record for special education? Is it the charter school itself or the authorizing district? This single answer determines who bears primary legal responsibility for implementing your child's IEP and arranging any related services.
  2. How are IEP services delivered on a daily basis? Does the school use co-teaching, a resource room, self-contained classrooms, or contracted outside providers? Can the school realistically accommodate the specific services, minutes, and supports already written into your child's current plan?
  3. What happens if my child needs a more intensive placement than the school can provide? In most states, the authorizing district retains responsibility for identifying and funding an appropriate alternative placement if the charter cannot meet a student's needs — but families should confirm this in writing before enrollment, not after a problem arises.
  4. What experience does the school have with my child's specific disability? A school that has successfully supported students with similar profiles and needs is a far stronger indicator of likely success than general reassurances about being "inclusive" or "supportive."
  5. How does the school communicate IEP progress to families? Ask specifically about the frequency of written progress reports, how the school involves parents in annual IEP meetings, and how staff coordinate with outside therapists or specialists your child already works with.
  6. Does the school's daily structure support my child's particular learning profile? An open, self-directed environment can be genuinely ideal for some students with disabilities and genuinely difficult for others. Be specific about what your child needs to concentrate, feel regulated, and stay safe — and ask how the school's typical day accommodates that.

The Bottom Line for 2026

Do charter schools have to follow IEPs? Yes — fully, completely, and without exception. They are public schools bound by federal law under IDEA. Do students with disabilities thrive in charter schools? Increasingly, the evidence says many do, particularly in inclusive settings where school quality is strong across the board. The right question for families in 2026 isn't whether to consider charter schools at all — it's how to rigorously evaluate whether a specific school has the staffing capacity, service delivery model, and school culture to meet your child's individual needs on their IEP.

Ask the hard questions before enrollment. Review how IEP services are actually delivered day to day, not just what the school's website says. And don't hesitate to loop in your district's special education office or a parent advocate if anything is unclear. Your child's legal rights travel with them — to every public school, including every charter, wherever they choose to learn.
