Do Charter Schools Outperform? What the Evidence Shows

# Do Charter Schools Outperform Traditional Public Schools? What the Evidence Shows in 2026

The question comes up every enrollment season, in every parent forum, and in nearly every education policy debate: Do charter schools actually work? The honest answer — backed by two-plus decades of serious research — is more actionable than a simple yes or no: outcomes vary enormously by school, and the factors that separate high performers from the rest are now well understood.

That's not a hedge. It's the most useful thing the research has produced, and families and school leaders who understand it are better equipped than those chasing a blanket verdict.

What the Strongest Studies Actually Find

The clearest long arc comes from Stanford University's Center for Research on Education Outcomes (CREDO), which has conducted three successive national charter school studies tracking student academic progress over time. Across all three studies, annual charter student learning in reading has risen by 22 days and math learning has increased by 23 days compared to traditional public school peers — gains that have grown as the sector has matured and chronically weak schools have been closed or improved.

High-quality studies find that charter schools boost achievement by more than their traditional-public-school counterparts — an advantage that is particularly large for students of color in disadvantaged urban communities, and one that has only grown larger as the charter sector has expanded.

The urban picture is especially striking. Compared to traditional public schools in the same areas, urban charters collectively provide significantly higher levels of annual growth in both math and reading — the equivalent of forty additional days of learning per year in math and twenty-eight additional days in reading, according to CREDO's Urban Charter School Study.

Long-term outcomes reinforce the test-score evidence. The first large-scale study of charter schools' effects on earnings in adulthood found that charter school graduates earn more than students who attend conventional public schools — and the study also reinforces earlier findings that students who attend charter high schools are more likely to enroll and persist in college. Research from Mathematica found that maximum annual earnings were approximately $2,300 higher for charter high school graduates aged 23–25 compared to conventional public school peers in Florida.

More recent peer-reviewed research on high-quality charter schools in Los Angeles found large positive effects on academic achievement, enrollment in any college, enrollment in any four-year college, and persistence to the second year of a four-year college — outcomes that go well beyond what standardized test scores alone can capture.

Where the Gains Are Most Concentrated

The research consistently points to three contexts where charter schools outperform most clearly:

Urban schools serving low-income students. Effects tend to be more pronounced among disadvantaged populations. Studies find that lower-scoring students experience the largest gains from charter attendance relative to their peers, and that urban charters following a high academic and behavioral approach produce substantial gains in both test scores and college enrollment.

Middle school grade spans. A comprehensive meta-analysis of charter school effects finds that the strongest and most consistent academic gains are concentrated in the middle school grade span, where charter schools produce positive and highly significant results — a finding that should factor into how families think about the K–8 and 6–12 models proliferating in many cities.

Schools with longer track records. The CREDO data show a sector that has improved over time as low-performing charters have closed and surviving schools have refined their models. A charter in its tenth year of operation, with consistent accountability ratings, is a different proposition than a newly opened campus with no track record.

One important counterpoint: students who attended virtual charter high schools are significantly less likely to graduate from high school, enroll in college, earn a bachelor's degree, or find formal employment as young adults — a finding that families considering fully online charter options should weigh carefully against the convenience factor.

The Five Practices That Separate High Performers

The most practically useful finding in the charter research literature comes from a landmark study by researchers Will Dobbie and Roland Fryer, Jr., who examined charter schools across New York City and identified what actually separates high performers from low performers. They found that an index of five characteristics — frequent teacher feedback, data-guided instruction, tutoring, high standards for students, and additional instruction time — explained nearly half the variation in measured charter school effectiveness.

That is a remarkable degree of explanatory power from just five variables. What each one looks like in a real school:

  • Frequent teacher feedback — not annual reviews, but regular, specific instructional coaching tied to classroom observation, often weekly
  • Data-guided instruction — teachers using assessment results to adjust what they teach next week, not just what gets reported in the spring
  • High-dosage tutoring — structured small-group or one-on-one support built into the school day, not an afterschool add-on that many students never use
  • High standards — consistent academic expectations applied to every student regardless of starting point, without tracking students into lower-expectation pathways
  • Extended instructional time — more hours deployed deliberately around the above practices, not just a longer day for its own sake

These are observable, verifiable practices — which means families and authorizers can actually look for them before committing to a school.

The Variation Problem — and Why It Matters More Than the Average

Here is what the headline averages conceal: academic outcomes at charter schools range quite widely, so the school itself matters far more than the category. The charter label alone predicts almost nothing. A strong charter and a weak charter share the same legal structure while producing radically different results for students.

Research examining college success and adult earnings has found large positive effects in some states and negative or no effects in others — differences researchers attribute to variation in average school quality, authorizer rigor, and the populations served. Context shapes everything.

This is precisely why statewide organizations focused on quality and accountability matter to the health of the sector. The Texas Public Charter Schools Association grounds its mission not in defending the charter model generically, but in advocating for and strengthening diverse, high-quality public charter schools — and in supporting the policy environment that holds individual schools accountable for results. Their publicly accessible school-finder tool is one practical resource for Texas families trying to navigate a large and varied landscape.

How to Evaluate a Specific School Before You Enroll

Given what the research shows about variation, the right question is never "Are charter schools good?" It is: Does this specific school demonstrate the practices associated with strong outcomes?

Here is what that evaluation looks like in practice:

Look at campus-level accountability data, not sector averages. In Texas, the Texas Education Agency releases annual A-F accountability ratings covering individual campuses across all public school types. The 2026 ratings cover 1,202 districts and 9,105 campuses — that data is public, searchable, and campus-specific. A school's multi-year trajectory matters more than any single year's grade; a campus earning consistent A's or B's over five years is telling you something real.

Ask directly about the five practices. When you visit a school, ask: How often do teachers receive instructional coaching? How does the school use assessment data between testing windows? What does tutoring look like — is it built into the school day or optional? A school that can answer these questions precisely has thought carefully about instruction. One that responds with mission-statement language probably has not.

Talk to current families, not just administrators. What parents should know is that if they are looking at a charter school, they want to tour the school, talk to administration and teachers, and ask to speak with a handful of parents who have their kids enrolled or have graduated. Peer perspectives reveal things brochures do not: whether teachers stay from year to year, how the school handles student conflict, and whether the culture actually matches the website.

Check the authorizer's record. Charter schools are authorized by state agencies or universities responsible for holding them accountable for academic and financial performance. A school that has sailed through multiple renewals with strong authorizer evaluations is a meaningfully different proposition than one operating under a corrective action plan — and that information is typically public.

Ask about outcomes beyond test scores. College enrollment rates, persistence data, and graduation rates are the most meaningful measures of whether a school is genuinely preparing students for what comes after K–12. A school proud of its results will share them readily.

What School Leaders Should Take From This

For charter operators and board members, the research sends a clarifying message: strong outcomes are not an automatic benefit of charter status. They are the product of deliberate instructional design — specifically, the five practices identified above — combined with a culture of internal accountability that does not wait for the state to issue a letter grade.

Blueprint Labs researchers studying the charter sector note that attending a charter school affects not only test scores but also high school graduation, college completion, employment, and earnings — which means the stakes of getting instruction right extend far beyond any single assessment window.

The schools driving the field's improving national averages share one defining trait: they treat the autonomy granted by their charter not as a privilege to enjoy, but as a responsibility to exercise in service of measurable results for every student enrolled. The evidence base is now rich enough that "charter school" alone tells you very little. What a school actually does with its freedom tells you everything.
