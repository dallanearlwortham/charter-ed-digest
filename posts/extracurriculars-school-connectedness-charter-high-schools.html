Why Extracurriculars Matter at Small Charter High Schools

# Why Extracurriculars Are a Graduation Strategy, Not a Nice-to-Have

When a charter high school lists 30 extracurricular activities on its website, that is not a marketing bullet point—it is an academic intervention. A growing body of research confirms what many experienced school leaders already suspect: students who participate in clubs, sports, arts programs, and student government are measurably less likely to drop out, more likely to graduate on time, and more likely to enroll in college. For small charter high schools trying to serve students who have often been underserved elsewhere, a robust extracurricular menu may be one of the most powerful retention tools available.

What the Research Actually Says

The link between extracurricular participation and school success runs through a concept researchers call school connectedness—the degree to which a student feels known, valued, and genuinely engaged at school. A May 2024 research brief from EdResearch for Action, authored by researchers from Johns Hopkins University, the BARR Center, the University of Chicago, and the American Institutes for Research, synthesized decades of evidence and reached an unambiguous conclusion: across all grade levels, students who feel more connected to school have higher attendance rates, higher academic outcomes, and higher graduation and post-secondary success rates.

Extracurricular activities are one of the strongest levers for building that connectedness. For many students, engagement in school develops through extracurriculars—and schools that offer students opportunities to mentor, tutor, or self-govern see stronger connectedness across the entire community. The same brief notes that leading extracurricular activities and clubs allows teachers to mentor and coach students in less formal, out-of-classroom settings—exactly the kind of relationship-building that is difficult to manufacture inside a 45-minute class period.

The graduation and college-going data are equally compelling. A longitudinal study published in 2023 found that extracurricular activity participation at one point in time predicted higher school belonging two years later, and in turn, higher school belonging reinforced positive mental health outcomes. On the dropout side, CDC data links volunteer extracurriculars to a 22% reduction in dropout rates among at-risk youth. A 2022 Georgetown University study found extracurricular participants are 25% more likely to attend college than their non-participating peers. And according to a 2019 study by the National Center for Education Statistics, high school students involved in extracurricular activities carried an average GPA of 3.0 compared to 2.7 for non-participants—a gap that compounds over four years.

Why Small Charter Schools Have a Structural Advantage

Does school size affect how much extracurricular participation matters? Yes—and small schools hold a distinct structural edge. Research from EdResearch for Action confirms that school connectedness among adolescents is strongly associated with positive classroom environments, reasonable disciplinary policies, small school size, and active student participation in extracurriculars. Those four factors reinforce each other, and charter schools that keep enrollment deliberately small are better positioned to activate all of them at once.

At a large comprehensive high school with 2,000 students, the student who does not make the varsity team or land the lead in the school play may have no obvious pathway into community life. There are simply not enough spots. At a small charter school, that same student can find a club, a team, or a leadership role because the ratio of activities to students is far more favorable. Research on what makes extracurricular programs effective at preventing dropout found that programs in effective schools were consistently oriented toward a single goal—supporting school perseverance—and that these schools had enough breadth and quality in their offerings to reach students with different interests and needs.

Variety matters enormously in this model. A student who will not join a sports team might connect deeply through a student council, an art program, or a peer mentoring role. Involving students in school councils and other organizational structures has been found to enhance school connectedness directly. And school service work—acting as a peer mentor, tutor, or school ambassador—creates more opportunities for students to experience school activities with prosocial components, reinforcing the sense that their presence at school matters to someone else. These are not add-ons to academic life. They are the connective tissue that holds a school community together.

The Connectedness Gap Is Bigger Than Most Leaders Realize

The urgency here is not abstract. National survey data paints a sobering picture of how disconnected many high schoolers actually feel. The 2021 Youth Risk Behavior Survey found that fewer than 40% of high school students reported feeling close to people at their school—a core element of connectedness. Ninth graders consistently report the lowest developmental support at school, even though engagement indicators tend to decline across all four high school grades.

Positive school connectedness has been consistently linked to various positive health outcomes, including lower levels of substance use, better mental health, and reduced violence. A significant number of students—particularly those from marginalized communities and ninth graders—report a lack of supportive adult relationships and meaningful engagement at school, according to the same EdResearch for Action brief.

That combination is a serious warning: the students who feel least connected are often in the grade most vulnerable to dropping out. A charter school that offers a genuine range of extracurricular entry points gives freshmen multiple chances to find their people before disengagement quietly becomes withdrawal. Ninth grade is the highest-stakes year, and extracurriculars are one of the few tools that can reach a student before a failing grade ever appears.

What This Looks Like in Practice

Sena HS, Gilbert L. Sena Charter High School in Albuquerque, NM, illustrates what it looks like when a small charter school deliberately pairs academic intensity with genuine community breadth. The school serves grades 9–12 with an individualized accelerated learning model and small class sizes—and running alongside that academic structure is a roster of 30 extracurricular activities for its student body. That density of options relative to enrollment is uncommon at any traditional comprehensive high school, and it maps directly onto what the school connectedness research prescribes: when activities outnumber the structural barriers to participation, more students find a reason to show up tomorrow.

Sena's reported outcomes reflect what that investment produces. The school cites a 95% graduation rate—a figure that does not emerge from curriculum design alone. Graduation rates at that level are built on students feeling seen, valued, and embedded in a school community they consider worth returning to each day.

Practical Takeaways for Charter School Leaders

If your school is designing or expanding its extracurricular program, the research points to several concrete priorities:

  • Breadth over prestige. Offer activities across multiple domains—athletics, arts, academics, leadership, and service—so students with different strengths and interests can all find a home. A school with five varsity sports and nothing else leaves a large share of students without an entry point into community life.
  • Lower the barriers to entry. Surveying students allows school leaders to gauge their interest in existing activities and better identify ways to increase access and participation, such as spotlighting demand for new clubs or eliminating activity fees that price out low-income families.
  • Build in prosocial roles. Peer mentoring, tutoring, and student government do not just occupy time—they create the sense of mattering to others that research identifies as a core driver of belonging.
  • Use extracurriculars as a teacher-relationship amplifier. Teachers who lead clubs and activities build the kind of informal trust with students that carries back into the classroom and strengthens academic engagement over time.
  • Treat 9th grade as your highest-risk window. Given that ninth graders report the lowest developmental support of any high school cohort, ensuring every incoming freshman has at least one extracurricular pathway is not optional—it is a retention strategy.
  • Measure participation the way you measure attendance. Track extracurricular involvement alongside grades and absences. A student who drops out of activities is often signaling disengagement before it ever shows up in the grade book.

The Bottom Line

School connectedness is not a soft goal or a secondary priority. It is, according to the CDC and leading education researchers, one of the most reliable predictors of whether a student graduates, stays healthy, and succeeds after high school. Extracurricular activities are among the most effective—and most underrated—tools for building it. For charter high schools that already operate with smaller enrollments, more flexible structures, and a mandate to serve students who need something different, the opportunity to get this right is real. The question is not whether extracurriculars are worth the investment. The question is whether your school is offering enough of them—and removing enough barriers—so that every student can find a reason to belong.
