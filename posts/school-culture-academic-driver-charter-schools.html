School Culture Is an Academic Driver, Not a Soft Skill

# School Culture Is Not a Soft Skill — It's an Academic Driver

When researchers at Harvard and the London School of Economics set out to study a 2012 New York City school discipline reform, they expected to find a trade-off: fewer suspensions might mean more disruption in classrooms, which would hurt student learning. What they found instead upended that assumption — and has real implications for every family choosing a school in 2026.

Published this year in The Economic Journal, the study found that middle schools where the discipline reform had the greatest effect saw math scores rise by 0.05 standard deviations and reading scores by 0.03 standard deviations over three years — gains the researchers compared to raising teacher quality by one-third of a standard deviation. Crucially, the gains were not driven mainly by the direct effect on students who would have been suspended. Instead, the improvements tracked almost entirely with changes in school culture: how students and teachers perceived safety, and the quality of their relationships with one another.

The implication is significant: school culture is not a soft, feel-good add-on to a rigorous academic program. It is the academic program's foundation.

What "School Culture" Actually Means — and Why It's Hard to Build

School culture is the sum of a school's norms, expectations, relationships, and shared identity. It shows up in whether students feel safe enough to take intellectual risks, whether teachers can spend instructional time actually teaching rather than managing disruption, and whether the daily experience of school feels purposeful or arbitrary.

The challenge is that culture is easy to describe and hard to engineer. Most district schools don't design culture intentionally — they inherit it from the surrounding environment, from year-to-year staff turnover, and from reactive policy decisions made under pressure. The result is enormous variation: two schools in the same zip code can feel entirely different.

Research consistently shows that this variation matters enormously for students:

  • Classroom order and learning time: When disruption is chronic, every student in the room loses instructional minutes — not just the student who misbehaved. The NYC study found that behavioral improvements benefited a broad range of students, including those who were unlikely to be suspended themselves.
  • Student-teacher relationships: Schools that improved on measures of relational trust between students and teachers saw the strongest academic gains — independent of other variables.
  • Perceptions of safety: Students who feel unsafe at school divert cognitive resources toward threat monitoring rather than learning. This is not metaphorical; it reflects how stress hormones affect memory consolidation and attention.

The Charter School Structural Advantage

Charter schools don't automatically have better culture than district schools. But they do have a structural advantage in building it: they can define their culture from the ground up, hire staff who share a common vision, and set clear, consistent expectations that families opt into voluntarily.

That voluntary enrollment is key. When a family chooses a school — reads its handbook, attends an information night, understands the dress code and behavioral norms, and decides to enroll — they are implicitly partnering with the school's cultural framework. Teachers aren't starting from scratch each fall trying to establish norms against student indifference or parental skepticism. The community that shows up on the first day of school largely chose to be there.

This is why academic research on "no-excuses" charter schools — networks with highly structured behavioral expectations, consistent routines, and explicit relationship-building — has repeatedly found achievement gains beyond what curriculum alone would predict. The research suggests it's not the strictness per se, but the coherence of the environment: everyone knowing what to expect, day after day.

What Families Should Actually Look For

What research says makes school culture a genuine academic asset: When families are evaluating schools, culture is often the hardest thing to assess — and the most important. Here are the indicators that distinguish intentional culture from marketing copy:

  1. Explicit, written behavioral expectations — not a vague "we value respect" statement, but specific, observable norms that are communicated to students consistently
  2. Low staff turnover — relationship continuity is foundational to trust, and high turnover breaks it repeatedly
  3. Structured daily routines — predictability reduces anxiety and frees cognitive bandwidth for learning
  4. Genuine family partnership — not just newsletters, but structured ways for families to understand school norms and reinforce them at home
  5. A shared intellectual identity — schools where students understand why they are learning what they are learning tend to produce stronger engagement than schools where curriculum feels arbitrary

In Utah, Jefferson Academy is an example of a charter school that has built culture around a coherent intellectual framework — a history-centered Core Knowledge curriculum with explicit, sequential, teacher-directed instruction — while also embedding family partnership directly into its model. The school's mission of engaging students "in partnership with family and community" reflects an understanding that culture is co-created, not just delivered from the front of the classroom.

The "Orderly Classroom" Debate in 2026

One tension in current education policy is between two legitimate concerns: the evidence that overly punitive discipline harms students (particularly students of color, who are disproportionately suspended), and the evidence that chronic classroom disorder suppresses learning for everyone. The NYC research helps clarify this apparent contradiction.

The findings suggest the goal isn't maximum punishment or maximum leniency — it's coherent culture. Schools that replaced harsh suspensions with consistent, less-disruptive interventions while simultaneously improving student-teacher relationships achieved better outcomes than schools that simply removed consequences. The mechanism was cultural, not merely behavioral.

This distinction matters for charter school leaders as they think about discipline policy, professional development, and school design in 2026-27. The question isn't "how strict should we be?" It's "how coherent and trust-building is our daily environment?"

The Bottom Line for School Choosers

Families evaluating schools this fall should resist the temptation to focus only on test score rankings or curriculum branding. Those matter — but a school's culture determines whether any curriculum actually reaches students. Ask about teacher retention. Sit in a classroom for an hour. Notice whether students seem to know what's expected of them and whether that expectation feels reasonable rather than merely imposed.

The research keeps arriving at the same conclusion: schools where students feel safe, known, and part of something coherent outperform schools where they don't — regardless of the demographics of the zip code. Culture isn't what happens after the academics. It's what makes the academics possible.
