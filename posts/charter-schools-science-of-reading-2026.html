The Reading Crisis Is Real—Charter Schools Are Answering It

# The Reading Crisis Is Real—Charter Schools Are Answering It

Six years after pandemic school closures scrambled foundational literacy instruction, the data remains sobering. Only 30% of eighth graders are reading proficiently, with no state showing gains since 2022. The students who were in kindergarten through third grade during those disrupted years are now filling middle school classrooms—and many are still carrying an unresolved reading debt. A fresh NWEA study released in August 2026 makes the picture even starker: middle school reading performance has not rebounded since the pandemic and continues to decline among the nation's lowest-achieving students, drawing on data from more than 20 million K–8 students in 30,000-plus U.S. schools.

For charter school leaders and families evaluating options for the 2026–27 school year, this crisis matters because not all schools are responding to it at the same pace—or with the same tools.

What "Science of Reading" Actually Means in 2026

The term has been circulating for years, but it reached a legislative and classroom tipping point in 2026. The practice of teaching children to guess at words using pictures, first letters, or context—known as three-cueing or MSV—has been thoroughly debunked by cognitive science, yet it persists in many classrooms, often unknowingly embedded in curriculum materials and teacher habits. What's replacing it is structured literacy and explicit decoding: direct, systematic instruction in how the brain actually learns to decode text.

States are now codifying this shift into law. Michigan's governor signed Public Act 26 of 2026, ensuring that by the 2031–32 school year, certified teachers from local school districts, intermediate school districts, and charter schools who provide literacy instruction will meet common Science of Reading training standards. Minnesota law similarly requires that every district or charter school use evidence-based curriculum and intervention materials at each grade level designed to ensure student mastery of phonemic awareness, phonics, and vocabulary development. The policy window is open—but mandates alone do not transform classrooms. Implementation does.

Why Charter Schools Have a Structural Edge

Here is a direct answer to a question many families and educators are searching right now: Can charter schools implement the Science of Reading faster and more effectively than traditional public schools?

The evidence suggests yes—and the reason is structural. Charter schools are accountable to many stakeholders, including students, parents, community organizations, and authorizers who have the power to close charters if they fail to demonstrate academic gains. That ever-present accountability pressure creates a genuine, ongoing incentive to adopt practices proven to work—not just practices that feel familiar or are politically convenient. A district school facing chronically low literacy scores rarely closes. A charter school does. That distinction changes the calculus around curriculum adoption, teacher training, and instructional fidelity in real and measurable ways.

High-quality studies find that charter schools boost achievement by more than their traditional-public-school counterparts—an advantage that is particularly large for students of color in disadvantaged urban communities, and one that has only grown larger as the charter sector has expanded and matured. On reading specifically, a sweeping study found that 83% of charter school students performed the same as or better than their peers in reading. Charter networks also tend to have far shorter decision-making chains than large districts. A charter leader who identifies a high-quality structured literacy curriculum in the fall can often pilot it school-wide by spring. In a large district, that same adoption might take two or three budget cycles and a curriculum committee vote.

The Uneven Recovery—and What It Reveals

The post-pandemic academic landscape is not uniform. About one in three schools have recovered in either math or reading following the pandemic, but far fewer have recovered in both subjects—in fact, only 1 in 7 schools have recovered in both math and reading. What separates recovering schools from stagnant ones? NWEA lead research scientist Emily Morton notes that while some schools recovered by avoiding initial declines, others rebounded with remarkable growth, and these "Rebounder" schools offer critical lessons about the practices and investments that help students regain lost ground.

The lowest-achieving middle schoolers are still showing pandemic-era losses because the interventions driving recovery elsewhere—such as strong K–3 literacy reforms—were built to work at those specific ages. That points squarely to a gap that charter schools spanning PreK through 12th grade are uniquely positioned to address: coherent, vertically aligned literacy instruction that does not stop at third grade and does not get reset every time a student changes buildings.

A Model Worth Watching

Freedom Prep, a public charter network in Utah serving students from PreK through 12th grade across campuses in Provo, Vineyard, and St. George—as well as online statewide—illustrates the structural advantage in concrete terms. When a single academic culture and instructional framework stretches across all grade levels under one charter, literacy instruction can be introduced in PreK, reinforced systematically through elementary, and extended into reading-across-content-areas work in middle and high school. There are no institutional handoff gaps, no curriculum resets, and no competing philosophies between a feeder elementary and a separate middle school. That kind of continuity is difficult for a fragmented district system to replicate.

What Families Should Ask Before Enrolling

Do not accept vague assurances about any school's reading program. Push past the marketing language and ask specific questions before committing for the 2026–27 school year:

  • What core literacy curriculum does the school use, and has it been independently reviewed by EdReports or an equivalent evaluator?
  • How do teachers receive ongoing Science of Reading training—is it embedded in regular coaching, or a one-time onboarding session?
  • How does the school screen for reading difficulties in the early grades, and what does a structured intervention look like in practice?
  • What do the school's state assessment reading scores look like year over year, and how do they compare to the local district average?

A school that answers those questions clearly and specifically has built its literacy program with genuine intention. A school that deflects or speaks only in abstract philosophy has not.

The Middle School Gap Nobody Talks About Enough

Most Science of Reading conversations focus on K–3, where foundational decoding skills are first built. But the August 2026 NWEA findings make clear that middle school is now the crisis frontier. Growing urgency around middle school literacy is building, along with the expectation that more schools will invest in systemic literacy supports that extend beyond elementary: embedding reading across subjects, rethinking instructional time, and rebuilding students' stamina and confidence to tackle challenging material. Charter schools with a PreK–12 span and a unified culture are structurally better positioned to deliver that continuity than systems where elementary and middle school operate as separate institutional worlds.

Ohio's momentum is illustrative: the Governor's Science of Reading Champions program expanded significantly in 2026, recognizing 109 schools across all 16 regions for exemplary implementation of evidence-based literacy practices, with applications highlighting strong assessment systems, consistent use of high-quality instructional materials, and a commitment to ongoing coaching.

The Bottom Line

The national reading crisis will not resolve itself through awareness or legislation alone. Research shows that 95% of learners can read proficiently when given a curriculum grounded in the Science of Reading, because a structured literacy approach teaches all the skills the brain needs to decode text systematically and explicitly. Charter schools—with their combination of accountability pressure, curricular autonomy, mission-driven culture, and shorter implementation chains—are structurally well-suited to make that model work faster and more consistently than large district systems.

Families who prioritize literacy when choosing a school are making one of the highest-leverage decisions in a child's education. The data to make that choice wisely is increasingly available in 2026. Use it.
