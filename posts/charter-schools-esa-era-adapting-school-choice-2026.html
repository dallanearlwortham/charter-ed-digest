Charter Schools in the ESA Era: Adapting to Win

# Charter Schools in the ESA Era: How to Stay Competitive When Families Have More Options Than Ever

For most of the past two decades, charter schools were school choice. They were the tuition-free, open-enrollment alternative for families frustrated with their assigned district school—innovative by design, publicly accountable by law, and accessible without a price tag. That positioning drove charter enrollment to record highs and made charters the centerpiece of the school choice movement.

That landscape is now fundamentally different.

As of 2026, 19 states have enacted universal school choice programs, most built around Education Savings Accounts (ESAs) that place public dollars directly into family-controlled accounts to spend on private school tuition, tutoring, homeschooling curricula, microschools, and more. Texas, for example, passed SB 2 in May 2025—establishing the state's first-ever ESA program—and the Texas Education Freedom Account launched for the 2026-27 school year with more than 100,000 families participating in its first year alone. At least eight additional states are actively considering ESA legislation in 2026, with momentum showing no signs of slowing.

For charter school leaders and families alike, this shift raises an urgent question: what does the charter school value proposition look like when virtually every family has access to some form of publicly funded school choice?

What the Research Shows: Enrollment Pressure Is Real

A landmark August 2026 report from the American Enterprise Institute—authored by researchers Ian Kingsbury and David Marshall—surveyed 285 charter school leaders in Arizona, Florida, and Utah, three states with robust, long-established charter sectors that also have mature universal ESA programs. The findings are a clear warning signal for the broader charter sector.

Nearly half of respondents reported enrollment losses due to students using an ESA for non-charter options such as private school tuition or subsidized homeschooling. In Arizona specifically, nearly half of charter leaders said students had withdrawn to use ESA funds, and 58% of Arizona leaders predicted the accounts would continue to decrease their enrollment going forward. Respondents also reported a growing perception among families that private and microschool models now offer superior flexibility and autonomy—a perception that charter schools have historically claimed as their own.

Thirty-eight percent of Arizona respondents and a quarter of Utah respondents said the ESA program made them more likely to consider opening a private school or microschool instead of continuing to operate a charter. And nearly two-thirds of charter leaders surveyed said they want relief from regulations, feeling the compliance burden puts them at a disadvantage relative to ESA-funded alternatives that operate under far lighter accountability requirements.

A separate analysis from the Thomas B. Fordham Institute asked the question directly: can charter schools survive universal school choice? The answer the sector must reckon with is: yes—but not by standing still.

Why the Charter Value Proposition Is Being Tested

The core tension is structural. When a family in an ESA state can receive a government-funded account worth thousands of dollars per year to customize their child's education—mixing a private school morning with an online course, a weekly tutoring session, and homeschool afternoons—the traditional charter school pitch of "free and innovative" becomes less automatically distinctive. ESA programs effectively turn school choice into a consumer marketplace. Charter schools, as tuition-free public institutions with open-lottery enrollment and state accountability requirements, operate by a different model.

That model has real, meaningful strengths. But in a crowded marketplace, strengths only matter if they are communicated clearly and delivered on consistently.

What exactly separates a charter school from an ESA-funded option? Charter schools are fully structured public schools that must accept all applicants through an open lottery, meet rigorous state academic and financial accountability standards, provide the full continuum of special education services, and employ qualified educators—all at zero direct cost to families. ESA-funded private schools, microschools, and homeschool arrangements face far lighter oversight. That distinction matters enormously, especially for families with children who have learning differences, who benefit from a stable peer community, or who want publicly verified performance data before they commit.

Strategic Moves for Charter Leaders Right Now

Charter school leaders cannot afford a wait-and-see posture. The enrollment pressure documented in Arizona, Florida, and Utah is a preview—not an outlier—of what will arrive in every state that expands ESAs. Here are the most consequential strategic moves:

Sharpen your mission story and make it specific. Families weighing a charter against an ESA-funded arrangement are high-agency shoppers doing real comparison shopping. Generic language about "rigorous academics" or "innovative learning" will not move them. What specific culture, pedagogy, outcome, or community does your school offer that cannot be replicated by assembling tutors and online courses? Every parent-facing communication should lead with a concrete, honest answer to that question.

Reframe accountability as a competitive advantage. The regulatory requirements that charter schools sometimes find burdensome are, from a family's perspective, a powerful trust signal. In a fragmented ESA marketplace where quality is genuinely difficult to compare across providers, charter schools offer something rare: transparent, state-verified academic performance data and a formal accountability structure with real consequences for underperformance. Make that data visible, accessible, and easy for prospective families to find and understand.

Invest seriously in community and belonging. Research on school choice consistently shows that culture, relationships, and a sense of belonging are among the top drivers of enrollment decisions—especially in the years since the pandemic reminded families how much students need stable peer bonds and trusted adult relationships. Robust extracurriculars, strong family engagement, a clear school identity, and mentorship programs are advantages that microschools and homeschool co-ops struggle to match at scale. These are not soft amenities; they are strategic differentiators.

Get to the policy table. States across the country are actively expanding and revising choice programs in 2026, and a new federal Education Freedom Tax Credit is set to launch in 2027, adding another layer of competition. Charter leaders who are not actively engaged in shaping the regulatory environment—advocating for accountability parity between charter and ESA-funded options, equitable per-pupil funding, and streamlined compliance requirements—will find themselves operating at a growing structural disadvantage with no one to blame but their own absence.

Texas: Ground Zero for the New Choice Landscape

Texas is a particularly instructive case, because the ESA wave arrived in a state that already had one of the most dynamic charter sectors in the country. Texas public school enrollment dropped by nearly 76,000 students in 2026, with falling birth rates, residential mobility, and expanding school choice all contributing to the pattern. The 2026-27 school year is, in many ways, Texas charter schools' first real test of operating in a mature ESA environment—with more than 100,000 families now holding publicly funded accounts they can direct outside the charter sector entirely.

That is precisely the kind of inflection point when statewide advocacy and infrastructure matter most. The Texas Public Charter Schools Association exists to support charter operators through exactly this kind of complexity—advocating for the policy and regulatory conditions that allow high-quality charter schools to compete and thrive, providing research, practitioner tools, and a coordinated advocacy presence at the state level. With Texas now marking 30 years of public charter schools, TPCSA's dual mission—defending charter quality and advancing the broader cause of expanded public school options—has never been more strategically important.

What Families Should Ask Before Choosing

If you're a family weighing a charter school against using an ESA to fund a private school, microschool, or home-based arrangement, push past the surface appeal of "flexibility" and ask the questions that actually reveal quality:

  • Accountability: Who evaluates this school or provider, and how publicly available are the results? What happens if quality falls short?
  • Special education: Does this option provide the full continuum of legally required services if your child has an IEP or 504 plan?
  • Educator credentials: Are teachers certified? What are class sizes and student-to-adult ratios?
  • Stability: What happens mid-year if the arrangement isn't working? How easy is it to transition back to a structured school?
  • Community: Will your child have consistent peers, extracurricular opportunities, and a stable sense of belonging over time?

For many families—particularly those whose children have complex learning needs, thrive in structured environments, or need the social infrastructure of a real school community—a high-quality charter school remains the strongest answer available. The decision should be made on honest comparison, not on whichever option has the most compelling marketing in a given moment.

The Bottom Line

ESA expansion does not make charter schools obsolete. It makes mediocre charter schools more vulnerable and excellent charter schools more valuable. In a fully competitive choice market, quality, mission clarity, and authentic community are not soft differentiators—they are the competitive moat. The charter schools that treat the ESA era as a forcing function for getting sharper, more mission-driven, and more genuinely connected to their families will not just survive this moment. They will define what excellent public education looks like on the other side of it.
