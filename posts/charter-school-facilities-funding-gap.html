The Facilities Funding Gap Hurting Charter Schools

# The Hidden Tax on Charter Schools: How the Facilities Funding Gap Drains Classrooms

Every dollar a charter school spends on rent is a dollar not spent on a teacher, a reading intervention, or a science lab. Yet across the country, charter schools routinely absorb facilities costs that traditional district schools cover through dedicated local tax revenue — bond elections, millage increases, and capital funds that charter students help generate but rarely share in. This structural inequity has persisted for decades, but 2025 brought the most significant wave of state-level action to address it in years. Charter leaders, families, and advocates need to understand what's at stake — and what's finally changing.

What the Data Actually Shows

The funding gap between charter schools and traditional public schools is well-documented and substantial. Research from the Thomas B. Fordham Institute found that urban charter schools receive significantly less money than traditional district schools, with the average funding gap in the cities studied reaching 29 percent — a $5,721-per-pupil disadvantage. Funding gaps of $1,500 per student or more remained in the majority of cities studied even after accounting for special education spending differences.

The main source of the gap is local revenue. Traditional districts often receive dedicated local funding for school buildings and maintenance through property tax millage and capital bond sales. Charter schools rarely share in those funds. Instead, they pay for facilities — rent, debt service, renovation — out of the same per-pupil formula allocations meant for instruction. The result: the facilities problem eats the classroom budget.

Why This Is More Than a Budget Problem

The facilities funding inequity doesn't just create accounting headaches. It shapes what kind of school a charter can realistically become.

When a school diverts instructional dollars to facilities costs, it faces concrete trade-offs that show up in classrooms:

  • Fewer teachers or larger class sizes to offset rent obligations
  • Inability to invest in science labs, libraries, or specialized learning spaces
  • Deferred maintenance in leased spaces that the school has no legal authority to address
  • Location instability — charters without secure, long-term facilities are far more likely to relocate or close, disrupting the students and families who chose them

This is why facilities funding isn't an administrative footnote. It is a direct determinant of academic quality, staff stability, and school longevity.

The 2025 State-Level Momentum

The most important education finance development of 2025 went largely under-covered in mainstream media: a coordinated wave of state legislation specifically targeting charter facilities inequity. In 2025, Florida, Idaho, Indiana, Missouri, Ohio, Tennessee, Texas, and Utah adopted laws creating or expanding funding for charter school facilities and students. Taken together, these actions generated $251 million in new facilities funding for charter schools nationwide — a significant and measurable shift.

The approaches varied by state, but each targeted a real gap:

  • Florida ensured charter schools have access to local infrastructure surtax revenue — the same pool of money that funds district building projects.
  • Indiana secured local property tax sharing for charters, improved zoning rules that had historically complicated school siting, and created local facilities and transportation authorities.
  • Illinois increased its dedicated charter facilities fund by $20 million, directly closing funding gaps that schools had been absorbing for years.
  • Texas removed a cap on per-pupil charter facilities funding, allowing it to increase from $157 to $373 per student — more than doubling it.
  • Colorado raised the cap on its Moral Obligation Bond Program to $1 billion and secured full Mill Levy Equalization funding, giving charters access to low-cost financing that district schools have long used.

What policy approaches close the facilities gap most effectively? The strongest models combine three tools: (1) a per-pupil facilities add-on embedded in the state's base funding formula, (2) equal access to state capital revolving loan programs, and (3) the right to share in local property tax or bond revenue generated within the school's community. States that have implemented even one of these mechanisms report meaningful relief in charter school operating budgets — and that relief flows directly to classrooms.

Why Statewide Advocacy Is the Highest-Leverage Tool

Policy data and model legislation exist. What actually moves state capitals is sustained, organized advocacy that keeps facilities equity on legislative agendas through multiple sessions — even as well-funded district lobbying groups push back, and even when the issue doesn't generate headlines.

This is precisely why statewide charter advocacy organizations matter in ways that individual schools cannot replicate. No single charter school principal has the bandwidth, the relationships, or the political standing to shape state capital funding formulas. That work requires a dedicated, persistent, statewide voice.

In South Carolina, the Public Charter School Alliance of South Carolina serves exactly this function — operating as the state's dedicated advocate for public charter schools, working on behalf of charter leaders, educators, and families to advance the policy conditions that allow high-quality schools to open, operate, and grow. Keeping facilities equity on the legislative agenda in Columbia is precisely the kind of structural work that determines whether South Carolina's charter schools compete on a level playing field or spend their best years compensating for a rigged one.

What Charter Leaders Can Do Right Now

While state policy catches up, school leaders aren't without options. Practical steps to reduce facilities pressure include:

  • Audit every lease line. Understand exactly what you're paying for and renegotiate where enrollment growth gives you leverage.
  • Explore shared-use arrangements. Partnerships with faith communities, community colleges, YMCAs, or municipal governments can reduce costs and expand usable square footage.
  • Apply for federal Charter Schools Program (CSP) planning and implementation grants. These can cover qualifying facility-related startup costs for new and expanding schools.
  • Quantify your facilities spend per pupil. Boards and leaders who can name this number precisely are far better positioned to make the case to authorizers, donors, and state legislators.
  • Track state legislation in real time. The 2025 wave of state wins didn't happen by accident — advocates were engaged in those legislative sessions from the first committee hearing.

The Equity Argument That Should Win Every Room

Charter school students are public school students. They generate the same per-pupil state revenue as their peers in district schools. When those students' schools are systematically excluded from the capital funding mechanisms that their tax dollars help support — because bond elections exclude them, millage rates don't follow them, and capital funds bypass them — it's the students who absorb the cost, in the form of crowded classrooms, inadequate spaces, and schools that struggle to survive long enough to demonstrate what they're capable of.

Momentum is building. Eight states proved in a single legislative year that this problem is politically solvable and fiscally achievable. The question for advocates, leaders, and families in every other state is straightforward: how long before your state legislature gets there, and who's doing the work to make it happen?

The facilities funding gap is a solvable problem. 2025 proved it. The work now is making sure every state — and every charter school still paying for classrooms out of its reading budget — doesn't have to wait another decade to find out.
