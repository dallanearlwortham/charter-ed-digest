Teacher Turnover Is Hurting Kids. Here's What to Look For.

# Teacher Turnover Is Hurting Kids. What Parents Need to Know in 2026.

Every fall, millions of students walk into a classroom and meet a stranger at the front of the room. The teacher they bonded with last year is gone — moved to another school, left the profession, or burned out somewhere between March and June. For many children, this isn't an occasional disruption. It is a recurring feature of their education.

The research on what this costs students is no longer ambiguous. In 2026, it deserves far more weight from families choosing schools than it currently gets.

The Scale of the Problem

Every school year, about 1 in 7 public school teachers moves schools or leaves the profession entirely — a higher turnover rate than in the 1990s and higher than rates seen in leading international education systems, according to the Learning Policy Institute's 2026 report on teacher turnover patterns and drivers. A parallel analysis from the National Council on Teacher Quality puts the annual departure figure at roughly 16% of all public school teachers.

The numbers get more complicated when you look at school type. Charter schools nationally show a turnover rate of 17.8%, compared to 14.9% in traditional public schools — a gap that should prompt any family evaluating charter options to ask pointed questions about staffing stability before they enroll.

Those aggregate figures mask enormous variation. Some charter schools hemorrhage staff. Others retain teachers for years, building exactly the kind of consistent, relationship-rich environment that research consistently associates with stronger student outcomes. The difference almost always comes down to school design, not luck.

What Teacher Turnover Actually Does to Students

The instinctive assumption is that turnover only damages the students of the teacher who left. The research says otherwise.

Studies find that turnover negatively affects not only the students of teachers who depart, but also the students in classrooms of teachers who stayed — meaning the harm ripples schoolwide, beyond the question of whether a replacement teacher is better or worse than the one who left. When teachers exit in significant numbers, overall school morale suffers in ways that drag down achievement across the board.

The mechanism isn't mysterious. High teacher turnover undermines a school climate built on student belonging, strong student-teacher relationships, and clear and consistent expectations — and when those things erode, discipline problems rise alongside them. The damage isn't purely academic. It's relational and cultural, wearing away the connective tissue that makes a school function as a community rather than a holding facility.

Teacher burnout compounds the problem before a single resignation letter is filed. When teachers are overwhelmed by oversized classes, administrative overload, and inadequate support, instructional quality declines and student relationships suffer — often for a full school year or more before a teacher finally walks out the door.

Why School Structure Is the Real Variable

Not every school faces equal turnover pressure, and the structural differences matter more than most families realize when they're comparing options.

The conditions that drive teachers out — unmanageable class sizes, no time for individual student support, anonymity within a large institution, and the exhausting sense that no one actually knows the students as individuals — are not inevitable features of schooling. They are design choices. And schools that make different design choices see different outcomes.

Research on charter school experiences finds that smaller class sizes and lower overall enrollment contribute directly to a sense of community, customized learning, and individual attention — conditions that also happen to make teaching more sustainable and professionally satisfying. Schools where teachers genuinely know every student tend to be places teachers want to stay. The stability that results is an academic advantage that compounds quietly across years.

Lumen, a tuition-free online public charter school serving K–12 students across Utah, builds this logic into its foundational structure. Its charter was written explicitly around individualized education for all students, every teacher has time scheduled daily for one-on-one student help, and small class sizes are a design principle rather than a talking point — intended to sustain the teacher-student relationships that make learning stick year after year.

The Question Every Parent Should Be Asking

What should families look for when evaluating whether a school provides real teacher stability?

Ask directly: What is your teacher retention rate? How many teachers returned this school year compared to last? Ask current parents whether their child has had the same teacher recognize their strengths across subjects, or whether the answer changes every September. Look for structural signals — not just stated values. Are class sizes manageable? Do teachers have dedicated time to work with students individually? Does the school design create conditions where a teacher could realistically stay for five or ten years and still find the work meaningful?

Families should also ask administrators how they support teachers through difficulty — mentorship, manageable workloads, planning time, professional development — rather than simply reacting to turnover after it happens. A school that can't answer these questions clearly is telling you something important about what it actually prioritizes.

Stability Is a Feature, Not a Bonus

In the broader school-choice conversation, families spend considerable energy comparing curriculum, test scores, extracurriculars, and commute times. Teacher stability rarely makes the list. It should rank near the top.

When schools face teacher shortages and high turnover, they often hire underprepared teachers, staff classrooms with substitutes, increase class sizes, cancel courses, and pile additional responsibilities onto whoever remains — all of which affect student achievement in ways that no single annual assessment captures. Those downstream effects are borne disproportionately by students with the fewest outside resources to compensate.

A child who moves through school with a consistent, caring, invested teaching staff — people who know their learning history, their blind spots, their particular way of understanding a hard concept — holds an educational advantage that accumulates in ways invisible to any ranking system. A child who meets a new stranger at the front of the room every August is starting over, again and again, in ways that only they fully feel.

School choice gives families genuine power. Using it well means asking not just what does this school teach but who will still be here teaching it next year — and the year after that.
